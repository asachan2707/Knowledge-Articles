How Simpler Architectures Made Me a Better Senior Developer

The trap most engineers fall into: adding complexity to signal competence. The best senior engineers do the opposite — they ruthlessly eliminate accidental complexity, resist the pull of distributed systems when a monolith would do, and measure architecture success not by elegance but by how rarely it wakes them up at 3 AM.

Domain: Architecture / Engineering Career Published: 2026-07-16

I spent the first few years of my career adding things. New framework. New service. New message queue. I thought good engineers built sophisticated systems. I was wrong. The engineers I respect most today are ruthless subtracters. Every new component is a liability until proven otherwise. Simplicity is not laziness — it is discipline. This is what I had to unlearn to become a better engineer.

The senior engineer's definition of success: A system is successful not when it is impressive at the architecture review, but when it runs quietly without requiring human intervention. The best systems are boring. The most boring system often wins.

Story · The over-engineered disaster

A team of five engineers built a startup product. They were good engineers — two had FAANG backgrounds, one had a PhD in distributed systems. They made what seemed like smart decisions. Kubernetes for container orchestration, because "we'll need to scale." Twelve microservices, each owning a bounded context, because "that's how Netflix does it." Kafka for event streaming between services, because "events are more decoupled than direct calls." A service mesh (Istio) for observability and mutual TLS, because "security and tracing are non-negotiable."

The system had 94% test coverage. Perfect CI/CD pipelines. Zero-downtime rolling deployments. OpenAPI documentation for every endpoint. Distributed tracing with Jaeger. Grafana dashboards with 40 panels.

It also went down every other day. Sometimes a service mesh configuration change caused all mTLS connections to fail simultaneously. Sometimes a Kafka consumer group rebalance triggered during a deployment and messages piled up in the dead-letter queue. Sometimes a network policy change in Kubernetes silently blocked one service from reaching another — no error, just timeouts.

The product had 200 daily active users. Two hundred. A single laptop could have served them all.

After six months, the team spent 40% of their engineering time on infrastructure. Features were late. Users churned. The company pivoted. Two of the FAANG engineers left because they were burned out from debugging distributed systems instead of building product.

I was one of those engineers. That experience changed how I think about architecture forever.

Lesson 1

Complexity Is a Debt, Not an Investment

Fred Brooks, in "No Silver Bullet" (1986), distinguishes between essential complexity (inherent in the problem — a payment system is genuinely complex) and accidental complexity (introduced by our tools and choices — the payment system does not need 12 microservices to be correct).

Every abstraction layer has an operational cost. Kubernetes adds a control plane that can fail, a networking model that can misconfigure, and a concept surface area that takes weeks to learn. Kafka adds broker management, consumer group coordination, partition rebalancing, and a whole new failure mode — consumer lag. A service mesh adds mTLS certificate management, sidecar proxy overhead, and traffic policy complexity.

These costs are real and they are ongoing. The engineer who adds Kafka "for scalability" at 1,000 requests per day is taking out a loan. The interest payments are the on-call hours, the debugging sessions, the onboarding time for new engineers, and the features that did not get built while you were fixing the broker.

How to calculate complexity cost: Count the number of independently failable components times their blast radius. A monolith with a PostgreSQL database has 2 failable components. If the monolith fails, the service is down. If the database fails, the service is down. Blast radius is one service. Twelve microservices with Kafka, Redis, and a service mesh might have 20+ independently failable components, each with failure modes that interact. The blast radius per component may be smaller, but the probability that something is failing at any given time is much higher.

The question to ask before every addition: Does the operational cost of this component over its lifetime exceed the benefit it provides? For a startup with 200 DAU, Kafka's operational cost is infinite relative to its benefit.

Lesson 2

Start with a Monolith, Earn Your Microservices

DHH (David Heinemeier Hansson), creator of Rails and co-founder of Basecamp, wrote "The Majestic Monolith" in 2016 — a polemic against premature microservice decomposition that holds up even better today. The key argument: a well-structured monolith with clear module boundaries is often faster to develop, easier to deploy, and simpler to debug than a microservices architecture with the same functionality.

Shopify, one of the largest e-commerce platforms in the world, runs on a Rails monolith. Stack Overflow, serving millions of developers per month, runs on a monolith. GitHub ran as a monolith for many years. These are not failures of ambition — they are pragmatic choices that allowed fast iteration.

When microservices actually pay off: The "2 pizza rule" is a useful heuristic — microservices make sense when a team cannot fit around a single table. More concretely:

  • Team autonomy requires independent deployment — different teams need to deploy their code on their own schedule without coordinating with others
  • Deployment frequency per service exceeds 5 per week — if each service deploys independently that often, tight coupling would cause deployment coordination overhead
  • Components have genuinely different scaling profiles — the image thumbnail service needs 10x more CPU than the user profile service, and scaling them together wastes money
  • Different runtime requirements — one component needs Python for ML, another needs Go for performance, another needs Node.js for an existing library

If none of those are true, you probably do not need microservices yet.

The strangler fig pattern — the only safe path to microservices: Never decompose a monolith by splitting it. Put an HTTP proxy in front of the monolith. Route specific paths to new services as you extract them. The monolith handles everything not yet extracted. Gradually move functionality to new services. At no point does the whole system go offline for a "big bang migration." The strangler fig metaphor: a vine that grows around a tree and eventually replaces it.

Lesson 3

The Database Is Almost Never the Bottleneck You Think It Is

I have seen this pattern more times than I can count. A system is slow. The engineer suspects the database. The proposed solution: add Redis, add a read replica, add an in-memory cache. The root cause: a missing index on a column used in every WHERE clause.

PostgreSQL is extraordinarily capable. A properly configured PostgreSQL instance on a single EC2 r6i.2xlarge (8 vCPUs, 64 GB RAM) can handle 10,000+ read requests per second for indexed queries. A query that takes 500ms without an index can take 0.5ms with a covering index — a 1000x improvement. No Redis cluster can match that gain for queries that were already fast enough to need no network round-trip.

The diagnostic sequence before adding infrastructure:

  1. Run EXPLAIN ANALYZE: Identify queries with Seq Scan (full table scan) on large tables. These are your index opportunities. Look for "cost=..." rows with very high estimated cost relative to row count.
  2. Check pg_stat_statements: This PostgreSQL extension tracks query execution statistics. Run SELECT query, total_time, calls, total_time/calls AS avg_time FROM pg_stat_statements ORDER BY total_time DESC LIMIT 20 to find your top time-consuming queries.
  3. Add missing indexes: For queries filtered on (user_id, created_at), add CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY ON events (user_id, created_at DESC). The CONCURRENTLY keyword builds the index without locking writes.
  4. Fix N+1 queries: If your ORM is generating one query per row in a loop ("get all orders, then for each order get the user"), replace with a JOIN or use eager loading. A 1,000-row result with N+1 generates 1,001 queries; with JOIN it is 1.
  5. Only then consider caching or replicas: If after proper indexing the database is still a bottleneck (measured, not assumed), then add a read replica for read-heavy workloads, or Redis for data that genuinely benefits from sub-millisecond access (session state, real-time counters).

True story: A team spent 3 weeks building a custom caching layer with Redis, cache invalidation logic, and a cache warmup job. The system was still slow after launch. A consultant ran EXPLAIN ANALYZE and found a missing index on the created_at column in the main query. Adding the index took 4 minutes. The custom caching layer was removed 2 weeks later.

Lesson 4

Synchronous Is Simpler — Until It Isn't

Async message queues are powerful. They also introduce a new category of debugging hell. Consider what you gain and lose:

When async is not worth it: The flow is a simple request-response where the user wants a result right now (search, form submission, data fetch). The operation completes in under 2 seconds. Your team does not have experience with Kafka or RabbitMQ. You have fewer than 1,000 requests per second. Adding a message queue here means:

  • Debugging a distributed trace (span 1 in service A → message in Kafka → span 2 in service B) instead of a simple stack trace
  • Dealing with message ordering (Kafka guarantees order within a partition, not globally)
  • Designing exactly-once or at-least-once semantics and building idempotent consumers
  • Operating a dead-letter queue and writing logic to retry or alert on failed messages
  • Monitoring consumer lag and writing alerts when lag grows unexpectedly

When async genuinely pays off:

  • Team decoupling: Service A and Service B are owned by different teams with different deployment schedules. Async messaging means A can publish without knowing if B is running — they are never blocking each other.
  • Spike absorption: You have 100 messages per second normally but 10,000 during a flash sale. A queue absorbs the spike; consumers process at their own rate. Without a queue, the spike hits your database directly.
  • Long-running tasks: Sending emails, generating PDFs, encoding video, running ML inference — anything that takes more than 2 seconds should be async. Return 202 Accepted immediately, process in the background, notify via webhook or polling.
  • Fan-out: One event (order placed) needs to trigger multiple independent downstream actions (send confirmation email, update inventory, notify logistics). Pub/sub is cleaner than chained synchronous calls.

A useful rule of thumb: if you cannot name specifically which of those four benefits applies to your use case, you probably do not need the message queue.

Lesson 5

Operational Simplicity Is a Feature

Dan McKinley's "Choose Boring Technology" (2015) is the most important architecture essay I have read. The core argument: every novel technology you choose depletes your "innovation tokens" — the finite capacity your team has for learning and debugging unfamiliar systems. PostgreSQL, Linux, and NGINX are boring. They are boring because thousands of engineers have debugged them, documented every failure mode, and written runbooks for every incident. Choosing a trendy alternative to PostgreSQL costs you all of that accumulated knowledge.

The best architecture is one that a junior engineer can understand at 3 AM. Not because 3 AM incidents are common, but because they are the test that reveals whether your system is truly observable and operable. If the person on call needs the original architect to debug a production issue, that is an architectural smell.

The "bus factor" for architecture: How many engineers on your team need to be hit by a bus before nobody understands the system? For our 12-microservice startup: the answer was one — the engineer who built the service mesh config. When she went on vacation, every infrastructure issue was blocked on her. That is an architecture failure.

Runbook-driven design: If you cannot write a 1-page runbook for a component — what it does, how it fails, how to restart it, how to check its health — it is too complex. Write the runbook before you build the component. If you struggle to write it, the component is too complex. A well-designed system has runbooks that any engineer can follow.

Lesson 6

Measure Before You Architect

Almost every premature optimization I have witnessed started with a sentence that contained the word "eventually." "We'll eventually need to scale." "This will eventually be a bottleneck." "We should eventually shard this database." The word "eventually" is load-bearing — it means you have not measured anything, you are guessing.

Load test before you shard: Run k6 or Locust against your production stack (or a staging environment that mirrors production). Ramp traffic from 10x to 100x expected peak. Watch where the system breaks. Only optimize the actual bottleneck. In my experience, 80% of the time the bottleneck is a slow query, not the database capacity.

Profile before you cache: Use flame graphs (Brendan Gregg's perf + FlameGraph, or clinic.js for Node.js) to find where your CPU time actually goes. I have seen teams add Redis caching to a function that was spending 95% of its time serializing a large JSON response to string — the cache hit rate was 0% because the cache key included user-specific data. The actual fix was streaming the response instead of buffering.

Monitor before you add circuit breakers: Circuit breakers protect against cascading failures from slow or failing downstream services. But if your monitoring does not show that a particular downstream is causing failures, adding a circuit breaker is adding complexity without evidence. Add monitoring first. Observe. Then add circuit breakers only around services that the monitoring shows actually fail.

The measurement toolkit:

  • Load testing: k6 (scripted in JavaScript, great CI integration), Locust (Python, easy to customize scenarios), Gatling (Scala, used for very high load)
  • Query analysis: pg_stat_statements (PostgreSQL), EXPLAIN ANALYZE (any SQL DB), Percona pt-query-digest (MySQL)
  • CPU profiling: clinic.js + flame graph (Node.js), py-spy (Python), async-profiler (JVM)
  • Memory profiling: Node.js --inspect + Chrome DevTools heap snapshot, Valgrind (C/C++), jmap (JVM)
Lesson 7

The Right Architecture for Your Team Size

Conway's Law (1968) states: "Any organization that designs a system will produce a design whose structure is a copy of the organization's communication structure." This is not a suggestion — it is an observation that has been confirmed repeatedly in practice. Your microservice boundaries will naturally follow your team boundaries because people communicate more easily within a team than between teams.

The corollary: design your architecture for your current team size, not your aspired future size. Building microservices for a 5-person team because Netflix has microservices and Netflix has 2,000 engineers is not a logical inference.

Architecture by team size:

  • Solo or 2-3 engineers: Monolith + PostgreSQL + Redis + background job queue (Sidekiq, BullMQ). One process. One database. Fast deploys. Zero coordination overhead.
  • 5-8 engineers: Modular monolith with clear domain boundaries (separate modules/packages per domain, same process). Add a caching layer only after profiling. Background jobs for async operations.
  • 10-20 engineers: 2-4 services split along team ownership boundaries. Not "one service per API resource" — one service per team that can deploy independently. Shared database is still OK if only one team writes to each table.
  • 20-50 engineers: Consider splitting services where teams have genuinely different deployment cadences or scaling profiles. Use the strangler fig pattern for any extraction.
  • 50+ engineers: Microservices are justified now. Teams deploy independently, each service has a clear owner, and the coordination overhead of a monolith exceeds the operational overhead of distributed services.
The Framework

The Senior Engineer's Architecture Decision Framework

Before every significant architectural decision — adding a new service, adopting a new database, introducing a message queue — I ask five questions:

  1. What specific problem does this solve today? Not "might solve" or "will eventually solve." Today. If the answer is vague ("it will make things more scalable"), it is not specific enough to justify the cost.
  2. What new problems does this introduce? Every technology has failure modes. Write them down. Kafka introduces consumer lag, message ordering complexity, and broker management. Redis introduces memory limits, eviction policy decisions, and the need to handle cache misses. Do you have capacity to operate these new problems?
  3. Can a junior engineer on my team debug this at 3 AM without my help? If the answer is no, either the component is too complex or your documentation and runbooks are insufficient. Fix whichever is true.
  4. What is the rollback plan if this turns out to be a mistake? Can you remove this component without rewriting dependent systems? Can you migrate data back? "We can't roll back" is a flag — that means you are permanently committed to the operational cost of this decision, regardless of what you learn later.
  5. Have we measured that this is actually the bottleneck? Show me the load test result, the profiler output, the p99 latency graph. If you are solving a theoretical future bottleneck, defer the solution until the bottleneck is real.

Most proposed architecture additions fail at question 1 or question 5. That is not a reason to stop asking them.

JavaScript Example

Boring vs. Over-Engineered: The Same Feature, Two Ways

JavaScript Example 
/**
 * A feature: "Get the top 10 products by view count for a given category."
 *
 * VERSION A: The "boring" version — direct DB call + in-process cache.
 * VERSION B: The over-engineered version — Redis + Kafka + microservice.
 *
 * Count the lines of code, failure modes, and operational dependencies in each.
 */

// ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
// VERSION A: BORING (44 lines, 2 failure modes, 1 dependency)
// ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
import { Pool } from 'pg';

const db    = new Pool({ connectionString: process.env.DATABASE_URL });
const cache = new Map(); // In-process LRU-style cache
const CACHE_TTL_MS = 60_000; // 1 minute

async function getTopProducts_boring(category) {
  const cacheKey = `top-products:${category}`;
  const cached = cache.get(cacheKey);

  if (cached && Date.now() - cached.at < CACHE_TTL_MS) {
    return cached.data; // Cache hit — return immediately
  }

  // Cache miss — query PostgreSQL directly
  const { rows } = await db.query(`
    SELECT id, name, view_count
    FROM products
    WHERE category = $1
      AND is_active = true
    ORDER BY view_count DESC
    LIMIT 10
  `, [category]);

  cache.set(cacheKey, { data: rows, at: Date.now() });
  return rows;
}

/*
 * FAILURE MODES (2 total):
 *   1. PostgreSQL is down → function throws, caller handles error
 *   2. Application crashes → cache lost, next request re-warms from DB
 *
 * DEPENDENCIES: PostgreSQL
 * LINES: ~25
 * DEBUGGABILITY: console.log + stack trace
 * 3AM RUNBOOK: "Is PostgreSQL running? Run: SELECT 1"
 */


// ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
// VERSION B: OVER-ENGINEERED (sketch — actual impl would be ~300+ lines)
// ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
//
// Architecture:
//   products-service  → publishes ProductViewed events to Kafka
//   view-counter-service → consumes events, aggregates view counts, writes to Redis sorted set
//   query-service → reads from Redis sorted set, falls back to PostgreSQL
//   cache-invalidation-service → subscribes to ProductUpdated events, purges Redis keys
//
// Pseudocode (each service is a separate deployment):

// products-service: publish event on every product view
async function trackView_overengineered(productId, category) {
  await kafka.producer.send({
    topic: 'product-events',
    messages: [{ value: JSON.stringify({ type: 'ProductViewed', productId, category, timestamp: Date.now() }) }],
  });
}

// view-counter-service: consume events, update Redis sorted set
async function consumeProductViewed_overengineered(event) {
  const { productId, category } = JSON.parse(event.value.toString());
  await redis.zIncrBy(`top-products:${category}`, 1, productId);
  // Also need: TTL management, hot key handling, Redis memory limits...
}

// query-service: read from Redis, fall back to PostgreSQL
async function getTopProducts_overengineered(category) {
  try {
    const results = await redis.zRangeWithScores(`top-products:${category}`, -10, -1, { REV: true });
    if (results.length === 10) return results;
    // Fall back to PostgreSQL...
  } catch {
    // Redis is down? Fall back to PostgreSQL...
  }
  return await db.query('SELECT ...'); // Same query as Version A
}

/*
 * FAILURE MODES (8+ total):
 *   1. PostgreSQL down
 *   2. Redis down
 *   3. Kafka broker down
 *   4. view-counter-service consumer group rebalancing
 *   5. view-counter-service crashes → events pile up in dead-letter queue
 *   6. Redis key evicted under memory pressure → stale top-products list
 *   7. Kafka partition skew → some categories updated slowly
 *   8. Cache invalidation service falls behind → stale data
 *   + all the failure interactions between the above
 *
 * DEPENDENCIES: PostgreSQL + Redis + Kafka + 3 services
 * LINES: ~300+ (across 3 services)
 * DEBUGGABILITY: distributed trace across 3 services + Kafka + Redis
 * 3AM RUNBOOK: 6 pages
 *
 * APPROPRIATE WHEN: 10M+ product views/day, dedicated platform team,
 * 50+ engineers, different teams own query-service vs view-counter-service.
 * NOT appropriate for: a startup with 200 DAU.
 */
JavaScript Example

Strangler Fig Pattern: Monolith to Microservice Extraction

JavaScript Example 
/**
 * Strangler fig pattern implementation.
 * An HTTP proxy sits in front of the monolith and gradually routes
 * specific paths to new extracted services.
 *
 * Step 1: Put this proxy in front of the existing monolith.
 * Step 2: Extract "user profiles" to a new service.
 * Step 3: Route /api/users/* to the new service; everything else stays in monolith.
 * Step 4: Gradually extract more paths as teams and services are ready.
 * Step 5: When the monolith is empty, decommission it.
 */
import express from 'express';
import { createProxyMiddleware } from 'http-proxy-middleware';

const router = express.Router();

// ── Route registry ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
// Add new entries here as each service is extracted from the monolith.
// Format: { pathPattern, targetUrl, extractedOn, owner }
const extractedRoutes = [
  {
    pattern:     /^\/api\/users/,
    target:      'http://user-service:3001',
    extractedOn: '2026-02-15',
    owner:       'platform-team',
  },
  {
    pattern:     /^\/api\/orders/,
    target:      'http://order-service:3002',
    extractedOn: '2026-04-10',
    owner:       'commerce-team',
  },
  // Add more extracted services here as you migrate them
];

const MONOLITH_URL = 'http://monolith:8080';

// ── Proxy middleware factory ────────────────────────────────────────────────
function makeProxy(target) {
  return createProxyMiddleware({
    target,
    changeOrigin: true,
    on: {
      error: (err, req, res) => {
        console.error(`[StranglerProxy] Error proxying to ${target}: ${err.message}`);
        // If the extracted service is down, fallback to monolith
        // (optional — depends on whether you trust the monolith as fallback)
        res.status(502).json({ error: 'Service temporarily unavailable', retryable: true });
      },
      proxyReq: (proxyReq, req) => {
        // Propagate trace context so distributed traces connect
        proxyReq.setHeader('X-Trace-Id', req.headers['x-trace-id'] || crypto.randomUUID());
        proxyReq.setHeader('X-Forwarded-By', 'strangler-proxy');
      },
    },
  });
}

// ── Route matching ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
router.use((req, res, next) => {
  // Check if this path has been extracted to a new service
  const match = extractedRoutes.find(r => r.pattern.test(req.path));

  if (match) {
    console.log(`[StranglerProxy] ${req.method} ${req.path} → ${match.target} (${match.owner})`);
    return makeProxy(match.target)(req, res, next);
  }

  // Fall through to the monolith
  console.log(`[StranglerProxy] ${req.method} ${req.path} → monolith`);
  return makeProxy(MONOLITH_URL)(req, res, next);
});

// ── Health endpoint (shows extraction progress) ─────────────────────────────
router.get('/meta/extraction-status', (req, res) => {
  res.json({
    extractedRoutes: extractedRoutes.map(r => ({
      pattern:     r.pattern.toString(),
      target:      r.target,
      extractedOn: r.extractedOn,
      owner:       r.owner,
    })),
    monolithUrl: MONOLITH_URL,
    advice: 'Add new entries to extractedRoutes as services are extracted.',
  });
});

export default router;

// ── Usage ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
// const app = express();
// app.use('/', router);
// app.listen(80);
//
// The proxy now handles all traffic. Extracted services get their paths.
// The monolith handles everything else — no change needed to the monolith.
// As you extract more services, add entries to extractedRoutes.
// When extractedRoutes covers all paths, you can decommission the monolith.
Practical checklist

Before Adding Complexity — 10 Questions

  • Have I measured the current bottleneck, or am I optimizing a theoretical future problem?
  • What specific problem does this addition solve today — not eventually, today?
  • What new failure modes does this component introduce, and do I have the capacity to operate them?
  • Can I write a 1-page runbook for this component right now? If not, it is too complex.
  • Can a junior engineer on my team debug this at 3 AM without waking me up?
  • What is the rollback plan if this turns out to be a mistake in 6 months?
  • Am I solving a scale problem before I have scale? (Do I have 100x more users than my current system can handle?)
  • Is this the "boring" solution, or is there a simpler technology that 10,000 engineers have battle-tested?
  • Does my team have experience operating this technology, or will this be a learning-curve tax on delivery?
  • If I removed this component tomorrow, would users notice? If no — do I need it?
FAQ

Common questions

Does "start with a monolith" mean I should never use microservices?

No. Microservices are the right choice at the right scale. The argument is against premature decomposition — starting with microservices when a monolith would serve you better for years. Most products will never reach the scale at which microservices become necessary. Of those that do, the strangler fig pattern gives you a safe migration path.

What about serverless? Is that simpler than a monolith?

Serverless (Lambda, Cloud Functions) reduces operational overhead — no servers to manage. But it introduces its own complexity: cold starts, function-level deployment, cold-start latency spikes, VPC networking for database access, and the challenge of tracing across many small functions. For simple CRUD APIs, serverless can genuinely be simpler. For complex event-driven systems with many interconnected functions, it can become the worst of both worlds — the operational complexity of microservices plus the cold-start tax.

How do I convince my team to resist premature optimization?

Show them the data. When someone proposes a new architectural component, ask them to demonstrate the problem it solves with a load test or profiler output. "This will eventually be a bottleneck" is not sufficient. If they cannot demonstrate the problem, propose deferring the solution until the problem is measurable. Teams that have been burned by over-engineering usually need only one incident to change their approach.

Is it ever too late to simplify an architecture?

Almost never — but the cost increases over time. The best time to simplify was before you built the complexity. The second best time is now. Start by identifying the components that cause the most operational pain (the ones that generate the most on-call alerts) and ask whether removing them would simplify the system without losing a measurable benefit. Often the answer is yes.

Key Takeaways

The hardest lesson I learned as an engineer was that adding things is easy, removing things is hard, and the best engineers are ruthless about the latter. Accidental complexity compounds — every component added to solve a theoretical future problem is a component that has to be operated, debugged, documented, and explained to every engineer who joins after you. The frameworks for avoiding this trap are: measure before you optimize, start with the boring technology, match architecture to team size (not aspirational team size), and treat every new component as a liability until it proves its value. Senior engineering is not about knowing how to build complex systems — it is about knowing when not to.
