Complete Financial Planning Guide for IT Professionals in India

A ₹1.5L/month MNC salary in a Tier-1 city is a strong foundation — but without a deliberate plan, lifestyle inflation, uninsured risks, and unplanned life events can quietly destroy it. This guide builds a complete financial roadmap: emergency fund, insurance, investments, home, kids, parents, and retirement — so you never need to borrow money or lose sleep over money again.

Finance · Health · Life Planning · 35 min read Added 2026-07-16

Most IT professionals earning well in their 20s and 30s reach 45 and realise they haven't built real wealth — they've built a lifestyle. The solution is not earning more, it's a system: one that routes every rupee with intention before it can be spent on impulse.

Ground truth for this plan: Gross income ₹1.5L/month (~₹18L/year), Tier-1 city (Mumbai/Bengaluru/Delhi/Pune/Hyderabad), MNC employee (EPF applicable), married, child planned in ~2 years, parents to support in future. All numbers are illustrative — adjust for your actual take-home, rent, and goals.

The Engineer Who Earned Well and Retired Broke

Rahul joined a top MNC at 24 with a ₹12L package. By 32 he was earning ₹1.8L/month. He had a nice apartment on rent, a car EMI, a credit card with a ₹2L limit that he paid "mostly" every month, and a SIP of ₹5,000/month that he'd started and forgotten about. When his father had a cardiac event, the hospital bill was ₹6.2L. Rahul didn't have it. He borrowed from two colleagues, broke his FD (losing 2 years of interest), and paid with a personal loan at 14% interest. He was 33, earning ₹1.8L/month, and genuinely broke in a crisis.

The problem wasn't income. It was the absence of a system: no term insurance, no health insurance for parents, no emergency fund, no structure. His money evaporated into rent, EMIs, dining, subscriptions, and Swiggy before it could compound.

This guide is the system Rahul didn't have.

The most important habit: automate before you spend

On salary credit day, set standing instructions to move investment amounts to separate accounts/instruments automatically. What remains in your salary account is what you can spend. Never invert this — never invest "whatever is left at month-end." There is never anything left at month-end.

Step 1 — Foundation

Emergency Fund: Your Financial Immune System

Before stocks, before SIPs, before anything else — build an emergency fund. This is the single most important financial instrument you'll ever create because it prevents every other financial plan from being derailed by an unexpected event.

How much?

6 months of total expenses — not income, expenses. If your monthly expenses (rent + food + EMIs + insurance + parent support) total ₹70,000, your emergency fund = ₹4.2L. As your life complexity grows (child, home loan, dependent parents), raise this to 9–12 months.

Where to keep it?

  • Liquid Mutual Funds: Best option — 1-day redemption, ~6–7% returns, no lock-in, better than savings account. Use for bulk of the fund.
  • High-interest savings account: IDFC First, AU Bank offer 6–7% on savings. Keep 1 month's expenses here for instant access.
  • NOT in Fixed Deposits: Premature withdrawal penalties defeat the purpose in an actual emergency.
  • NOT in equities: Markets can be down 30% exactly when you need the money.

How to build it?

Allocate ₹10,000–15,000/month specifically for the emergency fund until it's full (4–6 months to complete). Once built, stop contributing — route that amount to investments instead. Replenish within 3 months if you ever use it.

Rule: The emergency fund is not an investment — it's insurance against your investments. Its job is to be boring, liquid, and always there. Resist the urge to "put it to better use."

Step 2 — Protection

Insurance: Protect Everything Before You Build Anything

Insurance is not an investment. It is risk transfer. Buying insurance is paying a small certain cost to avoid a large uncertain catastrophe. Skipping insurance to save premium is the most expensive financial mistake an IT professional can make.

1. Term Life Insurance (Priority: URGENT)

A pure term plan pays your family a lump sum if you die during the policy term. No investment component. No surrender value. Just protection — and that's exactly right.

  • How much cover: 15–20x your annual income. At ₹18L/year: ₹2.7–3.6Cr cover. Round up to ₹1–1.5Cr minimum; ₹2Cr is ideal if premium fits.
  • Policy term: Until age 60–65, or until your youngest dependent (child) is financially independent.
  • When to buy: TODAY. Premium is lowest when you are young and healthy. A 28-year-old pays ~₹8,000–12,000/year for ₹1Cr cover. At 38, the same cover costs ₹18,000–25,000/year.
  • Where to buy: LIC (highest claim settlement ratio, government backing), HDFC Life, ICICI Prudential, Max Life — all have 97%+ claim settlement. Compare on Policybazaar.
  • What to avoid: ULIPs (Unit Linked Insurance Plans) — they mix insurance and investment poorly, with high charges. Buy term separately and invest separately.

2. Health Insurance (Priority: URGENT)

Your MNC likely provides group health insurance — but it only covers you while you're employed there. You need a personal policy independent of your employer.

  • Family floater policy: Covers you + spouse + child under one sum insured. ₹10–15L cover is minimum for a Tier-1 city; ₹20–25L is safer. Premium: ₹15,000–25,000/year for a ₹15L family floater for a family of 3 under 35.
  • Super top-up: After your base cover is exhausted, a super top-up kicks in. E.g., base ₹5L (employer) + super top-up of ₹15L above ₹5L deductible = effective ₹20L cover at very low premium (~₹8,000–12,000/year). Smart way to get high coverage cheaply.
  • Key features to look for: Restore benefit (refills cover after a claim), no co-payment clause, day-care procedures covered, no room rent cap, pre- and post-hospitalization cover (60–90 days).
  • Waiting periods: Most policies have 2–4 year waiting periods for pre-existing conditions and maternity. Buy NOW — waiting periods run from the policy start date. By the time you need it (childbirth, parents' health), the waiting period is over.

3. Health Insurance for Parents (Priority: HIGH)

This is the most expensive and most neglected piece. Insuring parents above 55 is costly (₹30,000–60,000/year per person), but one hospitalisation without insurance can cost ₹3–8L for cardiac, orthopaedic, or cancer treatment.

  • Buy a dedicated senior citizen health policy for parents — separate from your family floater (insuring them on the same policy makes it expensive for everyone).
  • Target: ₹5–10L sum insured per parent. Niva Bupa, Star Health, Care Insurance have strong senior citizen plans.
  • If parents are uninsurable due to pre-existing conditions: create a dedicated medical corpus (FD or liquid fund of ₹5–10L) as a self-insurance fund.

4. Critical Illness Cover

Pays a lump sum if you're diagnosed with a listed critical illness (cancer, heart attack, stroke, kidney failure). This covers lost income and lifestyle changes during treatment — which regular health insurance doesn't. ₹25–50L cover; premium ₹5,000–8,000/year as a rider or standalone policy. Especially important for IT professionals who face high sedentary lifestyle risk.

5. Personal Accident Cover

Pays on accidental death or disability. ₹50L–1Cr cover costs only ₹2,000–4,000/year. Essential if you commute by road or travel frequently.

Step 3 — Investments

Investment Strategy: Build Wealth Systematically

Once the emergency fund is in place and insurance is bought, every rupee you invest should work for a specific goal. Invest with purpose, not randomly.

The Core Investment Framework

Three buckets by time horizon:

  • Short-term (0–3 years): Child's birth expenses, house down payment, car. Use: Liquid funds, arbitrage funds, FD, RD. Capital protection > returns.
  • Medium-term (3–7 years): Child's early schooling, home loan prepayment, parent care corpus. Use: Balanced advantage funds, debt mutual funds, PPF.
  • Long-term (7+ years): Child's higher education, retirement, wealth creation. Use: Equity mutual funds (index funds), NPS, PPF, direct equity (optional).

EPF — Don't Ignore It

Your MNC deducts 12% of basic salary as EPF (Employees' Provident Fund). This is compulsory and earns ~8.15% tax-free. Over a 30-year career, this alone can build a ₹1–2Cr corpus. Track your EPF balance on the EPFO portal. Consider voluntary PF contributions (VPF) if you want to increase this bucket.

Key Investment Principles

  • Don't try to time the market. SIPs work precisely because they invest regardless of market levels — you buy more units when markets are cheap.
  • Don't churn funds. Switching funds based on last year's returns destroys returns. Stick to a chosen fund for 5+ years before evaluating.
  • Step up SIPs annually. Increase SIP amount by 10–15% every April (after increment). ₹10K SIP stepped up 10% annually = ₹1.7Cr in 20 years. Without step-up = ₹76L.
  • Avoid direct stock picking until you have 2+ years of investment experience and a separate "learning capital" pool you're comfortable losing.

The power of starting early: ₹10,000/month from age 28 at 12% returns = ₹3.5Cr at 58. Starting at 38 with the same amount = ₹1Cr at 58. The 10-year head start is worth ₹2.5Cr. Every year you delay is extraordinarily expensive.

80C Instruments (₹1.5L/year deduction)

  • EPF: Already deducted; counts toward 80C
  • PPF: ₹50,000/year planned above; counts toward 80C
  • ELSS (Equity Linked Savings Scheme): 3-year lock-in mutual funds; tax-saving + equity returns. Use if 80C limit not exhausted by EPF + PPF alone.
  • Life insurance premium: Term plan premium counts toward 80C

  • NPS 80CCD(1B): Additional ₹50,000 deduction over and above 80C limit — pure tax saving
  • Home loan: Interest deduction up to ₹2L/year (section 24b) — significant for high-cost city buyers
  • Children's tuition fees: Up to ₹1.5L per child under 80C (already within 80C limit)
  • Sovereign Gold Bonds: No capital gains tax on maturity if held to 8 years
  • LTCG on equity: ₹1.25L/year is exempt; harvest gains annually to reset cost basis
Step 5 — Home

Home Buying: The Biggest Decision, Done Right

Buying a home in a Tier-1 city is a ₹80L–2Cr decision. Done right, it's an asset and psychological anchor. Done wrong (with under-saving, over-borrowing, or at the wrong time), it strains every other financial goal for 20 years.

Rent vs Buy — The honest answer

In Tier-1 cities with price-to-rent ratios of 30–50x (meaning a ₹1Cr flat rents for ₹20,000–33,000), renting is often financially superior to buying until you have a 20–30% down payment, stable family location, and a home loan EMI below 35% of take-home pay. Don't let social pressure or family expectation rush you into a home loan you can't comfortably service.

When to buy

  • You have saved 20–25% of the property value as down payment (no LTV >80%)
  • Post-EMI take-home is still enough to continue SIPs, insurance, and savings — don't stop investing to service a home loan
  • You plan to stay in the city for at least 5–7 years
  • Emergency fund is fully intact (home purchase should not deplete it)

The Down Payment Plan

For a ₹1Cr flat (entry level in Tier-1 cities), you need ₹20–25L down payment + ₹5–7L for registration, stamp duty, and interiors. Total: ₹25–32L. At ₹5,000/month in a liquid fund + ₹3–5L lump sum from bonuses annually, this takes 4–6 years. Start the down payment SIP now even if the purchase is 5 years away.

Home Loan Guidelines

  • EMI should be ≤35% of net take-home pay
  • Take a floating rate loan (linked to RLLR/EBLR) — typically 1–2% cheaper than fixed rate in early years
  • Prepay aggressively in years 1–10 (interest front-loading means early prepayment saves the most)
  • Don't take a home loan just for tax benefits — the net saving after interest paid is often negative
  • Keep home loan tenure at 15–20 years, not 30 years, even if the bank offers 30
Step 6 — Child Planning

Planning for Your Child: From Birth to Graduation

A child's education costs are one of the largest and most predictable expenses in a parent's life — and inflation in education outpaces general inflation by 10–12% annually. Planning 2 years before birth gives you an 18–20 year runway. Use it.

Birth Costs (immediate)

  • Normal delivery at private hospital: ₹50,000–1,20,000
  • C-section: ₹1,20,000–2,50,000
  • Post-birth expenses (pram, feeding, first year): ₹50,000–1,00,000
  • Action: Ensure your health insurance's maternity waiting period is over (usually 2–4 years). If not, check hospital cash benefit plans. Start a dedicated child birth fund (liquid fund) of ₹2–3L now.

Early Childhood (0–5 years)

  • Daycare or nanny: ₹8,000–25,000/month (Tier-1 city)
  • Pre-school fees: ₹50,000–1,50,000/year
  • Budget ₹12,000–20,000/month additional expense post-child in the first 5 years
  • One of you taking a career break is a real possibility — model this in your finances

Child Education Fund Plan

Start a ₹5,000/month SIP in an equity index fund (or a dedicated child education plan) from now. Over 18 years at 12% CAGR, this grows to approximately ₹52L — enough for domestic engineering or MBA. Over 20 years: ₹75L+.

To target ₹1Cr+ (for top private college or foreign education), step up to ₹8,000–10,000/month and step up 10% annually. This gets you to ₹1–1.3Cr over 18–20 years.

Don't bet everything on JEE/NEET. Plan for the middle path — a good private college with full fees, not just the hope of a government college seat. Education is the worst area to underfund; education loans at 9–12% interest start the child's career in debt.

Child Insurance — What to Buy and What to Avoid

  • Buy: A child rider on your term insurance (ensures premium waiver if you die — policy continues without payment)
  • Avoid: Child ULIPs and traditional child plans — high charges, low returns, poor flexibility. A simple equity SIP outperforms any child ULIP over 10+ years.
  • Your term life insurance + child education SIP = the complete child financial plan
Step 7 — Parents

Supporting Parents: Plan Proactively, Not Reactively

Supporting ageing parents is a reality for most Indian IT professionals in Tier-1 cities whose parents remain in their hometown. Without a plan, this becomes reactive and emotionally + financially disruptive.

Have the financial conversation now

Most families never discuss money openly. Before you can plan, you need to know:

  • Do your parents have pension income, rental income, or savings?
  • What are their monthly expenses today? What will they need from you?
  • Do they own property? Is there a will? (If not, write one — and get them to write one too.)
  • What are their current health conditions? Any pre-existing diseases?

Monthly Support Budget

Decide a fixed monthly transfer — say ₹10,000–20,000/month — and make it a non-negotiable line item in your budget. Don't send money "when they ask." Proactive, regular support is less stressful and more dignified for both parties.

Medical Emergency Corpus for Parents

Even with health insurance, parents will face co-payments, non-covered treatments, and the emotional and logistical burden of hospital management. Build a dedicated "parents' medical fund" of ₹5–10L in a liquid fund, separate from your personal emergency fund. Contribute ₹3,000–5,000/month until the target is reached, then pause and restart only after a withdrawal.

Future care planning

  • If parents will eventually live with you: factor larger home (more BHKs) into your home buying plan
  • If parents remain in hometown: build a "hometown support network" — a trusted local family member or professional who can help in acute situations
  • Eldercare costs (care home, nursing) are rising rapidly in India. For parents currently 55–65, model ₹15,000–30,000/month eldercare costs from age 75+ onward in your retirement plan.
Step 8 — Retirement

Retirement Planning: The Goal Everything Else Serves

Retirement feels far when you're 30. It is not. You need to accumulate enough wealth that it generates income forever without you working. This is called financial independence, and it is the ultimate goal of all financial planning.

The Number: How much do you need to retire?

The 4% rule (from the Trinity Study, US-based but directionally applicable): your corpus should be 25x your annual expenses at retirement.

If your lifestyle at 60 costs ₹1.5L/month (₹18L/year in today's money), adjusted for 6% inflation over 30 years = ₹1.03Cr/year in 2055 rupees. Corpus needed = ₹25Cr. That sounds enormous — but with EPF + PPF + NPS + equity SIPs + company bonuses compounded over 30 years, it's achievable for an IT professional who starts investing seriously at 30.

  • Asset allocation: 75% equity (up to age 50) via auto-choice — maximise equity in early years
  • Choose NPS CRA through your employer for additional tax deduction (80CCD2 — employer contribution is deductible)
  • NPS annual charges: 0.01% — the cheapest retirement instrument in India
Step 9 — Health

Health Planning: Your Most Valuable Asset Is Your Body

IT professionals in Tier-1 cities face a specific health risk profile: sedentary work, high stress, irregular sleep, poor diet (Swiggy dependency), and little physical activity. By their mid-30s, many have pre-diabetes, hypertension, or obesity — all of which increase insurance premiums, reduce earning capacity, and shorten careers.

Health as a Financial Strategy

  • Every preventive rupee saves ₹10–50 in future medical treatment. Annual check-ups that catch diabetes early save lakhs in long-term management.
  • Maintain health insurance continuity — a lapse resets waiting periods and can make you uninsurable if a condition develops in the gap.
  • Don't drink, smoke, or use tobacco. These increase term insurance premiums significantly and create massive future health liabilities.
  • Build 7–8 hours of sleep as a non-negotiable. Sleep deprivation impairs decision-making — including financial decisions.

The financial case for health: A healthy IT professional can work until 55–60 with high earnings. An unhealthy one may be forced out of demanding roles by 45 due to burnout, chronic conditions, or caregiving demands. The difference in lifetime earning potential is ₹1–3Cr. Invest ₹1–2L/year in health to protect ₹1–3Cr in earnings.

Step 10 — Career

Career as a Financial Asset: Protect and Grow It

Your earning capacity is your largest financial asset. At 30, you have 25–30 years of earning ahead — valued at ₹5–8Cr in total lifetime earnings at current salary. Protect this asset.

Income Protection Checklist

  • Always maintain 3 months of interview-ready state: Updated LinkedIn, polished resume, 2–3 live projects. Job markets move fast; being prepared is an insurance policy.
  • Build a skill buffer: In IT, skills depreciate in 3–5 years. Invest ₹20,000–50,000/year in learning (courses, certifications, conferences). ROI is 10–50x in salary increments.
  • Negotiate every 2 years: At MNCs, staying in the same role for 5+ years often means salary stagnation. External offers reset your market value. At ₹1.5L/month, a 20% raise is ₹30,000/month — ₹3.6L/year — worth negotiating aggressively.
  • Build savings before switching: If you plan a break, career change, or startup attempt, ensure 12 months of expenses saved and all EMIs manageable on zero income before leaping.

Year-by-Year Salary Growth Target

At an MNC in IT, targeting 15–20% annual CTC growth through a combination of appraisal increments + strategic job changes is realistic for the first 10 years. ₹18L at 28 → ₹40–50L at 35 → ₹80L–1Cr+ at 40 for strong performers. Each salary step-up should be matched with a proportional investment step-up, not a lifestyle step-up.

// ── SIP Future Value Calculator ───────────────────────────────────────────────
function sipFutureValue(monthlyAmount, annualReturnPercent, years) {
  const r = annualReturnPercent / 100 / 12; // monthly rate
  const n = years * 12;                      // total months
  // Future value of annuity: FV = P × [(1+r)^n - 1] / r × (1+r)
  const fv = monthlyAmount * (((Math.pow(1 + r, n) - 1) / r) * (1 + r));
  return Math.round(fv);
}

function stepUpSIP(initialMonthlyAmount, annualReturnPercent, years, stepUpPercent = 10) {
  const monthlyRate = annualReturnPercent / 100 / 12;
  let corpus = 0;
  let currentSIP = initialMonthlyAmount;

  for (let year = 1; year <= years; year++) {
    // Each year's SIP contribution compounds for the remaining years
    const monthsRemaining = (years - year + 1) * 12;
    const yearlyFV = currentSIP * (((Math.pow(1 + monthlyRate, monthsRemaining) - 1) / monthlyRate) * (1 + monthlyRate));
    corpus += yearlyFV - (currentSIP * monthsRemaining); // subtract principal added after year boundary
    // Simpler: accumulate month by month
    currentSIP = Math.round(currentSIP * (1 + stepUpPercent / 100));
  }

  // More accurate month-by-month simulation
  corpus = 0;
  currentSIP = initialMonthlyAmount;
  let balance = 0;
  for (let month = 1; month <= years * 12; month++) {
    balance = (balance + currentSIP) * (1 + monthlyRate);
    if (month % 12 === 0) {
      currentSIP = Math.round(currentSIP * (1 + stepUpPercent / 100));
    }
  }
  return Math.round(balance);
}

// ── Emergency Fund Adequacy Check ────────────────────────────────────────────
function emergencyFundCheck(monthlyExpenses, currentFund, targetMonths = 6) {
  const target = monthlyExpenses * targetMonths;
  const gap    = Math.max(0, target - currentFund);
  const months = gap > 0 ? Math.ceil(gap / (monthlyExpenses * 0.15)) : 0; // saving 15% of expenses

  return {
    targetAmount: target,
    currentAmount: currentFund,
    gap,
    fullyFunded: gap === 0,
    monthsToFund: months,
    status: gap === 0 ? '✅ Fully funded' : `⚠️  Need ₹${gap.toLocaleString('en-IN')} more (${months} months at current pace)`,
  };
}

// ── Retirement Corpus Estimator ───────────────────────────────────────────────
function retirementEstimate(currentAge, retirementAge, monthlyExpenseToday, inflationPercent = 6) {
  const yearsToRetire = retirementAge - currentAge;
  const futureMonthlyExpense = monthlyExpenseToday * Math.pow(1 + inflationPercent / 100, yearsToRetire);
  const futureAnnualExpense  = futureMonthlyExpense * 12;
  const corpusNeeded         = futureAnnualExpense * 25; // 4% withdrawal rule

  return {
    yearsToRetire,
    futureMonthlyExpense: Math.round(futureMonthlyExpense),
    futureAnnualExpense:  Math.round(futureAnnualExpense),
    corpusNeeded:         Math.round(corpusNeeded),
  };
}

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I buy a house or keep renting in a Tier-1 city?
Rent if your price-to-rent ratio is above 30x (i.e., buying price divided by annual rent exceeds 30). In most Tier-1 cities, this favors renting in the early career years. Buy when you have a 20–25% down payment saved and the EMI is under 35% of take-home. Do not buy under social pressure — a wrong home purchase at the wrong time can set back financial goals by 5–7 years.
How much should I send my parents every month?
There's no universal answer — it depends on their income, health, and needs. Have an explicit conversation. Typical range: ₹8,000–25,000/month, rising over time. Make it a fixed line item in your budget, not reactive. Also separately maintain a parents' medical corpus of ₹5–10L for emergencies.
Is an LIC policy a good investment?
LIC traditional plans (endowment, money-back) are poor investment products — returns of 4–6% are well below inflation. Buy LIC for their excellent term plans (pure protection, high claim settlement). For investment, use mutual funds and PPF. Never mix insurance and investment.
My company gives health insurance. Do I need a personal policy?
Yes, always. Group corporate insurance disappears if you leave your job (including during notice period or layoffs). Many people are uninsured in exactly the vulnerable period of a job transition. A personal health policy is portable and cumulative (no-claim bonus builds over years). Buy it when you're healthy — you can't easily buy it after a diagnosis.
When should I start a child's education fund?
2 years before the child is born — ideally now. With 20 years of compounding, even ₹2,000/month grows to ₹20L+ at 12% returns. The single biggest determinant of the child education corpus size is how early you start, not how much you invest per month.
What's the safest way to invest money I might need in 2–3 years?
Never put short-term money (under 3 years) in equity funds. Use: liquid mutual funds (redemption in 1 business day, ~6–7% returns), arbitrage funds (equity taxation but near-debt risk), bank FDs (safe, predictable), or recurring deposits. Capital protection matters more than returns for money you'll need soon.
How do I handle a sudden large expense (medical, parent emergency) without derailing my plan?
This is exactly what the emergency fund and parents' medical corpus are for — use them without guilt. The system is designed for this. After the emergency, systematically replenish within 3–6 months. Do not stop SIPs to handle an emergency; the emergency fund is precisely so SIPs never have to stop.

Key Takeaway

Financial security is not about how much you earn — it's about having a system that works automatically before you have a chance to make emotional decisions with your money. The system is straightforward: protect first (insurance + emergency fund), then invest with purpose (one goal per instrument), then optimise (tax, step-ups, prepayments). An IT professional earning ₹1.5L/month in a Tier-1 city who runs this system from age 28 will retire at 58 with no debt, a paid-off home, a funded child's education, parents cared for, and a corpus that generates more passive income than their salary ever did. The only thing that can prevent this outcome is not starting.
