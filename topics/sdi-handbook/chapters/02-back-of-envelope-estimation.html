Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation

Estimation turns a vague scale statement into QPS, storage, bandwidth, cache and server counts, so you can pick an architecture that matches the load instead of guessing. This chapter covers powers of two, latency numbers, the nines of availability, a worked micro-blog example, and a reusable method with the arithmetic shown.

An estimate answers one question: which order of magnitude are we in? The difference between 100 QPS and 100,000 QPS is the difference between one small database and a sharded fleet. Nobody needs the third significant digit; everybody needs the exponent.

Core idea: do the arithmetic out loud, with round numbers and labelled units, then convert the result into an architectural decision. The process is graded, not the final number.

A well-known source

"Numbers everyone should know"

The habit comes from Jeff Dean, a Google Senior Fellow, who publicly described using thought experiments and a short list of latency and throughput constants to decide whether a design is even plausible before building it. His list, later popularised as "latency numbers every programmer should know", has been updated by others as hardware changed. The exact values drift; the ratios (memory is orders of magnitude faster than disk; a datacenter round trip is orders of magnitude faster than a cross-continent one) have stayed remarkably stable, and the ratios are what drive design.

Tool 1

Powers of two and data sizes

Computers count in powers of two, but when estimating, treat each step as roughly a factor of a thousand. The binary and decimal figures differ by under 10 percent at the TB level and under 13 percent at the PB level, which is below the noise of your assumptions.

PowerApprox. valueNameThink of it as
2101 thousand1 KBA short text message
2201 million1 MBA small photo or a minute of compressed audio
2301 billion1 GBA feature-length SD video; fits in RAM trivially
2401 trillion1 TBOne commodity disk; a large single-node database
2501 quadrillion1 PBA storage cluster, not a server

Seconds and per-unit shortcuts worth memorising

  • A day has 86,400 seconds. Round to 105 when mental math must be fast: 1 million events per day is about 10 per second (exactly 11.6).
  • A month is about 2.5 million seconds; a year is about 31.5 million (roughly pi times 107).
  • 1 million per day is about 12 per second; 100 million per day is about 1,200 per second; 1 billion per day is about 12,000 per second.
  • 1 Gbps is about 125 MB/s; 10 Gbps is about 1.25 GB/s. Network is quoted in bits, storage in bytes, so check which one you are holding.
Tool 2

Latency numbers and what they imply

OperationApprox. latencyRelative to memory read
L1 cache reference0.5 ns1/200
L2 cache reference7 ns1/14
Main memory reference100 ns1x
SSD random readabout 150 µs1,500x
Round trip within a data centerabout 500 µs5,000x
HDD seekabout 10 ms100,000x
Round trip between continentsabout 150 ms1,500,000x

These are order-of-magnitude figures for modern (circa 2020) hardware; cloud NVMe can be faster than the SSD figure and congested networks slower than the data-center one. Do not quote them as constants; use them as ratios. Five design rules fall out directly:

  1. Memory is fast, disk is slow. Serve the hot working set from RAM; that is the whole case for caches.
  2. Avoid random disk seeks. Sequential IO on a spinning disk is hundreds of times faster than random IO, which is why log-structured storage (chapter 6) appends and merges instead of updating in place.
  3. Network hops cost real time. A page that makes 40 sequential service calls at 0.5 ms each spends 20 ms just on round trips, before any work. Batch, parallelise, or co-locate.
  4. Crossing regions is expensive. A synchronous cross-region write adds on the order of 100+ ms to every request. This is why multi-region systems replicate asynchronously and accept anomalies.
  5. Compress before sending over a slow or metered link. CPU is cheap compared with bytes on the wire, especially across regions.
Tool 3

Availability and the nines

Availability is the fraction of time a service is usable, quoted in nines. Convert it into an allowed downtime budget, because the budget is what you plan operations against.

AvailabilityNinesDowntime per yearPer month (30 d)
99%2about 3.65 daysabout 7.2 hours
99.9%3about 8.8 hoursabout 43 minutes
99.99%4about 52.6 minutesabout 4.3 minutes
99.999%5about 5.3 minutesabout 26 seconds
99.9999%6about 32 secondsabout 2.6 seconds

Major cloud providers typically publish service-level agreements around three to four nines for individual services; they do not promise five for a single zone. Remember the vocabulary: an SLI is a measurement (successful requests / total), an SLO is your internal target for it, and an SLA is the contractual promise with penalties, usually looser than the SLO.

Composite availability: the arithmetic interviewers love

  • In series (a request needs A and B): multiply. Two 99.9% components give 0.999 x 0.999 = 99.8%. A chain of ten 99.9% dependencies is only about 99.0%, which is 3.65 days a year.
  • In parallel (either A or B suffices, failures independent): 1 minus the product of failure rates. Two independent 99% replicas give 1 - 0.01 x 0.01 = 99.99%.
Independence is the catch. Replicas in the same rack, behind the same deploy pipeline, or sharing the same bug do not fail independently. Real availability gains come from separating fault domains (zones, regions, release rings), not merely from adding copies.
Worked example

A micro-blog service: QPS and storage

Assumptions (write them down first)

  • 300 million monthly active users (MAU); 50 percent use it on a given day.
  • Each active user posts 2 times per day on average.
  • 10 percent of posts carry one media item averaging 1 MB.
  • Data is retained 5 years.

Traffic

DAU = 300M x 50% = 150M posts/day = 150M x 2 = 300M write QPS = 300M / 86,400 s ≈ 3,470 → say ~3,500 peak QPS = 2 x 3,500 ≈ 7,000 (peak factor 2 is an assumption)

Storage

media/day = 150M x 2 x 10% x 1 MB = 30 TB/day 5-year media = 30 TB x 365 x 5 ≈ 55 PB text/day = 300M x ~200 B ≈ 60 GB/day (≈ 0.1 PB in 5 years: noise next to media) x3 replicas ≈ 165 PB raw (or ~80 PB with erasure coding at ~1.5x)

Bandwidth, the number people forget

ingress = 30 TB / 86,400 s ≈ 350 MB/s ≈ 2.8 Gbps average, ~6 Gbps at peak egress = assume each media item is viewed 100 times: 30 TB x 100 = 3 PB/day ≈ 35 GB/s ≈ 280 Gbps average. That is 100x ingress and far beyond one origin: a CDN is non-optional.

Takeaways: 3,500 write QPS is modest for a partitioned store but not for one primary; the 55 PB number says object storage plus CDN, not a database; and media dominates, so the text path can be designed for speed and the media path for cost. A note on the source figures: a tweet identifier is normally a 64-bit integer, i.e. 8 bytes, not 64 bytes, and 140 characters has long since become 280. Neither changes the conclusion because the media term swamps both, which is itself a lesson in noticing what dominates.

Method

A repeatable estimation recipe

StepFormulaDesign decision it drives
1. UsersDAU = MAU x active ratioEverything below scales from this.
2. Average QPSDAU x actions per user per day / 86,400Number of app servers, DB write capacity.
3. Peak QPSaverage x peak factor (2 to 10; consumer apps spike with events)Capacity you must provision or autoscale to; queues to flatten bursts.
4. Read/write ratioassume it explicitly (feeds read heavily, logging write heavily)Replicas, cache, CQRS, choice of storage engine.
5. Storagerecords/day x bytes/record x retention days x replicationSQL vs object store; sharding; tiering hot/cold.
6. Bandwidthbytes/s = QPS x payload sizeNIC limits, CDN need, compression.
7. Cachehot 20 percent of daily read bytes (the 80/20 rule), then check it fits RAMCache cluster size and whether caching is viable at all.
8. Serverspeak QPS / per-server QPS, plus N+1 or N+2 headroomFleet size, autoscaling bounds.

How many servers? Little's law

Concurrency in flight = arrival rate x latency. At 7,000 QPS and 50 ms average service time, there are about 350 requests in flight. If one server comfortably runs 100 concurrent requests, you need 4 plus headroom, so call it 6 to 8. The same law sizes connection pools and thread pools. Utilisation above roughly 70 percent makes queueing delay grow sharply, so size for 50 to 60 percent at peak, not 100.

Tips

Habits that separate good estimates from bad ones

  • Round aggressively. 99,987 / 9.1 is 100,000 / 10 = 10,000. Nobody is checking the fifth digit.
  • Write assumptions first and label units. "5" is meaningless; "5 MB" and "5 million per day" are not. Unit errors (bits vs bytes, per second vs per day) are the usual cause of an estimate that is off by 8x or 86,400x.
  • Find the dominant term. In the micro-blog, media is 99.9 percent of bytes. Spend your precision there and ignore the rest.
  • Cross-check two ways. Estimate storage from users x items and from throughput x time; if they disagree by 10x, an assumption is wrong.
  • Be conservative with peaks and generous with growth. Plan for 2 to 3 years of growth unless told otherwise, and state the planning horizon.
  • End with a decision. "That is 55 PB, so object storage with tiering" is the sentence that scores points.
Traps. Forgetting replication (x3); forgetting indexes (often 20 to 100 percent extra on relational stores); confusing DAU with concurrent users (concurrency is usually a few percent of DAU); treating average QPS as capacity; ignoring read amplification (one feed read may fan out to dozens of backend calls).
Code

Runnable example: a small estimator with assertions

The script encodes the recipe above as one function, reproduces the micro-blog example, converts nines into downtime, and sizes a hot-set cache with the 80/20 rule. Every number printed in the worked example can be regenerated by changing the inputs.

JavaScript Example 
import assert from 'node:assert/strict';

const SEC_PER_DAY = 86_400, DAYS_PER_YEAR = 365;
const KB = 1e3, MB = 1e6, GB = 1e9, TB = 1e12, PB = 1e15;   // decimal, as used when estimating
const human = b => b >= PB ? (b / PB).toFixed(1) + ' PB' : b >= TB ? (b / TB).toFixed(1) + ' TB'
  : b >= GB ? (b / GB).toFixed(1) + ' GB' : b >= MB ? (b / MB).toFixed(1) + ' MB' : b + ' B';

// Downtime allowed per year for N nines
const downtime = nines => {
  const s = (10 ** -nines) * DAYS_PER_YEAR * SEC_PER_DAY;
  return s >= 3600 ? (s / 3600).toFixed(2) + ' h' : s >= 60 ? (s / 60).toFixed(1) + ' min' : s.toFixed(1) + ' s';
};
for (const n of [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]) console.log(`${n} nines -> ${downtime(n)}/year`);

// Generic write-heavy estimator
function estimate({ mau, dauRatio, actionsPerUserDay, peakFactor, bytesPerAction, mediaRatio = 0, mediaBytes = 0,
                    retentionYears, replication = 1, readWriteRatio = 1, rpsPerServer = 1000 }) {
  const dau = mau * dauRatio;
  const writesPerDay = dau * actionsPerUserDay;
  const qps = writesPerDay / SEC_PER_DAY;
  const peakQps = qps * peakFactor;
  const bytesPerDay = writesPerDay * (bytesPerAction + mediaRatio * mediaBytes);
  const mediaPerDay = writesPerDay * mediaRatio * mediaBytes;
  const total = bytesPerDay * DAYS_PER_YEAR * retentionYears * replication;
  const readPeak = peakQps * readWriteRatio;
  return { dau, qps, peakQps, mediaPerDay, bytesPerDay, total, readPeak,
           servers: Math.ceil((peakQps + readPeak) / rpsPerServer) };
}

// The classic micro-blog example: 300M MAU, 50% DAU, 2 posts/day, 10% with 1 MB media, 5 years
const r = estimate({ mau: 300e6, dauRatio: 0.5, actionsPerUserDay: 2, peakFactor: 2,
                     bytesPerAction: 204, mediaRatio: 0.1, mediaBytes: MB, retentionYears: 5 });
console.log('DAU            ', r.dau / 1e6, 'M');
console.log('write QPS      ', Math.round(r.qps), '(peak', Math.round(r.peakQps) + ')');
console.log('media per day  ', human(r.mediaPerDay));
console.log('5y media       ', human(r.mediaPerDay * DAYS_PER_YEAR * 5));
console.log('5y total x3    ', human(estimate({ mau: 300e6, dauRatio: .5, actionsPerUserDay: 2, peakFactor: 2,
  bytesPerAction: 204, mediaRatio: .1, mediaBytes: MB, retentionYears: 5, replication: 3 }).total));

assert.equal(r.dau, 150e6);
assert.ok(Math.abs(r.qps - 3472) < 1);              // ~3,500 QPS
assert.ok(Math.abs(r.mediaPerDay - 30e12) < 1);     // 30 TB/day
assert.ok(Math.abs(r.mediaPerDay * 365 * 5 / PB - 54.75) < 0.01); // ~55 PB
assert.equal(downtime(3), '8.76 h'); assert.equal(downtime(5).startsWith('5.3'), true);
// Cache sizing via the 80/20 rule: cache the hot 20% of one day's read volume
const readsPerDay = r.dau * 10 /* timeline reads/user/day */, bytesPerRead = 2 * KB;
console.log('80/20 cache    ', human(0.2 * readsPerDay * bytesPerRead));
console.log('all checks passed');

Prints downtime for 2 to 6 nines (for example "3 nines -> 8.76 h/year"), then DAU 150 M, write QPS 3472 (peak 6944), 30 TB per day, about 54.8 PB over 5 years, 164.6 PB with 3x replication, and a 600 GB 80/20 cache, ending with "all checks passed". Executed with node.

Failure modes

How estimates go wrong

MistakeConsequenceFix
Sizing for average, not peakFalls over at the daily or event peakAlways apply a peak factor; examine the traffic shape (diurnal, flash sales)
Ignoring read amplification and fan-outUnderestimates internal QPS by 10x or moreEstimate backend calls per user request
Forgetting replication, indexes, overheadStorage plan 3x too smallMultiply by replication and an overhead factor
Assuming perfect independence for availabilityOverstates ninesIdentify shared dependencies and common-cause failures
Using cache size without hit-rate reasoningCache that thrashesEstimate the working set, not total data
Mixing bits and bytes, days and secondsError of 8x or 86,400xLabel every unit
Production

From estimate to capacity plan

  • Validate with load tests. The per-server QPS figure is the most guessed number in any plan; measure it with a realistic mix, not hello-world.
  • Plan for N+2. One node in maintenance and one failing should still leave you above peak.
  • Watch the knees. Latency does not degrade linearly; it climbs steeply past 70 to 80 percent utilisation on CPU, connection pools or disks. Alert on saturation, not only on errors.
  • Revisit after launch. Compare estimates with metrics, record the error, and correct your priors. Teams that do this get better quickly.
  • Cost is a dimension. 55 PB at a cloud object-storage list price is a very large monthly bill; tiering and compression are architectural choices driven by the estimate.
Checklist

Estimation checklist

  • Assumptions written down and confirmed with the interviewer.
  • DAU, average QPS, peak QPS, read/write ratio computed.
  • Storage includes retention, replication and overhead.
  • Bandwidth computed in the right unit; CDN need judged.
  • Cache sized from the hot working set.
  • Server count includes headroom.
  • Availability target converted to a downtime budget.
  • Each result linked to a design decision.
FAQ · Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

How precise do I need to be?

One significant digit and the right power of ten. State that you are rounding and move on; spending two minutes on exact division reads as poor prioritisation.

What peak factor should I assume?

Say 2 to 3 for steady consumer traffic, 5 to 10 for event-driven spikes (flash sales, live sports, push notification blasts), and confirm with the interviewer. Name the factor out loud; the exact value matters less than showing you considered peaks.

Should storage numbers use binary or decimal units?

Decimal is fine and simpler (1 TB = 1012 bytes). The binary difference is under 10 percent at TB scale, smaller than your assumption error. Only care when quoting disk specs versus OS-reported capacity.

How do I estimate cache size?

Take daily read volume in bytes, assume 20 percent of the content generates 80 percent of the reads, and cache that 20 percent. Then check it fits across a reasonable number of nodes (a few hundred GB to a few TB is a normal cache cluster); if it does not, cache a smaller slice or cache derived data.

Why does the estimate matter if architecture is a judgement call?

Because it picks the branch. Under about a thousand QPS and a few TB, a replicated relational database is likely enough. At hundreds of thousands of QPS or petabytes, you need partitioning, tiered storage and CDNs. The estimate tells you which regime you are in.

How do I go from a user count to concurrent connections for a chat or presence system?

Concurrent users are typically a small fraction of DAU, often 5 to 20 percent at peak depending on the product. Multiply by per-connection memory (tens of KB for a lean WebSocket server) to size the gateway fleet; see the WebSocket page for connection-limits detail.

Key Takeaway

Estimation is a decision tool, not a math test. Round aggressively, label units, state assumptions, and always finish by translating the number into a design consequence: one machine or a fleet, memory or disk, one region or two. A number that does not change the design was not worth computing.
