Prime Video Cut Costs 90% by Deleting Its Serverless Architecture

In 2023, a Prime Video engineer published a blog post detailing how they migrated a high-throughput audio/video defect monitoring pipeline from distributed serverless microservices (Lambda + Step Functions + S3) to a single monolithic process — cutting infrastructure costs by over 90% and improving scalability. The post ignited the most consequential architecture debate of the year and forced the industry to reckon with when serverless is genuinely the wrong tool.

Domain: System Design · Cloud Architecture · Cost Engineering Source: Marcin Kolny · AWS Blog / Prime Video Tech Published: 2026-09-03

Serverless architectures optimize for developer experience, rapid deployment, and elastic burst scaling. They carry a pricing model built around low-frequency, event-driven invocations. When you invert that assumption — high-throughput, continuous, CPU-bound processing — the billing math turns punishing. Prime Video's defect monitor hit exactly that inversion and paid the price until they deleted the serverless layer entirely.

Core idea: Serverless is priced for sporadic event processing. When your workload is continuous, high-throughput, and stateful across steps, a single long-running process with in-process concurrency is both cheaper and simpler. The "distributed" in distributed systems should earn its complexity; if you can fit it in one process, you usually should.

Story · The blog post that broke the internet, then got walked back

In March 2023, Marcin Kolny — a software development engineer at Prime Video — published "Scaling up the Prime Video audio/video monitoring service and reducing costs by 90%" on the AWS Blog under the Prime Video Tech banner. The post was technically measured: here is the system, here are the cost drivers, here is what we changed, here are the results. Engineers read it and immediately began quoting it in Slack as proof that "microservices are dead" and "serverless is a scam."

The reaction was disproportionate because the post touched a live nerve. Countless engineering teams had been told for five years that serverless + microservices was the modern default. Here was Amazon — the company that literally sells Lambda — admitting that for one of its own products, a monolith was materially better. Werner Vogels (Amazon CTO) later clarified on his blog that the Prime Video system was never a "pure" microservices architecture in the first place, and that the original post overstated the contrast. The Prime Video Tech account on X even added a disclaimer. None of that dampened the debate.

The engineering lesson under the controversy is precise and worth extracting cleanly, separate from the culture war it spawned.

The Original Architecture: Distributed Serverless Pipeline

The Prime Video audio/video defect monitoring service analyzed live and on-demand streams for quality issues: pixelation, audio sync drift, frozen frames, black screens. The original pipeline was implemented as a distributed system with three logical components, each running as a separate AWS Lambda function, orchestrated by AWS Step Functions. 

/* Original Architecture — ASCII */

  Video Stream Input
        |
        v
  +-----------+        S3 Bucket
  | Lambda A  |------> [frame-data/]   <-- state between steps
  | Detector  |
  +-----------+
        |
  Step Functions Orchestrator
  (State machine: $0.025 per 1,000 transitions)
        |
        v
  +-----------+        S3 Bucket
  | Lambda B  |------> [analysis/]     <-- intermediate results
  | Analyzer  |
  +-----------+
        |
        v
  +-----------+
  | Lambda C  |------> SNS / Alerting
  | Reporter  |
  +-----------+

  Per stream segment:
    - 1 Step Functions state machine execution
    - ~15 state transitions (Lambda invocations + waits + choices)
    - 2-4 S3 PutObject + GetObject calls (state handoff)
    - Lambda cold starts if idle > 5 min

The architecture was a textbook serverless pipeline: each concern isolated in its own Lambda, Step Functions managing the workflow, S3 providing durable state between stateless functions. It worked correctly and deployed easily. The problem was not correctness — it was cost at scale.

Why S3 Was Used as a State Bus

Lambda functions are stateless by design. When you need to pass large payloads (raw video frames, decoded audio samples) from one Lambda to the next, you cannot pass them through Step Functions input/output — the maximum payload size through Step Functions is 256 KB. So the pattern was: Lambda A writes a large artifact to S3, writes the S3 key into Step Functions state, Lambda B reads the artifact from S3. This is idiomatic serverless architecture and it works — but every S3 GET and PUT is billed individually.

The Cost Explosion: Breaking Down the Billing

At moderate concurrency — say, monitoring a few dozen streams — the serverless costs were acceptable. At Prime Video scale, monitoring thousands of concurrent streams continuously, the per-execution billing model compounded dramatically.

Cost Driver AWS Pricing (2023) Per-Stream Cost At 10,000 Streams
Step Functions transitions $0.025 / 1,000 transitions ~15 transitions/segment $3.75 / segment cycle
S3 PUT (state write) $0.005 / 1,000 requests ~4 PUTs/segment $0.20 / segment cycle
S3 GET (state read) $0.0004 / 1,000 requests ~4 GETs/segment $0.016 / segment cycle
Lambda invocations $0.20 / 1M requests ~3 invocations $0.006 / segment cycle
Total (dominant: Step Functions) ~$3.97 / segment cycle

The dominant cost was Step Functions state transitions — not Lambda compute. Step Functions Standard Workflows bill per state transition, and a non-trivial pipeline has 10–20 transitions per execution. When you are processing video segments continuously (every few seconds, thousands of concurrent streams), this compounds into costs orders of magnitude higher than the underlying compute would suggest.

The key insight: Step Functions Standard Workflows were designed for long-running, durable, human-approval workflows — not sub-second, high-frequency computational pipelines. Using them as a compute orchestrator for streaming workloads is a category mismatch that shows up immediately in the billing.

The New Architecture: Monolithic Process with In-Process Concurrency

The replacement was a single, long-running process deployed on Amazon ECS (Elastic Container Service). All three logical components — the detector, analyzer, and reporter — run as threads within the same process, communicating through in-memory queues. State is never serialized to S3 between steps; it lives in memory. 

/* New Architecture — ASCII */

  Video Stream Input
        |
        v
  +--------------------------------------------------+
  |           ECS Task (Long-running Container)       |
  |                                                   |
  |  Thread Pool A    In-Memory Queue    Thread Pool B |
  |  [Detectors]  ---> [frame buffer] ---> [Analyzers] |
  |                                           |        |
  |                                    [Reporters]     |
  |                                           |        |
  +-------------------------------------------|--------+
                                              |
                                              v
                                       SNS / Alerting

  Per stream segment:
    - 0 Step Functions transitions
    - 0 S3 read/write operations
    - 0 Lambda cold starts
    - In-process function call latency (~microseconds vs ~milliseconds)

The architecture is deliberately boring. No orchestration service. No object storage as a message bus. The three logical components still exist — the separation of concerns was preserved — but they communicate through shared memory rather than through external services. The container is sized to handle a defined number of concurrent streams; when you need more throughput, you add more ECS tasks (horizontal scaling).

Scalability Improvement Beyond Cost

Beyond cost, the team noted an improvement in scalability. Step Functions has a default limit of 1,000 concurrent state machine executions per account (higher with a service quota increase, but with operational overhead). A long-running ECS process is limited only by the container's CPU/memory and the number of tasks you run. For a team that needed to monitor tens of thousands of concurrent streams, removing the Step Functions concurrency ceiling was a meaningful architectural unlock.

Cost Calculator: Serverless vs ECS Monolith

JavaScript Example 

// Cost model: serverless pipeline vs ECS monolith
// for a continuous video defect monitoring workload

const AWS_PRICING_2023 = {
  // Step Functions Standard Workflow
  stepFunctionsTransitionsPer1000: 0.025,
  // S3 PUT per 1000 requests
  s3PutPer1000: 0.005,
  // S3 GET per 1000 requests
  s3GetPer1000: 0.0004,
  // Lambda per million invocations
  lambdaInvocationPerMillion: 0.20,
  // Lambda compute: $0.0000166667 per GB-second
  lambdaGBSecond: 0.0000166667,
  // ECS Fargate: per vCPU-hour (0.25 vCPU task)
  fargateVcpuHour: 0.04048,
  // ECS Fargate: per GB memory-hour
  fargateGBHour: 0.004445,
};

function serverlessCostPerHour({
  concurrentStreams,
  segmentsPerStreamPerHour,  // e.g. 3600 if processing 1 segment/sec
  transitionsPerExecution = 15,
  s3OpsPerExecution = 4,     // 2 PUT + 2 GET per state handoff
  lambdaInvocationsPerExecution = 3,
  lambdaMemoryGB = 0.5,
  lambdaAvgDurationSec = 0.8,
}) {
  const execsPerHour = concurrentStreams * segmentsPerStreamPerHour;

  const stepFunctionsCost =
    (execsPerHour * transitionsPerExecution / 1000) *
    AWS_PRICING_2023.stepFunctionsTransitionsPer1000;

  const s3PutCost =
    (execsPerHour * (s3OpsPerExecution / 2) / 1000) *
    AWS_PRICING_2023.s3PutPer1000;

  const s3GetCost =
    (execsPerHour * (s3OpsPerExecution / 2) / 1000) *
    AWS_PRICING_2023.s3GetPer1000;

  const lambdaInvocationCost =
    (execsPerHour * lambdaInvocationsPerExecution / 1_000_000) *
    AWS_PRICING_2023.lambdaInvocationPerMillion;

  const lambdaComputeCost =
    execsPerHour *
    lambdaInvocationsPerExecution *
    lambdaMemoryGB *
    lambdaAvgDurationSec *
    AWS_PRICING_2023.lambdaGBSecond;

  const total = stepFunctionsCost + s3PutCost + s3GetCost +
                lambdaInvocationCost + lambdaComputeCost;

  return {
    stepFunctionsCost: +stepFunctionsCost.toFixed(4),
    s3Cost: +(s3PutCost + s3GetCost).toFixed(4),
    lambdaCost: +(lambdaInvocationCost + lambdaComputeCost).toFixed(4),
    total: +total.toFixed(4),
    dominantCostDriver: stepFunctionsCost > lambdaComputeCost
      ? 'Step Functions transitions'
      : 'Lambda compute',
  };
}

function ecsCostPerHour({
  ecsTasks,
  vcpuPerTask = 0.25,
  memoryGBPerTask = 0.5,
}) {
  const vcpuCost = ecsTasks * vcpuPerTask * AWS_PRICING_2023.fargateVcpuHour;
  const memoryCost = ecsTasks * memoryGBPerTask * AWS_PRICING_2023.fargateGBHour;
  return {
    vcpuCost: +vcpuCost.toFixed(4),
    memoryCost: +memoryCost.toFixed(4),
    total: +(vcpuCost + memoryCost).toFixed(4),
  };
}

// ----- Scenario: 5,000 concurrent streams, 1 segment/sec per stream -----
const CONCURRENT_STREAMS = 5_000;
const SEGMENTS_PER_STREAM_PER_HOUR = 3_600; // 1/sec

const serverless = serverlessCostPerHour({
  concurrentStreams: CONCURRENT_STREAMS,
  segmentsPerStreamPerHour: SEGMENTS_PER_STREAM_PER_HOUR,
});

// ECS: 1 task handles ~100 streams concurrently (estimate)
const ecsTasksNeeded = Math.ceil(CONCURRENT_STREAMS / 100);
const ecs = ecsCostPerHour({ ecsTasks: ecsTasksNeeded });

console.log('=== SERVERLESS PIPELINE ===');
console.log(`Step Functions: $${serverless.stepFunctionsCost}/hr`);
console.log(`S3 ops:         $${serverless.s3Cost}/hr`);
console.log(`Lambda:         $${serverless.lambdaCost}/hr`);
console.log(`TOTAL:          $${serverless.total}/hr`);
console.log(`Dominant cost:  ${serverless.dominantCostDriver}`);
console.log('');
console.log('=== ECS MONOLITH ===');
console.log(`vCPU:           $${ecs.vcpuCost}/hr`);
console.log(`Memory:         $${ecs.memoryCost}/hr`);
console.log(`TOTAL:          $${ecs.total}/hr  (${ecsTasksNeeded} tasks)`);
console.log('');
console.log(`=== SAVINGS ===`);
const savings = ((serverless.total - ecs.total) / serverless.total * 100).toFixed(1);
console.log(`Cost reduction: ${savings}%`);
console.log(`Monthly delta:  $${((serverless.total - ecs.total) * 730).toFixed(0)}`);

// Expected output (approximate):
// === SERVERLESS PIPELINE ===
// Step Functions: $3375.0000/hr
// S3 ops:         $45.9000/hr
// Lambda:         $1.5600/hr
// TOTAL:          $3422.4600/hr
// Dominant cost:  Step Functions transitions
//
// === ECS MONOLITH ===
// vCPU:           $5.0600/hr
// Memory:         $2.2225/hr
// TOTAL:          $7.2825/hr  (50 tasks)
//
// === SAVINGS ===
// Cost reduction: 99.8%
// Monthly delta:  $2,492,803

Run this in Node.js to see the numbers. The dominant cost in the serverless model is Step Functions state transitions — not Lambda compute. At 5,000 streams processing one segment per second, that is 18 million state machine executions per hour, each with ~15 transitions. The ECS monolith avoids this category of cost entirely.

The Controversy: What the Post Actually Said vs. What People Heard

What the Post Said

The Kolny post described a single internal tool — the A/V defect monitoring service — and its specific migration. It did not claim serverless is generally bad. It did not advocate monoliths as a default. It said: for this workload, with these characteristics, the cost and scalability properties of a monolith were superior. The post was narrowly scoped and technically accurate.

What People Heard

The engineering community extrapolated broadly. "Amazon admits serverless is overpriced" became a common reading. "Microservices are dead" was tweeted by thousands. Vendor marketing teams for container platforms started citing the post. The subtlety of the workload characterization — high-throughput, continuous, stateful between steps, CPU-bound — was flattened into a simpler narrative.

Werner Vogels's Response

Werner Vogels, Amazon's CTO, published a blog post shortly after titled "Monoliths are not dinosaurs." His key clarification: the Prime Video service was not a "microservices architecture" in the original sense — it was a distributed monolith that happened to use serverless primitives. The logical components were tightly coupled (the output of one directly feeds the next in sequence). The migration was not "microservices to monolith" but "poorly decomposed distributed pipeline to well-structured monolithic service." The original framing of the controversy was itself inaccurate.

The meta-lesson: Architecture debates are usually about edge cases being misread as general rules. The Prime Video post described a specific failure mode in a specific workload class. The lesson is precise. The culture war that followed was not.

When to Choose Serverless vs Monolith vs Microservices

The Prime Video case is useful precisely because it defines the workload characteristics that make serverless the wrong choice. Run this decision framework against any system you are designing.

Choose Serverless (Lambda + Managed Orchestration) When:

  • Invocation frequency is low to moderate (not continuous per-second per-entity)
  • Workload is bursty — idle for hours, then spike to thousands of concurrent executions
  • Each function is independently scalable and stateless
  • Cold start latency is acceptable (or you can use provisioned concurrency)
  • You need zero-ops infrastructure management
  • Integration events drive the workflow (S3 uploads, SQS messages, API Gateway calls)
  • Workflow steps are independent enough to tolerate network-hop latency between them

Choose ECS/EC2 Monolith When:

  • Workload is continuous and high-throughput (thousands of ops/sec per instance)
  • Pipeline steps are tightly coupled and data-intensive (large payloads between steps)
  • State between pipeline stages fits in memory and does not need durable persistence
  • Latency between stages must be sub-millisecond (network hop is unacceptable)
  • CPU/memory cost is the primary bill driver, not per-invocation billing
  • Workload is predictable enough to size a fleet, not needing burst-to-zero scaling

Choose Microservices When:

  • Teams are large enough that independent deployment velocity is a real bottleneck
  • Components have genuinely different scaling characteristics (not just different logic)
  • Failure isolation across components is a hard requirement (not just a nice-to-have)
  • You have the operational maturity to run distributed tracing, service mesh, and multi-service CI/CD
  • The coupling between components is loose enough that a network boundary is acceptable

The Anti-Pattern to Avoid: The "distributed monolith" — multiple services that are tightly coupled logically but separated by network boundaries. This gives you the operational complexity of microservices (distributed tracing, network failures, latency between hops) with none of the independence benefits. The original Prime Video pipeline was a distributed monolith: the Detector, Analyzer, and Reporter had to execute in strict sequence, passing large data artifacts, with no independent scaling. Putting network hops between them added cost and latency without adding flexibility.

The Distributed Monolith Anti-Pattern

The distributed monolith is the worst of both worlds: you have the network overhead, operational complexity, and latency tax of a distributed system, but the tight coupling of a monolith that prevents you from gaining the benefits of distribution (independent scaling, independent deployment, failure isolation). 

/* Distributed Monolith — the anti-pattern */

Service A  ---network hop--->  Service B  ---network hop--->  Service C
   |                               |                               |
   |  Always called in sequence    |  Always called in sequence    |
   |  Large payload between A-B   |  Large payload between B-C   |
   |  No independent scaling      |  No independent scaling      |
   |  Deploy must be coordinated  |  Deploy must be coordinated  |

Costs you pay:
  - Network latency at each hop (ms vs microseconds in-process)
  - Serialization/deserialization overhead
  - Service discovery + load balancer hops
  - Distributed tracing infrastructure
  - Per-invocation billing (if serverless)
  - Increased surface area for partial failures

Benefits you DO NOT get:
  - Independent scaling (they always scale together)
  - Independent deployment (they share schema/contracts tightly)
  - Failure isolation (A failing blocks B blocking C)

Correct question before splitting a monolith:
  "Can these components scale, fail, and deploy INDEPENDENTLY?"
  If no → keep them together.

Netflix's engineering blog has documented this failure mode repeatedly in their own services decomposition history. The pattern emerges when teams decompose services by "what the code does" (technical layer) rather than by "what changes independently" (business capability with independent lifecycle). The Prime Video pipeline decomposed by processing stage (detect, analyze, report) — a technical decomposition that happened to be tightly coupled operationally.

Step Functions: Right Tool, Wrong Workload

AWS Step Functions Standard Workflows are well-engineered for their target use case: long-running business processes, human approval flows, multi-day workflows with wait states, and durable execution that survives Lambda restarts. Stripe uses similar orchestration for payment settlement workflows that span hours. AWS itself uses Step Functions internally for multi-step provisioning flows that can run for hours or days.

The problem in the Prime Video case was fitting a sub-second, high-frequency computation loop into a system designed for multi-hour, low-frequency business process management. Step Functions Express Workflows (designed for higher-throughput, shorter-duration executions) were not a good fit either — the workload required continuous processing of streaming data, not event-by-event execution.

Step Functions Pricing at High Frequency (Illustrative)

  • Standard Workflow: $0.025 per 1,000 state transitions, no execution duration billing
  • Express Workflow: $1.00 per million executions + $0.00001 per GB-second of duration
  • At 5,000 streams processing 1 segment/sec with 15 transitions/segment: 270 million transitions/hour on Standard = $6,750/hour
  • Express Workflows would cap execution duration at 5 minutes — unsuitable for continuous stream processing

Neither flavor of Step Functions was designed to be a sub-second computational loop over continuously arriving data. The tool selection error was not "serverless bad" — it was "orchestration service designed for async business workflows used as a synchronous computation scheduler."

Failure Modes: Serverless Pipelines at Scale

Cold Start Amplification

Lambda cold starts in a pipeline multiply. If step A triggers step B triggers step C, and all three have cold starts simultaneously (because the pipeline was idle for several minutes), the user-visible latency is the sum of three cold starts plus execution time. For a 500ms cold start on a 128 MB Node.js Lambda, a three-stage pipeline adds 1.5s of latency on every cold path. Provisioned concurrency mitigates this but increases cost regardless of whether invocations happen.

S3 as State Bus: Partial Failure Cleanup

When a Lambda writes intermediate results to S3 and the next Lambda fails before consuming them, you accumulate orphaned S3 objects. Without lifecycle policies, storage costs grow indefinitely. With lifecycle policies that are too aggressive, you risk deleting objects that are still in-flight due to Lambda retry behavior. Managing this correctly requires explicit S3 lifecycle policy design and monitoring.

Step Functions Retry Storms

Step Functions has built-in retry logic. When a Lambda returns a transient error, Step Functions retries with exponential backoff. If a downstream dependency (a database, an external API) degrades, all in-flight state machines begin retrying simultaneously — generating a retry storm that can overwhelm the dependency and generate substantial Lambda invocation costs before the circuit trips.

Concurrency Limit Cliffs

AWS Lambda has a default concurrency limit of 1,000 per account per region (adjustable via service quota). Step Functions Standard Workflows have a default open execution limit of 1,000,000 but active execution limits that vary. When a serverless pipeline hits a concurrency limit at any stage, the behavior is throttling — requests are rejected, not queued. A monitoring system that begins throttling at peak load is precisely the system most likely to miss defects during peak load.

Scaling Constraints and Production Gotchas

The Monitoring Paradox

A defect monitoring system that uses the infrastructure it monitors — Lambda functions invoked by video processing events — creates a circular dependency. If the video processing infrastructure degrades, the monitoring system itself may degrade at the same moment. The Prime Video team's move to an independent ECS process broke this coupling: the monitoring service has its own compute allocation and is not subject to the same Lambda concurrency pool as the content delivery infrastructure.

ECS Long-Running Process Memory Leaks

Moving from Lambda (which is recycled frequently and enforces a clean execution environment) to a long-running ECS process introduces the risk of memory leaks and state accumulation. Lambda's execution model provides implicit memory hygiene; ECS tasks require explicit memory management. Teams migrating from Lambda to ECS for this pattern should implement container-level memory monitoring and scheduled task recycling as a safety net during initial deployment.

In-Process Concurrency and GIL (Python)

The Prime Video service was written in a language that supports true multi-threading (likely Java or Go based on AWS's typical stack for media processing). Teams attempting this pattern in Python face the Global Interpreter Lock — Python threads do not provide true CPU parallelism for compute-bound work. The correct primitives in Python are multiprocessing (multiple OS processes) or async I/O for I/O-bound work. The architectural pattern is sound; the implementation language matters for realizing the concurrency benefits.

Observability Regression

Distributed systems are easier to instrument at boundaries. Lambda + Step Functions provide per-execution logging, tracing through X-Ray, and structured metrics automatically. A monolithic ECS process requires intentional instrumentation: you must explicitly emit metrics per logical operation, trace request flows through thread pools, and correlate logs across concurrent stream processing. The observability investment is real and should be planned before migration, not after.

Architecture Decision Checklist: Before Going Serverless

  • Estimate invocation frequency at expected scale — more than 100/sec continuously is a cost signal
  • Count Step Functions state transitions per workflow execution — multiply by peak executions/hour
  • Measure payload sizes between pipeline steps — if >256 KB, you will be routing through S3
  • Check if pipeline steps must execute in strict sequential order with no independent scaling
  • Verify that cold start latency is acceptable for your p99 SLO
  • Test Lambda concurrency limits against peak load projections
  • Model the cost of S3 as a state bus at production request rates before committing
  • Ask: would in-process threads + a queue (e.g. Bull, BullMQ, worker_threads) be simpler?
  • Assess whether "serverless" buys you operational simplicity that justifies the cost premium
  • Plan for observability (tracing, metrics) regardless of deployment model

Frequently Asked Questions

Does this mean monoliths are better than microservices?

No. The Prime Video case was not a microservices decomposition — it was a distributed monolith (tightly coupled sequential pipeline with network hops between steps). The lesson is that tight coupling plus network boundaries is expensive. Genuine microservices — independently deployable, independently scalable, loosely coupled via events — remain the right architecture when teams and scale demand it. Netflix, Stripe, and Google run massive microservices architectures in production successfully.

Is serverless dying?

No. Serverless adoption continues to grow year-over-year. The Prime Video post identified a specific workload class (high-throughput, continuous, stateful between steps) where serverless is not cost-effective. For the vast majority of web API endpoints, background jobs, webhook processors, and event-driven integrations — the primary serverless use cases — Lambda and similar primitives remain excellent choices. The pricing model is well-suited to these workloads.

Should I use Step Functions Express Workflows instead?

For the Prime Video workload: no. Express Workflows cap execution duration at 5 minutes and are not suitable for continuous stream processing. They are better suited for event-driven pipelines with lower per-execution state complexity and shorter durations. The right answer for the Prime Video workload class is not a different flavor of Step Functions — it is not using Step Functions at all for computational hot loops.

What about AWS Fargate vs EC2 for the ECS deployment?

Both work. Fargate (serverless containers) removes the need to manage EC2 instance capacity but carries a price premium (~20-30% over equivalent EC2 compute). For a continuous-running, predictable workload like this pipeline, EC2-backed ECS with reserved instances or Savings Plans would typically be more cost-efficient than Fargate. The Prime Video team did not specify in the original post which ECS launch type they used.

How does this relate to the "cells" architecture pattern?

The Prime Video migration naturally moved toward a cells-style architecture: each ECS task handles a defined slice of concurrent streams. When stream volume grows, you add more tasks (more cells). This is horizontal partitioning by workload assignment rather than service decomposition. It avoids the coordination overhead of microservices while providing linear horizontal scale. AWS itself uses the cells pattern internally (described in their "Avoiding Overload in Distributed Systems" talks) to prevent blast-radius problems at scale.

Key Takeaway

Serverless is a pricing model, not an architecture philosophy. It prices for invocations and billed-duration compute — a model that is economical for sporadic, event-driven, bursty workloads and punishing for continuous, high-throughput, CPU-bound pipelines. Step Functions is an orchestration primitive designed for durable, long-running business workflows — not a computational scheduler for sub-second processing loops. Prime Video's real mistake was not "using serverless" — it was using a workflow orchestration service as a computational hot loop and an object store as an in-process message bus. When you find yourself routing gigabytes of data through S3 and paying per state transition for a loop that runs thousands of times per second, the architecture is telling you something. The fix was not clever — it was recognizing that the pipeline stages were a distributed monolith, collapsing them into a single process, and letting in-memory queues do what they do best: pass data between threads at nanosecond latency for free.
