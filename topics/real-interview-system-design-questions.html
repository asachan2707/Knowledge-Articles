System Design Interview Handbook: Real Questions from 10+ Companies and 60+ Scenarios

Real system design questions from Google, Meta, Amazon, Apple, Netflix, Microsoft, Uber, Twitter/X, Airbnb, Stripe, and LinkedIn with full component breakdowns, plus a catalog of 60+ scenarios each reduced to its core insight. Includes the evaluation rubric, cross-cutting patterns, a 45-minute answer structure, and a runnable fan-out example.

Sources: interviewnoodle.com — Real Interviews at 10+ Companies; 60+ Real-World System Design Scenarios (Deep Concept, Medium)

Story · The Interview Room

Two Candidates Walk In

The room is small. A whiteboard on the wall, a single marker on the tray, and an interviewer who has seen hundreds of candidates. Candidate A walks in, picks up the marker, and immediately draws boxes. A box labeled "Client." An arrow to a box labeled "Server." An arrow to a box labeled "Database." The interviewer nods politely.

"Okay," says the interviewer. "A celebrity with 100 million followers posts a tweet. What happens?"

Candidate A stares at the boxes. "The client sends a request... to the server... which writes to the database... and then... all the followers read it?" The interviewer writes a note. Candidate A did not pass.

Candidate B walks in next. Before picking up the marker, Candidate B asks: "How many active users do we have? What is our write QPS at peak? How many followers does a typical user have versus a celebrity?" The interviewer relaxes. This is different.

When the celebrity tweet question comes, Candidate B does not skip a beat: "This is the classic fan-out problem. For regular users with, say, fewer than 10,000 followers, we fan out on write — we push post IDs into each follower's Redis timeline sorted set immediately. But for a celebrity with 100 million followers, synchronous fan-out would block for minutes. So we use a hybrid push-pull model: celebrity posts are stored separately and pulled at read time, merged with the pre-computed timeline from Redis. The trade-off is slightly higher read latency for celebrity content versus unbounded write amplification if we fan out to 100 million Redis keys in-line."

Candidate B passed. Not because the answer was correct — there is rarely one correct answer in system design — but because Candidate B knew the trade-offs, named the problem (fan-out), proposed a concrete mechanism (hybrid push-pull, Redis sorted sets), and explained the trade-off clearly (write amplification vs. read latency). That is what system design interviews evaluate. Not components. Trade-offs.

Framework

How System Design Interviews Are Evaluated

Every major tech company uses a rubric. The labels differ but the dimensions are remarkably consistent across Google, Meta, Amazon, and Microsoft. Understanding the rubric does not let you game the interview — it tells you where to invest your explanation time.

The Four Dimensions

(1) Problem Scoping — 20%. Did you ask the right clarifying questions before drawing? Did you distinguish functional requirements (what the system does) from non-functional requirements (how well it does it — latency, throughput, availability, consistency, geo-distribution)? A candidate who jumps straight to the whiteboard without scoping loses most of this dimension automatically.

(2) Component Design — 35%. This is the largest single dimension. Can you identify the right components for the problem? Can you explain the API contracts between them? Can you propose a data model? Interviewers want to see that you can translate a vague problem statement into concrete services with well-defined responsibilities.

(3) Scale Thinking — 25%. Can you reason about what breaks first as load grows? Can you identify the hot path? Can you propose horizontal scaling, sharding, or caching in the right places? This dimension rewards candidates who can do back-of-envelope math confidently: "At 1 million DAU with 10 posts per day average, that is about 115 writes per second, which a single Postgres instance handles easily — but at 100 million DAU, we need to think about sharding the write path."

(4) Trade-off Articulation — 20%. This is what separates senior from mid-level assessments. Did you acknowledge that every choice has a cost? Did you name the alternatives you considered and explain why you chose what you chose?

Interviewers grade process, not correctness. Two candidates can design the same system with opposite technology choices and both pass — if both articulate why they made those choices and what they gave up.

Method

The Three-Sentence Answer: How to Use the Scenario Catalog

System design interviews test pattern recognition under pressure. The candidate who passes is not the one who has the "correct" architecture memorised — it is the one who can identify which class of problem they are solving and which trade-offs are central to it. The scenario catalog later in this page organises 60+ problems into recognisable families, each with the core insight that unlocks the design.

How to use it: for each scenario, identify (1) the dominant constraint (read-heavy? write-heavy? latency-sensitive? consistency-critical?), (2) the breakpoint at scale (what fails first?), and (3) the one architectural decision that addresses both. That three-sentence answer is a passing response. Everything else is depth.

Google

Design Google Docs: Real-Time Collaborative Editing

This question appears in Google engineering interviews at L4 through L6 and is one of the most technically demanding on this list. The core difficulty is not building a document editor — it is handling concurrent edits from multiple users without corrupting the document state.

Key Components

WebSocket Server. Every active client maintains a persistent WebSocket connection. The server fans out edit operations to all clients currently viewing the same document. HTTP polling is a non-starter at Google Docs scale — you need sub-200ms latency for edits to feel real-time.

Operational Transformation (OT) Engine. This is the heart of the system. When two users type simultaneously at different cursor positions, their operations need to be transformed against each other so both views converge to the same final document. The OT engine runs both on the server (authoritative) and client (optimistic updates).

Document State Server. The canonical document state is stored in Google's Bigtable or Spanner depending on the consistency requirements. Spanner is preferred for documents that require strong consistency across geographic regions — but Bigtable is used for high-throughput append-only operation logs.

Presence Service. A lightweight service that tracks which users are in which documents, their cursor positions, and selection ranges. This is read-heavy and can use a separate Redis cluster with TTL-based expiry for cursor positions.

History and Version Store. All operations are stored in an append-only log. This enables document history, undo/redo, and version restoration. Compaction runs periodically to snapshot the document state.

OT vs. CRDT: The Key Trade-off

Operational Transformation is the classic approach — Google Docs uses it. CRDTs (Conflict-free Replicated Data Types) are the newer approach used by systems like Figma and Notion. OT requires a central server to order operations; CRDTs are peer-to-peer and always converge. The trade-off: OT has lower overhead for two-party editing but complexity grows with number of concurrent users; CRDTs are more complex upfront but scale to decentralized scenarios.

Most common mistake on this question: drawing a database and an API server without explaining conflict resolution at all. Interviewers flag "no conflict resolution explanation" as the single most common gap on the Google Docs question.

Meta / Facebook

Design News Feed: Fan-Out at Scale

The News Feed question is the canonical fan-out system design problem. It appears at Meta, Twitter, LinkedIn, and Snapchat interviews in various forms. The version asked at Meta is specifically focused on the feed generation pipeline and the celebrity problem.

Key Components

Feed Write Service. When a user creates a post, the Feed Write Service receives the event. For users with fewer than 10,000 followers, it fans out immediately — it writes the post ID into each follower's Redis timeline sorted set (scored by timestamp). This is fan-out on write, also called push-based feed.

Feed Read Service. When a user opens their feed, the Feed Read Service fetches the sorted set of post IDs from Redis (ZREVRANGE), hydrates the actual post objects from a separate post store, and merges in celebrity posts fetched on-demand. It then passes the post list through the EdgeRank ranking service before returning.

Redis Timeline Cache. Each user has a sorted set in Redis keyed by user ID. The set contains post IDs scored by epoch timestamp. Redis sorted sets support ZREVRANGEBYSCORE and ZREVRANGE efficiently. The set is capped at the last 1000 posts to control memory. This is the hot path — it must return in under 10ms.

EdgeRank ML Ranking Service. The raw chronological list from Redis is passed through EdgeRank (Meta's feed ranking model) which scores each post by affinity (how close are you to this person?), weight (is this a video, photo, link?), and time decay. The output is a re-ranked feed.

Media CDN. Post thumbnails, images, and videos are served from CDN edge nodes co-located with ISPs. The Feed Read Service returns post metadata with CDN URLs — it never serves media bytes directly.

The Celebrity Problem: Hybrid Push-Pull

If a user has 100 million followers and you fan out on write, you are writing 100 million Redis keys in-line with the post creation request. Even at 100,000 writes/second, that takes over 16 minutes — completely unacceptable. The solution is a threshold: above approximately 10,000 followers, skip the fan-out. Instead, maintain a separate "celebrity post" store and merge celebrity posts into the timeline at read time. The read is slightly heavier — you fetch the Redis timeline and do a set union with the celebrity posts — but it bounds write latency.

The most common mistake on the News Feed question is not asking about follower count distribution before proposing an architecture. If you assume all users are equal, you propose pure fan-out on write, and your system collapses when Cristiano Ronaldo posts a photo. Always ask: "What is the distribution of follower counts? What is the p99 follower count?"

Amazon

Design Product Page / Order System: Inventory Exactly-Once

Amazon's system design questions focus heavily on reliability, exactly-once semantics, and distributed transactions — reflecting the actual engineering challenges Amazon solves at retail scale. The most common variant asks you to design the product page and order placement flow with correct inventory management.

Key Components

Product Catalog (DynamoDB). Product metadata (title, description, price, images) is read-heavy and can tolerate eventual consistency. DynamoDB with DAX (in-memory cache) handles millions of reads per second with single-digit millisecond latency. Data is partitioned by product ID.

Inventory Service (Conditional Writes). Inventory is the hardest part. Each product-warehouse combination has a stock count. To reserve inventory, the Inventory Service uses DynamoDB conditional writes: "decrement count where count greater than 0." If the condition fails, the item is out of stock. This is optimistic concurrency — no locks, no two-phase commit.

Order Service (SQS FIFO). Once inventory is reserved, the Order Service writes the order record and publishes to an SQS FIFO queue. FIFO queues guarantee exactly-once delivery within a message group (order ID), preventing duplicate order processing. The order also has an idempotency key derived from the client request ID.

Payment Service (Stripe + Idempotency). The Payment Service calls Stripe's API with an idempotency key equal to the order ID. If the network times out and we retry, Stripe returns the original charge result rather than charging twice. The Payment Service updates the order state to PAID and publishes to the fulfillment queue.

Fulfillment Worker. Consumes from the fulfillment SQS queue. Routes the order to the nearest warehouse with sufficient stock. Handles backorder logic if stock ran out between reservation and fulfillment.

The Last-Unit Race Condition

The most important failure mode to discuss is the last-unit race condition: two users simultaneously place orders for the last item in stock. With optimistic concurrency on DynamoDB, only one conditional write succeeds. The other receives a ConditionalCheckFailedException and must be returned a "sorry, out of stock" response. You must explain this explicitly — interviewers at Amazon listen for it.

Apple

Design APNS: Persistent Connections at Scale

The Apple Push Notification Service design question is unique to Apple interviews. It tests knowledge of persistent connection management, certificate-based authentication, and store-and-forward messaging for offline devices. Apple runs APNS at the scale of billions of registered device tokens.

Key Components

Device Token Store (Cassandra). Every Apple device generates a unique device token when it registers for push notifications. These tokens are stored in a Cassandra cluster partitioned by app bundle ID and device token. Cassandra is well-suited here: write-heavy workload (tokens refresh periodically), geographically distributed, and tolerant of eventual consistency since a delayed token update merely delays a notification briefly.

Persistent TCP Connection Manager. iOS devices maintain a persistent TCP connection to APNS servers. The Connection Manager is a specialized service that handles millions of concurrent long-lived connections using event-driven I/O (similar to nginx's event loop model). Each connection is authenticated with the device's TLS certificate during handshake.

Notification Fanout Service (Consistent Hashing). When an app server sends a push notification, the Fanout Service routes it to the Connection Manager node that holds the target device's persistent connection. Consistent hashing ensures that if a Connection Manager node goes down, only the connections on that node need to reconnect — the other nodes are unaffected.

Delivery Receipt Store. APNS provides delivery feedback to app developers. The Delivery Receipt Store records whether each notification was delivered, when, and whether the device token became invalid (device was wiped or app uninstalled).

Certificate Auth. App servers authenticate to APNS using either a certificate-based (P12) or token-based (JWT with ES256) auth method. The auth layer validates credentials before accepting any notification for delivery.

Offline Device Store-and-Forward

When a device is offline (airplane mode, dead battery, out of coverage), notifications cannot be delivered immediately. APNS stores the most recent notification per app per device in the offline queue — when the device reconnects, the stored notification is delivered. If multiple notifications arrived while offline, only the most recent is delivered (APNS does not buffer all missed notifications). Most common mistake: not explaining this store-and-forward behavior for offline devices, or incorrectly assuming APNS buffers all missed messages.

Netflix

Design Video Streaming: CDN Pre-Positioning and ABR

Netflix's system design question focuses on video delivery infrastructure. The core insight Netflix engineers want to see is that you understand why a naive CDN deployment fails and what Netflix's Open Connect architecture solves. The question also tests understanding of adaptive bitrate streaming.

Key Components

Open Connect CDN (ISP Co-location). Netflix does not use a third-party CDN for its main traffic. It operates its own CDN by placing Open Connect Appliances (OCAs) directly inside ISP networks. This means video bytes travel only the last mile from ISP to subscriber — they never traverse long-haul internet links. Netflix pre-positions (proactively caches) popular titles at each OCA based on viewing patterns in that region. This is distinct from reactive caching: Netflix pushes content before anyone requests it, during off-peak hours.

Encoding Pipeline (20+ Quality Levels). Every video on Netflix is encoded into over 20 quality levels — different combinations of resolution (240p through 4K) and codec (H.264, H.265/HEVC, AV1). AV1 offers 30-40% better compression than H.264 at the same perceptual quality, which reduces CDN bandwidth costs significantly. The encoding pipeline runs on AWS EC2 with GPU acceleration.

ABR Algorithm (MPEG-DASH, Bandwidth Measurement Every 2s). The client player implements Adaptive Bitrate (ABR) streaming using MPEG-DASH. The player downloads the video in small 2-4 second segments. After each segment download, it measures the achieved throughput. If throughput is high, it requests the next segment at a higher quality level. If throughput drops, it steps down. This measurement happens every 2 seconds, allowing smooth adaptation to network conditions.

Manifest Service. When a client starts playback, it requests a manifest from the Manifest Service. The manifest is an XML/JSON document listing all available quality levels and the URLs for each video segment at each quality. The client uses this to construct download requests to the nearest OCA.

Playback API (Anycast Routing). The Playback API handles session start, license validation (DRM), and OCA selection. It uses Anycast routing to direct the client to the nearest API server globally with sub-100ms latency. The API returns the best OCA IP address based on the client's ISP and current OCA load.

The Critical Mistake to Avoid

The most common failure on the Netflix question is proposing "just use a CDN" without explaining adaptive bitrate streaming. A CDN without ABR means the client downloads at a fixed quality level — if the connection degrades, playback stops and buffers. ABR is what makes Netflix resilient to variable network conditions. Interviewers listen for this distinction explicitly.

Microsoft · Uber · Twitter/X · Airbnb · Stripe · LinkedIn

Six More Companies: Key Design Decisions

Microsoft: Design Teams (Presence and File Sync)

Presence Service. Teams presence (Available, Busy, Away, Do Not Disturb) is maintained via WebSocket heartbeats. Each client sends a heartbeat every 30 seconds. The presence server sets a Redis key with TTL=45 seconds on each heartbeat. If the TTL expires without a new heartbeat, the user is marked offline. This TTL approach avoids the need for explicit disconnect events, which are unreliable on mobile networks.

File Sync (CRDT). Teams uses CRDTs for co-authoring in Word and PowerPoint files synced via OneDrive. Unlike OT, CRDTs do not require a central server to order operations — they converge automatically when replicas merge. This enables offline editing and sync when connectivity is restored.

Message Store (Cosmos DB). Chat messages are stored in Azure Cosmos DB with a partition key of channel ID and a sort key of timestamp. Cosmos DB's multi-region write capability enables low-latency writes for Teams' global enterprise customer base.

Uber: Ride Matching and Surge Pricing

Geospatial Indexing. Uber uses Redis Geo commands (GEOADD, GEORADIUS) to store driver locations. Geohash level 6 cells (approximately 1.2km x 0.6km) are used for aggregating supply and demand. When a rider requests a trip, the system searches the geohash cell of the pickup location and adjacent cells for available drivers.

Driver Matching (Geohash Level 8). For actual driver assignment, the system uses geohash level 8 cells (approximately 38m x 19m) for precise proximity ranking. ETA is calculated using a pre-computed routing graph — not real-time shortest path — to achieve sub-100ms response times.

Surge Pricing. Surge multiplier is calculated as demand/supply ratio per geohash level 6 cell. If the ratio exceeds a threshold (e.g., 1.5x), the cell enters surge. The surge engine runs as a streaming job consuming location update events from Kafka.

Trip State Machine. Each trip is modeled as an explicit state machine: REQUESTED -> DRIVER_ASSIGNED -> DRIVER_ARRIVED -> IN_PROGRESS -> COMPLETED (or CANCELLED at any state). State transitions are persisted to a relational database (MySQL at Uber) with strict idempotency — retrying a state transition has no effect if already in that state.

Twitter/X: Hybrid Timeline and Search

Hybrid Timeline. Twitter uses the same hybrid push-pull model described for Meta: for users with fewer than approximately 10,000 followers, tweets are fanned out on write to Redis timeline sorted sets. For accounts above that threshold (celebrities, public figures), tweets are not fanned out — they are pulled and merged into followers' timelines at read time.

Search (Elasticsearch via Kafka). Tweets are indexed in Elasticsearch for full-text search. Indexing happens asynchronously: a Kafka consumer reads from the tweet creation topic and writes to Elasticsearch. This decouples search indexing from the write hot path. Elasticsearch handles both keyword search and trending topic aggregation via term frequency queries.

Airbnb: Search and Double-Booking Prevention

Search (Elasticsearch). Airbnb's search combines geographic proximity and text matching. Elasticsearch handles both: geographic queries use geo_distance filters on latitude/longitude, and text matching handles title, amenity, and neighborhood search. The availability filter is implemented as a dense bitfield: one bit per day for the next 365 days indicates whether a listing is available on that date.

Double-Booking Prevention. Two guests should never be able to book the same listing for overlapping dates. Airbnb uses a two-layer lock: Redis SETNX at the application layer (fast, distributed lock for the booking flow) and SELECT FOR UPDATE at the database layer (Postgres row lock on the availability record). The database lock is the authoritative lock; Redis SETNX reduces database load by preventing most duplicate requests from reaching the DB.

Dynamic Pricing. Listing prices vary by date based on demand, seasonality, and local events. A pricing service reads demand signals (search views, booking velocity) and publishes price recommendations to hosts. Dynamic pricing data is cached per listing per date in Redis with a 1-hour TTL.

Stripe: Payment Processing and Idempotency

Idempotency Key. Every Stripe API call that creates a charge requires an Idempotency-Key header — a client-generated UUID. Stripe stores the request and response for 24 hours. If the same idempotency key is received again (network timeout, client retry), Stripe returns the original response without processing a new charge. This is the foundation of Stripe's reliability guarantee.

Two-Phase Auth and Capture. Card payments use two phases: authorization (reserve funds on the card, no money moves) and capture (actually move the money). This separation allows merchants to authorize at order time and capture at shipment, reducing risk. The authorization hold expires after 7 days if not captured.

Webhook Retry (Exponential Backoff). Stripe sends webhook events to merchant servers. If the merchant server returns a non-2xx status, Stripe retries up to 10 times with exponential backoff (1s, 2s, 4s... up to several hours). Merchants must make their webhook handlers idempotent — the same event may arrive multiple times.

PCI-DSS Tokenization. Raw card numbers never touch merchant servers. Stripe's JavaScript element captures the card number in an iframe hosted by Stripe's PCI-DSS compliant servers and returns a single-use token to the merchant. The merchant exchanges this token for a charge via the Stripe API. This keeps merchants out of PCI scope.

LinkedIn: Graph Queries and People You May Know

Graph Database (Neptune). LinkedIn's social graph — who is connected to whom — is stored in Amazon Neptune (a managed graph database). Graph queries like "find all 2nd-degree connections of user X" are expressed as Gremlin traversals. Neptune handles the graph traversal natively, which would require expensive multi-table JOINs in a relational database.

People You May Know (Collaborative Filtering). PYMK recommendations use collaborative filtering: "users who have connection X in common with you also have connection Y — you may know Y." The model runs as a batch Spark job overnight. Precomputed recommendations are stored in Voldemort (LinkedIn's custom key-value store) for O(1) read access.

Feed (Kafka Streams). LinkedIn's professional feed uses Kafka Streams for real-time aggregation of activity events (new job, post, article, reaction). A Kafka Streams topology joins activity events with social graph edges to determine which followers should see a given update. This replaces the batch feed generation that LinkedIn used in its earlier architecture.

Patterns

Cross-Cutting Patterns That Appear in Every FAANG Interview

Across all 11 companies, six patterns appear in nearly every system design interview. These are not company-specific; they are solutions to universal distributed systems problems. Knowing them fluently — being able to name them, describe the problem they solve, and give a real example — is a prerequisite for FAANG system design interviews.

1. Consistent Hashing. Problem it solves: when you add or remove nodes from a distributed cache or service cluster, naive modulo-based hashing requires remapping all keys. Consistent hashing minimizes the number of keys that need to move when a node is added or removed — only keys on the adjacent arc of the ring are remapped. One-line example: Redis Cluster uses consistent hashing to distribute keys across shards so that adding a shard rebalances only a fraction of keys.

2. Pub-Sub (Kafka). Problem it solves: decoupling producers from consumers. When a post is created, the write service should not synchronously call the notification service, the search indexer, the analytics pipeline, and the feed fan-out service. Instead, it publishes one event to Kafka and each consumer processes it independently. One-line example: Kafka is used by Twitter, Uber, LinkedIn, and Airbnb to decouple write events from downstream processing.

3. CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation). Problem it solves: read and write workloads have fundamentally different requirements. Writes need consistency and durability; reads need low latency and high throughput. CQRS separates the write model (normalized, consistent) from the read model (denormalized, cached). One-line example: Twitter writes tweets to a MySQL primary but serves timelines from Redis sorted sets — a CQRS separation.

4. Circuit Breaker. Problem it solves: when a downstream service is slow or failing, all upstream callers pile up waiting for responses, exhausting thread pools and cascading the failure. A circuit breaker monitors error rates and, when a threshold is exceeded, short-circuits calls to the failing service — returning an error immediately rather than waiting for a timeout. One-line example: Netflix's Hystrix library introduced circuit breakers to protect microservices from cascading failures.

5. Idempotency Keys. Problem it solves: networks fail. Clients retry. Without idempotency keys, a payment may be charged twice, an email sent twice, or an order placed twice. An idempotency key is a client-generated unique ID included with every mutating request. The server stores the response keyed by this ID — if the request arrives again, the stored response is returned without re-executing. One-line example: Stripe requires an Idempotency-Key header on all charge creation requests.

6. Caching Layers. Problem it solves: databases are slow under high read load. Caching layers (Redis, Memcached, CDN, local in-process cache) serve reads from fast memory rather than disk. Key decisions: cache-aside vs. write-through, TTL strategy, cache invalidation (hardest problem in computer science). One-line example: Meta's news feed reads from Redis sorted sets first; only on a cache miss does it query the post database.

If you cannot discuss these 6 patterns fluently — naming the problem they solve, a concrete example of each, and at least one trade-off — you are not ready for a FAANG system design interview. These are table stakes, not differentiators.

Scenarios · Links, Rate Limits and Edge

URL Shortener, Rate Limiter and Throttling Scenarios

  • Design a URL shortener (TinyURL/Bit.ly)
    Core insight: The redirect read path is 100:1 vs write. Cache the full shortcode→URL mapping in Redis with a long TTL. The hard constraint is shortcode uniqueness at scale — use base62 encoding of a distributed auto-increment ID (Snowflake), not random strings (collision risk at scale).
  • Design a URL shortener with analytics
    Addition: write click events to Kafka, consume async for analytics aggregation. Never write analytics in the redirect hot path — it adds latency to the 99th percentile.
  • Design a QR code service
    Same as URL shortener. QR generation is CPU-bound — pre-generate and store, not on-demand per request at scale.
  • Design an API rate limiter
    Core insight: Token bucket (allows bursts, intuitive) vs sliding window log (accurate, memory-heavy) vs sliding window counter (approximate, Redis efficient). Production recommendation: sliding window counter in Redis with INCRBY + EXPIRE or a Sorted Set for the log. Decision: accuracy vs memory.
  • Design a distributed rate limiter (multi-region)
    Addition: each region has its own Redis. Rate limits can be exceeded slightly due to replication lag — decide whether to allow temporary over-limit (AP) or require cross-region consensus (CP). AP is usually correct for rate limiting; brief over-limit is preferable to added latency.
  • Design a DDoS mitigation layer
    Core insight: move rate limiting to the edge (CDN / load balancer), not the application. Application-level rate limiting is too slow and expensive under DDoS load.
Scenarios · Messaging and Notifications

Chat, Push Notifications and Email Scenarios

  • Design WhatsApp / Discord chat
    Core insight: WebSocket for delivery (persistent connection per client), Kafka for message fanout between servers, message DB with (conversationId, messageId) compound key. The hard problem: offline delivery — store undelivered messages, push on reconnect, track read receipts.
  • Design a group chat with 100,000 members
    Core insight: fanout-on-read for large groups. Do not write each message to 100k inboxes. Store messages in a group-level log; clients pull on open.
  • Design a real-time typing indicator
    Core insight: this is presence, not messaging. Use a separate lightweight pub/sub channel. Suppress if the user stops typing for 2s. Never persist typing events to the database.
  • Design end-to-end encryption for messages
    Core insight: server stores only ciphertext. Key exchange uses Signal Protocol (double ratchet + X3DH). The server cannot decrypt — it only relays. Key challenge: key backup when user gets a new device.
  • Design a push notification system
    Core insight: fan-out via Kafka → notification workers → APNs/FCM with rate limiting per provider. Dedup with Redis NX (24h TTL). Token hygiene: delete invalid tokens immediately on 410 from APNs. Celebrity post = fanout-on-read for notifications (same as feed).
  • Design an email delivery system
    Core insight: transactional email (OTP, receipts) = immediate, synchronous, small volume. Marketing email = bulk, async, Kafka-queued, respects unsubscribe lists and time-zone-aware delivery windows. Hard: deliverability — SPF/DKIM/DMARC, IP warming, bounce handling.
Scenarios · Social Networks and Feed

Social Networks, Timelines and Counters

  • Design Twitter's timeline / Instagram feed
    Core insight: hybrid fanout. Fanout-on-write for normal accounts (< 1M followers), fanout-on-read for celebrities (> 1M). Read path merges both. The decision point: write amplification vs read latency.
  • Design Facebook's news feed ranking
    Core insight: this is an ML ranking problem, not primarily a storage problem. The distribution mechanism is the same as timeline; the ranking model runs offline. In the interview, focus on the serving layer (pre-computed feed in a feed cache), not the ML architecture.
  • Design a "like" counter that shows 1.2M likes
    Core insight: approximate counts via Redis INCR are sufficient. Exact counts require distributed locks and are unnecessary. Edge: 1 person double-clicking the like button — client-side dedup + idempotent API (like is a toggle, not an increment).
  • Design a trending topics system
    Core insight: stream the last 60 minutes of posts into a sliding window count per hashtag. Use HyperLogLog for unique user counts (memory-efficient approximate cardinality). Trend = recent velocity, not absolute count (prevents gaming by old viral content).
Scenarios · Search and Discovery

Search, Typeahead, Recommendation and Similarity Systems

  • Design a typeahead / autocomplete service (Google)
    Core insight: a trie is the textbook answer, but a full English-vocabulary trie plus per-user history is ~20GB in memory per server. In production, precompute the top-K (typically K=10) suggestions per prefix offline from query logs (refreshed daily) and store them in Redis sorted sets (key = prefix, score = suggestion popularity) — simpler and horizontally scalable. For personalized completions, combine global popularity with per-user recency: scores are merged at query time. Latency budget: <50ms end-to-end including network; serving from Redis takes <5ms (ZRANGEBYLEX is sub-millisecond).
  • Design a full-text search engine (Elasticsearch-like)
    Core insight: an inverted index maps each term to the list of document IDs containing it (the "posting list"). Query execution merges posting lists: AND = set intersection, OR = set union. TF-IDF or BM25 ranks results by term frequency weighted by inverse document frequency — common words (the, is) score low, rare words score high. At scale, shard the index by document ID/range (not by term — that creates hot shards for common terms) and replicate for read scale; Elasticsearch hashes the document ID to pick the shard. Every query fans out to all shards in parallel and merges the top-K results. Primary interview challenge: indexing pipeline (bulk write throughput) vs query serving (sub-100ms reads).
  • Design a product search with filters (e-commerce, faceted search)
    Core insight: two data structures serve two query types — an inverted index for text matching ("red sneakers") and structured aggregations for facets ("47 results in Electronics, 23 in Sports") covering category, price range, and brand. Facet counts require computing cardinalities across the result set; Elasticsearch's aggregation pipeline does this but it is the performance bottleneck at scale. Pre-compute facet counts for common filter combinations, or cache aggregation results per filter combination with a short TTL (60s) — facet counts tolerate slight staleness.
  • Design a recommendation system
    Core insight: collaborative filtering ("users who liked X also liked Y") uses matrix factorization — decompose the user-item interaction matrix into latent feature vectors. ALS (Alternating Least Squares) scales to billions of interactions. Content-based filtering uses item attributes (genre, author, price) to recommend items similar to what the user has liked. Netflix, Spotify, and YouTube use hybrid approaches. The cold-start problem (new user or new item with no interaction history) requires a fallback: content-based recommendations for new items, popular/trending content for new users, plus onboarding questions to bootstrap preferences.
  • Design a plagiarism detection system
    Core insight: exact substring matching scales to small corpora; at billion-document scale you need probabilistic methods. Shingling: slide a k-word window over the document, hash each window — a document becomes a set of hash values. MinHash: generate multiple hash functions; the minimum hash across all shingles is the document's signature. Two documents with similar MinHash signatures have similar Jaccard similarity. LSH (Locality-Sensitive Hashing): group documents with similar signatures into buckets — only compare documents in the same bucket, reducing O(n²) to O(n log n). Turnitin and Google Scholar use variants of this approach.
  • Design a vector similarity search system
    Core insight: exact k-NN (nearest neighbor) search over N vectors in D dimensions is O(N×D) — far too slow at 100M+ vectors. Approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) algorithms trade marginal accuracy for massive speed gains. HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) builds a multi-layer navigable graph: query time is O(log N) with ~95% recall. IVF-PQ (Inverted File Index + Product Quantization) compresses vectors to reduce memory. Pinecone, Weaviate, and pgvector all use HNSW or IVF-PQ internally. Design the update path carefully: HNSW graphs are expensive to rebuild; use an append-only insert strategy with periodic compaction.
Scenarios · Storage and Databases

Files, Logs, Object Storage and Databases at Scale

  • Design a distributed file storage (Dropbox/Google Drive)
    Core insight: chunked uploads — split large files into 4MB chunks, upload each independently, store in S3/GCS with a metadata DB tracking chunk→file mapping. Dedup at chunk level using content hash (SHA-256). The hard problem: sync conflict resolution between devices.
  • Design a distributed key-value store (Redis / DynamoDB)
    Core insight: consistent hashing assigns keys to nodes; virtual nodes (vnodes) even out distribution and uneven physical capacity; a gossip protocol handles membership. For conflict resolution choose vector clocks (accurate, complex) or last-write-wins (simpler, loses concurrent writes). Quorum: with N replicas, R + W > N gives strong consistency; R=1, W=1 gives eventual consistency for maximum throughput. Hard problems: partitioning (data skew on hot keys), eviction (LRU vs LFU vs TTL), and rebalancing when nodes join or leave without taking the cluster offline — with a consistent hashing ring only the keys between the leaving node and its predecessor move.
  • Design a distributed log (Kafka)
    Core insight: append-only log partitioned by key, replicated with leader-follower, consumers read at their own pace with offset tracking. Hard problem: exactly-once delivery — requires idempotent producers AND transactional consumers.
  • Design S3 (object storage)
    Core insight: flat namespace (no real directories), immutable objects (no in-place updates), eventual consistency for GETs after PUT (now strongly consistent in real S3). Architecture: metadata service (which node holds the object?) + data nodes (actual bytes). Hard: erasure coding for storage efficiency vs replication.
  • Design a document database (like MongoDB)
    Core insight: BSON documents with collection-level indexes (B-tree on a single field, compound indexes for multi-field queries). The critical decision is the sharding key: it is irreversible after data is written. Choose a high-cardinality field with even write distribution — user_id is good for user-scoped queries; created_at is bad (all inserts go to the "hot" most-recent shard). A bad shard key creates a hot partition that cannot be fixed without a full resharding operation (hours of downtime at scale).
  • Design a time-series database (for IoT/metrics)
    Core insight: time-series data is write-heavy, time-ordered, and rarely updated. LSM-tree-based storage (used by InfluxDB, Cassandra, TimescaleDB) is optimized for sequential writes — far better than B-tree (used by PostgreSQL) for this pattern. Store data in time-partitioned chunks (e.g., one chunk per day per device). Query "last 24 hours" scans only today's and yesterday's chunks regardless of total dataset size. Aggregate old data into lower-resolution rollup tables (5-min averages, hourly averages) to control storage growth — raw 1-second samples from 3 years ago have no business value.
  • Design a graph database for social connections
    Core insight: adjacency list in a relational database works up to ~10M nodes and ~100M edges. Beyond that, graph-native storage (Neo4j, Amazon Neptune) stores relationships as first-class citizens with O(1) relationship traversal — each node directly contains pointers to its neighbor nodes, not foreign keys that require a lookup table scan. The query "find all 2nd-degree connections of user X" requires two hops: in a relational DB this is two self-joins on a 100M-row edge table — catastrophically slow. In a graph DB it is two pointer dereferences — milliseconds.
Scenarios · Payments and Financial Systems

Payments, Wallets, Fraud and Billing

  • Design a payment processing system
    Core insight: idempotency is the foundation. Every payment request carries a client-generated idempotency key; the server persists the key plus status (PENDING / SUCCESS / FAILED) before initiating the charge (e.g., Redis NX or a DB unique key). If the same key arrives again (network retry), return the stored result rather than charging again — Stripe guarantees same key + same request body = same response for 24 hours. Sequence: persist idempotency record → call payment processor → update record → return response. Never call the processor before writing the idempotency record — a crash between the two leaves an orphan charge with no local record. Use the outbox pattern for payment events and never confirm payment before writing the order record. Hard: gateway timeouts — poll for status; do not retry the charge without the idempotency key.
  • Design a wallet / balance system
    Core insight: use a ledger model — never UPDATE a balance column. INSERT one append-only debit/credit row per transaction; current balance = SUM of ledger entries. The ledger is tamper-evident (deleting a row changes the balance), auditable (every state transition is recorded), idempotent (an idempotency key prevents duplicate inserts), and makes races visible (two simultaneous credits are both recorded, no update collision). Prevent concurrent credit+debit races with row-level locking (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE) on the wallet row or optimistic concurrency with a version counter. Maintain a materialized balance for performance rather than summing the full history at query time, and validate it against the ledger on any discrepancy.
  • Design a fraud detection system
    Core insight: two tiers at different latency budgets. Tier 1: a synchronous, lightweight rules engine (<50ms) in the payment path — e.g., velocity checks such as >3 different cards attempted in 5 minutes = block. Tier 2: an ML scoring model (gradient boosted trees on 100+ features per transaction, <200ms) — synchronous if latency permits, otherwise async with a brief hold on high-risk transactions. Never block the payment path on the ML model's latency. Flag suspicious transactions for human review rather than auto-blocking — false positives are expensive. Update rules continuously from ML outputs (if the model keeps flagging rule-passing transactions, tighten the rule) and run the ML model in "shadow mode" alongside rules before promoting it.
  • Design a subscription billing system
    Core insight: idempotent charge jobs driven by a per-subscription state machine: TRIAL → ACTIVE → PAST_DUE → CANCELLED / PAUSED. Schedule by events, not cron: billing-cycle events are published to Kafka (or each subscription row stores next_billing_date and a job queries WHERE next_billing_date <= NOW() AND status = 'ACTIVE' in batches); a billing worker consumes, charges, and records. Retry failed charges with exponential backoff over 7 days before PAST_DUE — dunning with 3 retries (day 1, 3, 7) and customer notification at each step; move to CANCELLED after the final failure and send a reactivation email. Proration: store the price at time of subscription (not the current price) plus billing cycle start/end dates; credit unused time and charge the new plan at line-item granularity for mid-cycle changes.
  • Design a currency exchange rate service
    Core insight: exchange rates change continuously but display staleness of 1 minute is acceptable for most applications. Cache rates in Redis with a 60s TTL; invalidate immediately on significant change (>0.5% delta triggers a push update). Rate source: pull from a primary provider (e.g., ECB, Fixer.io) with automatic failover to a backup on timeout. The tricky sub-problem: rates for exotic currency pairs (USD→KES) may not be directly available — route through intermediary currencies (USD→EUR→KES) and multiply rates, accounting for bid-ask spread at each conversion.
Scenarios · Maps and Location

Maps, Nearby Search and Driver Tracking

  • Design Google Maps / Uber's map layer
    Core insight: geohashing tiles at multiple zoom levels. Each tile is cached independently. Route calculation uses A* or Dijkstra on a road graph (precomputed shortest paths for common origin-destination pairs). Hard: real-time traffic integration means edges (roads) have dynamic weights — pre-compute base paths, apply traffic multipliers at serve time.
  • Design "find nearby restaurants" (Yelp/Foursquare)
    Core insight: geospatial index. Options: Redis GEO (GEORADIUS query), PostGIS (ST_DWithin), H3 hex index (query adjacent hexagons at appropriate resolution). The right choice depends on update frequency (Redis for frequently-moving objects, PostGIS for static businesses) and query complexity (proximity only vs polygon-in-polygon).
  • Design a real-time driver tracking system
    Core insight: write path = high-frequency GPS updates → Redis GEO (latest position) + Kafka (event log). Read path = WebSocket push to customer map. Client-side dead-reckoning between 5s server updates. Hard: accurate ETA with real-time traffic — Kafka stream of traffic events feeds the routing engine every 30 seconds.
Scenarios · Video, Media and Collaboration

Video, Images, Podcasts and Collaborative Editing

  • Design YouTube / Netflix video upload and streaming
    Core insight: upload and playback are completely separate systems. Upload: chunked raw video → transcoding queue (FFmpeg workers) → multiple resolutions/bitrates (360p, 720p, 1080p, 4K) in object storage (S3-compatible) → CDN push. Playback: CDN caches segments close to the viewer; adaptive bitrate streaming via HLS/DASH — the client downloads a manifest listing quality levels and the player switches quality based on measured bandwidth and buffer level. Hot videos (first 24h of release) are proactively pushed to CDN edge nodes (prewarming) — pull-on-miss is too slow for viral content; cold videos (>30 days) move to cold storage tiers.
  • Design a live streaming platform (Twitch)
    Core insight: ingest (OBS → RTMP ingest server) → transcoder (FFmpeg) → HLS segment storage → CDN → viewers. Latency is 5–30s with HLS (DASH-LL can reduce it to ~1s); WebRTC gives sub-second latency for gaming, auctions, and sports betting, but Twitch uses HLS because 100K concurrent viewers matter more than sub-second latency. Chat is a separate WebSocket system, never coupled to the video pipeline. Live chat at 100K viewers: a single WebSocket server cannot hold the connections, so messages arrive at a chat relay, are published to Pub/Sub (Redis Pub/Sub or Kafka), and chat servers subscribe per channel and push to their connected viewers; on a full viewer buffer, drop chat messages rather than blocking delivery. Hard: ingest failover when the encoder disconnects mid-stream.
  • Design an image CDN with on-the-fly resizing
    Core insight: generating every size variant at upload time is wasteful (thousands of combinations), and never resize at request time for popular images. Enumerate the finite set of sizes needed (thumbnail, card, full) and resize on first request per size, caching the result at the CDN edge; for truly on-demand content with arbitrary crop params, resize at the edge node with an in-memory LRU cache. The cache key encodes the transform parameters: {original_id}?w=400&h=300&quality=80&format=webp (the model used by Cloudflare Images and Imgix). Invalidation: use content-addressable naming (include a hash of the original in the URL) so stale variants fall out of cache naturally; never use the original filename as the cache key for mutable images.
  • Design a podcast platform
    Core insight: podcast episode discovery uses RSS — a pull model where players periodically poll the feed URL for new episodes. The feed is a simple XML document; generating it on every poll for a large podcast at scale requires caching (Redis or CDN with 5-minute TTL). Store episode audio in object storage; serve via CDN with byte-range support for seek (player jumps to minute 32 without downloading the previous 32 minutes). Analytics: unique listener counting without PII uses hashed device fingerprints (IP + User-Agent hash). Completion rate tracking requires client-side SDKs sending playback events to an analytics pipeline — never server-inferred.
  • Design a real-time collaborative document editor (Google Docs)
    Core insight: two users editing simultaneously must converge to the same document regardless of network latency. Each character edit is a delta merged on the server and broadcast to collaborators over WebSocket. Two approaches: OT (Operational Transformation) transforms each operation against concurrent ones before applying it — hard to implement correctly and requires a central sequencer that orders all edits; CRDT gives each character a globally unique ID (user_id + logical clock), merge = union of character IDs sorted by ID, and deletions are "tombstoned" — decentralised and provably correct, but with merge complexity for rich text (Yjs and Automerge implement CRDTs in production). Storage: append every operation to a log; document state is the log reduced, with periodic snapshots to avoid full-log replay on open. See the Google Docs deep dive above for the full component breakdown.
Scenarios · Distributed Systems and Infrastructure

Distributed Systems Fundamentals and DevOps Platforms

  • Design a distributed ID generator (Snowflake)
    Core insight: 64-bit IDs = timestamp (41 bits) + machine ID (10 bits) + sequence (12 bits). Monotonically increasing within a machine. Hard: clock skew — if system clock goes backward, the generator must wait until current timestamp exceeds last-issued timestamp.
  • Design a leader election system (Zookeeper-like)
    Core insight: distributed lock in a consensus store (etcd, ZooKeeper). Candidate that acquires ephemeral node becomes leader. Ephemeral = auto-released on disconnect. Hard: split-brain during network partition — Raft consensus requires majority agreement before electing.
  • Design a distributed cache (Memcached/Redis Cluster)
    Core insight: consistent hashing for node assignment, virtual nodes for even distribution. Cache-aside read pattern. Hard: thundering herd on cache miss — mutex lock pattern (only one thread fetches from DB, others wait for cache population).
  • Design a circuit breaker
    Core insight: three states — closed (normal), open (reject all requests, no downstream calls), half-open (allow one probe request to test recovery). Transitions: closed→open on error rate threshold, open→half-open after timeout, half-open→closed on probe success. Hard: choosing error rate threshold and timeout — too sensitive = nuisance trips, too lenient = cascading failures.
  • Design a distributed job scheduler (Celery / Temporal / Airflow)
    Core insight: task queue (Kafka or Redis) + worker pool + result store + dead-letter queue. Model jobs as DAGs (B cannot start until A succeeds) with a per-task state machine: PENDING → QUEUED → RUNNING → SUCCESS / FAILED / SKIPPED. Delivery is at-least-once by default (retry on failure); deduplicate at the task ID level. Hard sub-problems: exactly-once execution — a worker claims a task via optimistic locking (status = RUNNING plus a claimed_by timestamp) and heartbeats; if no heartbeat arrives within 60s the scheduler re-queues it, and idempotent tasks make re-queuing safe (at-least-once + idempotent tasks = effectively exactly-once). Cron in a distributed system — which worker runs the 9 AM job? Use a leader-elected scheduler service, not per-worker cron.
  • Design a CI/CD pipeline
    Core insight: every pipeline stage must be idempotent and artifact-immutable. Build artifacts are stored in a content-addressable registry (Docker registry tagged by image SHA256, S3 with object key = artifact hash). The same commit always produces the same artifact — no "works on my build" variance. Deployment strategies: blue-green (run old version alongside new until health checks pass, then cut traffic); canary (route 1% → 5% → 20% → 100% based on error rate and latency thresholds — roll back automatically if error rate exceeds threshold). Secrets management: never embed secrets in images or pipeline YAML; inject at runtime from a secrets store (Vault, AWS Secrets Manager) with short-lived credentials.
  • Design a feature flag system
    Core insight: flag evaluation must be local (no network call per check — that adds 50ms+ latency to every render). Architecture: flag configuration is stored centrally (LaunchDarkly, Unleash, GrowthBook); the SDK caches a snapshot locally with background refresh (<100ms). User/group targeting requires consistent bucketing: hash(user_id + flag_key) mod 100 maps the user to a bucket in [0, 100). Consistent hashing ensures the same user always sees the same variant. Percentage rollout: expose to bucket [0, 10) = 10% of users, then expand. Never use random() per request — a user would see different variants on different page loads.
  • Design a distributed tracing system (like Jaeger or Zipkin)
    Core insight: trace context must be propagated across every service boundary — injected as HTTP headers (traceparent per W3C spec, or proprietary B3 headers). Each service creates a "span" (start time, end time, operation name, parent span ID); spans from one request form a tree rooted at the entry-point span. This tree = one trace. Storage: Cassandra is the standard backend (high write throughput, time-ordered, good for time-range queries). Query patterns: trace by trace ID (O(1) lookup by ID), trace by service + error (secondary index, expensive). Sampling: tracing every request at 1M req/s is cost-prohibitive — use head-based sampling (sample 0.1% at entry point) or tail-based sampling (always record traces where error occurred or P99 latency exceeded threshold).
  • Design an on-call alerting system (like PagerDuty)
    Core insight: the biggest operational failure of alerting systems is alert fatigue from lack of deduplication. A single underlying issue fires dozens of monitoring checks simultaneously — all create separate pages. Deduplication: group alerts by service + metric + time window; create one incident per group rather than one per alert. Alert lifecycle state machine: TRIGGERED → ACKNOWLEDGED → RESOLVED. Escalation policies: if primary on-call doesn't ACK within 5 minutes, page secondary; if no ACK in 10 minutes, page engineering manager. Guaranteed delivery: on-call pages must never be silently dropped — use dead-letter queuing for undeliverable pages with fallback channels (SMS if push fails, phone call if SMS fails). Store escalation policy configuration as immutable audit-logged records — accidental policy deletion must not prevent delivery.
Strategy

How to Structure Your 45-Minute Answer

One of the most consistent failure modes in system design interviews is time mismanagement. Candidates spend 35 minutes on the happy path diagram and never discuss scaling, failure modes, or trade-offs. The following time allocation is based on post-interview feedback from candidates at Google, Meta, and Amazon.

0-5 Minutes: Requirements and Clarification

Before touching the whiteboard, ask. Interviewers grade this heavily. Distinguish between functional requirements (what the system does) and non-functional requirements (how well it does it). For functional requirements, identify 3-5 core features — do not try to design everything. For non-functional requirements, always ask: What is the expected scale in terms of DAU and peak QPS? What is the acceptable read latency SLO (99th percentile)? What consistency model is required — strong, eventual, or causal? Is the system globally distributed or single-region? What is the expected data retention period?

5-10 Minutes: Capacity Estimation

Back-of-envelope math demonstrates scale thinking. Calculate peak QPS for reads and writes. Estimate storage requirements over 5 years. Estimate bandwidth requirements for media-heavy systems. Compute the read/write ratio — this often drives the architecture choice. Common anchors: 1 million DAU with 10 actions per day equals approximately 115 QPS average, with 3-5x peak = 350-575 peak QPS. At 100 bytes per action, 1 million DAU generates roughly 1GB of storage per day.

10-30 Minutes: Core Design

This is the largest block and covers the most ground. Start with the API design — define the 3-5 key endpoints with request and response schemas. Then draw the data flow from user action through to storage. Next, propose a database schema: 2-3 core tables with primary keys, foreign keys, and the 1-2 indexes that matter for your access patterns. Finally, add caching: identify which reads are hot and place Redis or CDN in front of them. Do not skip caching — interviewers notice.

30-40 Minutes: Scale and Bottlenecks

Identify the hot path — the sequence of operations executed on every single request. Ask yourself: what breaks first as load doubles? This is usually the database write path or a synchronous fan-out. Propose horizontal scaling for stateless services (add instances behind a load balancer). Propose sharding for stateful services (database, cache). Name the shard key and explain why you chose it. Discuss read replicas for read-heavy workloads.

40-45 Minutes: Trade-offs and Wrap-Up

In the last five minutes, explicitly name the trade-offs you made. "I chose eventual consistency for the timeline because strong consistency would require a distributed lock on every write, which would hurt write throughput significantly." "I chose fan-out on write for regular users because it makes reads cheap, but the cost is higher write amplification for users with many followers." Also briefly mention what you would monitor: error rate, p99 latency, cache hit rate, dead letter queue depth.

Pitfalls

Most Common Mistakes in System Design Interviews

These six mistakes appear repeatedly in post-interview debriefs. Each one is fixable with deliberate practice.

1. Jumping to Solution Without Requirements. This is the single most common failure. The candidate hears "design Twitter" and immediately draws a client-server-database diagram. But design Twitter for 100 users is completely different from design Twitter for 500 million users. Always ask at least three clarifying questions before drawing a single box. This takes discipline — the impulse to demonstrate knowledge by drawing is strong. Resist it.

2. Ignoring Non-Functional Requirements. Many candidates focus entirely on functional correctness ("the user can post a tweet and followers can see it") and never address latency, availability, consistency, or geographic distribution. Interviewers expect you to articulate: what is the latency SLO for feed reads? Is the system multi-region? Can users tolerate seeing slightly stale data? These choices drive architecture decisions more than the functional requirements do.

3. No Trade-off Discussion. Stating "I will use Cassandra for the user table" without explaining why — and what you sacrifice by not using Postgres or DynamoDB — is a red flag. Every design decision is a trade-off. You are not being evaluated on whether you chose Cassandra or Postgres; you are being evaluated on whether you understand what each choice gives up.

4. Over-Engineering for Stated Scale. If the interviewer states "design a system for 10,000 active users," proposing a multi-region active-active Cassandra cluster with Kafka-based event sourcing and a custom CDN demonstrates poor judgment. Match the complexity of your architecture to the stated scale. Over-engineering wastes the interview's limited time on unnecessary complexity.

5. Not Knowing Failure Modes. What happens when your Redis cluster goes down? What happens when the payment service returns a timeout? What happens when a Kafka consumer falls behind? Interviewers probe failure modes deliberately. If your answer to every failure is "it fails gracefully," you are not demonstrating distributed systems depth. Know your failure modes: timeouts, retries, circuit breakers, dead letter queues, and fallback paths.

6. Never Asking for Feedback. At the end of your design, ask: "Does this address the key concerns you had in mind? Is there a part you'd like me to go deeper on?" This demonstrates collaborative problem-solving rather than solo performance. Interviewers routinely reward this behavior — it is closer to how real engineering works.

Mistake number 1 — jumping to solution without requirements — accounts for more failed system design interviews than any other single factor. Practice starting every system design session with a five-minute requirements conversation, even when practicing alone.

Implementation

JavaScript: Fan-Out News Feed Write Path

The following code illustrates the hybrid push-pull fan-out pattern discussed in the Meta News Feed section. It is simplified for clarity — production systems would add error handling, circuit breakers, and distributed tracing — but it demonstrates the core logic interviewers want to see.

JavaScript Example 
const redis = require('ioredis');
const { Kafka } = require('kafkajs');

const redisClient = new redis({ host: 'localhost', port: 6379 });
const kafka = new Kafka({ brokers: ['localhost:9092'] });
const producer = kafka.producer();

// Threshold above which a user is treated as a "celebrity"
// and we skip the fan-out on write.
const CELEBRITY_FOLLOWER_THRESHOLD = 10_000;

// Maximum number of post IDs to retain per user timeline in Redis.
const TIMELINE_MAX_LENGTH = 1000;

/**
 * Called when a user publishes a new post.
 * @param {string} userId - The ID of the user creating the post.
 * @param {string} content - The text content of the post.
 * @param {object} db - Database connection for posts and followers.
 */
async function publishPost(userId, content, db) {
  // 1. Persist the post to the primary database.
  const postId = await db.posts.insert({
    authorId: userId,
    content,
    createdAt: Date.now(),
  });

  // 2. Check the author's follower count to decide fan-out strategy.
  const followerCount = await db.followers.countByUserId(userId);

  if (followerCount < CELEBRITY_FOLLOWER_THRESHOLD) {
    // Fan-out on write: push this post ID into each follower's
    // Redis timeline sorted set, scored by current epoch timestamp.
    // This is efficient for normal users but would cause write amplification
    // for accounts with millions of followers.
    const followers = await db.followers.getFollowerIds(userId);
    const score = Date.now();

    const pipeline = redisClient.pipeline();
    for (const followerId of followers) {
      const timelineKey = `timeline:${followerId}`;
      // ZADD: add postId with score = timestamp so ZREVRANGE
      // returns posts in reverse chronological order.
      pipeline.zadd(timelineKey, score, postId);
      // Trim the timeline to avoid unbounded Redis memory growth.
      pipeline.zremrangebyrank(timelineKey, 0, -(TIMELINE_MAX_LENGTH + 1));
    }
    await pipeline.exec();
  } else {
    // Celebrity / high-follower-count path: skip the fan-out.
    // Instead, publish an event to Kafka. A downstream consumer will
    // store this post in the "celebrity posts" collection.
    // At read time, followers will pull celebrity posts and merge them
    // into their Redis timeline on-the-fly.
    await producer.send({
      topic: 'celebrity-post-created',
      messages: [{ key: userId, value: JSON.stringify({ postId, userId, createdAt: Date.now() }) }],
    });
  }

  // 3. Always publish a general post-created event for analytics,
  //    notifications, and search indexing — regardless of follower count.
  await producer.send({
    topic: 'post-created',
    messages: [{ key: postId, value: JSON.stringify({ postId, userId, content }) }],
  });

  return postId;
}

/**
 * Reads the timeline for a given user.
 * Returns a merged, sorted list of post IDs from:
 *   (a) the pre-computed Redis timeline (normal users the viewer follows), and
 *   (b) recent posts from celebrities the viewer follows (pulled at read time).
 *
 * @param {string} userId - The ID of the user requesting their timeline.
 * @param {object} db - Database connection.
 * @param {number} limit - Number of posts to return.
 */
async function getTimeline(userId, db, limit = 20) {
  const timelineKey = `timeline:${userId}`;

  // Fetch recent post IDs from Redis sorted set in reverse chron order.
  // ZREVRANGE returns members with the highest score (most recent) first.
  const cachedPostIds = await redisClient.zrevrange(timelineKey, 0, limit - 1);

  let postIds = cachedPostIds;

  // If Redis has fewer posts than requested, fall back to the database
  // for older posts (handles cold start and TTL expiry cases).
  if (cachedPostIds.length < limit) {
    const dbPostIds = await db.posts.getTimelinePostIds(userId, {
      limit: limit - cachedPostIds.length,
      before: cachedPostIds[cachedPostIds.length - 1] || null,
    });
    postIds = [...cachedPostIds, ...dbPostIds];
  }

  // Pull recent posts from celebrities this user follows (hybrid pull).
  const celebrityPostIds = await db.posts.getCelebrityPostIds(userId, { limit });

  // Merge and sort all post IDs by score/timestamp, then take the top N.
  const merged = mergeAndSort([...postIds, ...celebrityPostIds], limit);
  return merged;
}

/**
 * Merges two arrays of post IDs (already sorted descending by timestamp)
 * and returns the top `limit` unique entries.
 */
function mergeAndSort(postIds, limit) {
  const unique = [...new Set(postIds)];
  // Post IDs are assumed to encode creation time (e.g., Snowflake IDs).
  // Sort descending so newest posts appear first.
  return unique.sort((a, b) => Number(BigInt(b) - BigInt(a))).slice(0, limit);
}
Checklist

Pre-Interview System Design Checklist

Run through this checklist before every mock interview. If you cannot confidently answer any item, that is a gap to practice before the real interview.

  • Always ask for scale — daily active users and peak QPS — before drawing a single box on the whiteboard
  • State non-functional requirements explicitly: latency SLO (p99), availability target (99.9% vs 99.99%), consistency model, and geographic distribution
  • Name your trade-offs explicitly — for every technology choice, state what you are giving up and why that is acceptable given the requirements
  • Address the celebrity / hot-key problem for any social platform: propose hybrid push-pull with a follower count threshold
  • Explain your database choice with at least two reasons (e.g., "DynamoDB because we need single-digit millisecond reads at any scale and the access pattern is always by primary key — we do not need complex joins")
  • Put analytics pipelines, notification dispatch, and search indexing on an async path (Kafka) — they should never block the user-facing write
  • Mention circuit breakers for every external dependency your system calls: payment processors, email providers, third-party APIs
  • Know the 6 cross-cutting patterns (consistent hashing, pub-sub/Kafka, CQRS, circuit breaker, idempotency keys, caching layers) well enough to give a one-line example and one trade-off for each
FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to know Kafka internals — partitions, replication factor, ISR?

No. Interviewers do not quiz you on Kafka's internal replication protocol. What they want to see is that you understand the problem Kafka solves (durable, ordered, replayable event stream; decoupling producers from consumers), the key properties (at-least-once delivery by default, exactly-once with idempotent producers and transactional API, log compaction for state topics), and when NOT to use Kafka (when you need sub-10ms latency and cannot tolerate broker overhead, or when your message volume is too low to justify the operational cost — use a simple SQS or even a database-based job queue instead).

How much database schema detail should I provide?

Aim for 2-3 core tables with primary key, the most important foreign key relationships, and the 1-2 indexes that serve your main access patterns. For example, a tweet table: tweet_id (PK), author_id (FK, indexed), content, created_at (indexed for time-range queries). You do not need to enumerate every column. Interviewers want to see that you understand normalization trade-offs and that you can identify which columns need indexes and why.

What should I say if I do not know the answer to a follow-up question?

Say: "I'm not certain of the exact implementation detail here, but here's how I would think about it." Then walk through your reasoning process. Interviewers at senior levels care far more about your problem-solving process than about your ability to recall specific implementation details. A candidate who says "I don't know" and stops is penalized far more than a candidate who says "I'm not sure but let me reason through it" and arrives at a plausible answer.

Should I memorize the answers in this article?

No. Memorizing answers without understanding the underlying patterns is counterproductive. Interviewers probe lateral thinking — they change one parameter (5x more users, add a geo-distribution requirement, change the consistency requirement) and see how your answer adapts. If you memorized the answer, the variant breaks you. If you understood the pattern, the variant is just another instance of the same problem. Study the patterns: fan-out, consistent hashing, idempotency, CAP trade-offs, caching strategies. The specific questions change; the patterns repeat.

Takeaways

What to Take Away

The 11 companies covered in this article — Google, Meta, Amazon, Apple, Netflix, Microsoft, Uber, Twitter/X, Airbnb, Stripe, and LinkedIn — collectively ask 15 to 20 system design problems in rotation. The specific questions change slightly year to year. The patterns underneath them almost never do.

The fan-out problem (Meta News Feed, Twitter timeline, LinkedIn feed) is the same problem across three companies. The idempotency problem (Amazon orders, Stripe payments) is the same problem in two different domains. The persistent connection problem (Apple APNS, Microsoft Teams presence) is the same problem with different constraints. Once you internalize the pattern, you can adapt it to any instance.

The evaluative framework is also consistent: Problem Scoping, Component Design, Scale Thinking, Trade-off Articulation. The largest single dimension is Component Design at 35%, but Trade-off Articulation is what distinguishes a passing answer from a standout answer. Two candidates can draw identical diagrams; the one who can clearly articulate why Redis sorted sets and not a relational database for the timeline, and what that choice costs, will score higher.

The six cross-cutting patterns (consistent hashing, Kafka pub-sub, CQRS, circuit breakers, idempotency keys, caching layers) are not optional knowledge. They appear in some form in nearly every FAANG system design interview. Make them reflexive — you should be able to apply them without searching your memory.

Finally: the 45-minute structure is a discipline. Requirements and scoping in the first five minutes. Capacity estimation in the next five. Core design in the middle twenty. Scaling and bottlenecks in the following ten. Trade-offs in the last five. Every minute spent drawing a beautiful diagram at the expense of discussing trade-offs is a minute that costs you points on the dimension interviewers weight most.

The 60+ scenarios in the catalog resolve into fewer than 15 fundamental patterns. Recognise the pattern, state the dominant constraint and the breakpoint at scale, name the core trade-off, then drive the conversation to depth on the hardest sub-problem. That is what differentiates a senior-level answer.

Study patterns. Practice trade-off articulation. Ask before you draw. These three habits, applied consistently, will take you further in system design interviews than memorizing any specific answer.
