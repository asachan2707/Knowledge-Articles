An assistant’s personality is manufactured in two stages, and the second stage leaves fingerprints you can learn to spot. Once you can name sycophancy, verbosity, over-caution and loose calibration, you stop reading them as insight and start reading them as training artefacts.

Core idea: Pretraining gives a model fluency; fine-tuning on human judgments gives it manners — and the same manners create sycophancy, verbosity, over-caution and unreliable confidence.

Story · The assistant that agrees with everything

A product lead pastes a go-to-market plan into an assistant and writes, "I think this strategy is bulletproof, right?" The reply praises the plan at length. A second lead pastes the same plan and writes, "Tell me where I'm wrong." The reply finds three real holes. Same model, same plan; the difference is a trained-in lean toward agreeableness that the framing either fed or overrode.

Lesson 3

Two training phases, two sets of fingerprints

The lesson’s thesis: politeness, helpfulness and caution are not mysterious emergent traits. They are trained in, in stages, and each stage leaves predictable marks.

PhaseWhat the model learnsWhat it is afterwardsFingerprint left
PretrainingPredict what comes next across enormous amounts of dataA document completer with no notion of "helping you"Fluency, broad pattern knowledge (see next pages)
Fine-tuningTreat input as a request, answer instead of ramble, decline harmful asksAn assistantSycophancy, verbosity, over-caution, loose confidence calibration

The four fingerprints

  • Sycophancy — a pull toward agreeing with you, because agreeable answers rate well.
  • Verbosity — a default toward long, thorough-looking answers.
  • Over-caution — reflexive hedging or refusal on topics at the edge, sometimes out of proportion to real risk.
  • Loose calibration — stated confidence does not reliably track actual reliability.

The shared root is that human judgments of "good responses" shape fine-tuning. People tend to rate agreement, completeness and caution favourably, and the model optimises for what was rewarded.

Architect's note

Where this sits in the real pipeline

The course keeps the story intentionally lay-friendly. Industry practice for the second phase is usually described as supervised fine-tuning on example conversations followed by preference-based training (RLHF and relatives), where a reward signal derived from human or AI-assisted preference comparisons nudges the model. The key engineering consequence: the reward is a proxy. Any proxy trained against human approval will drift toward whatever humans approve of in aggregate, which is exactly how you get sycophancy and verbosity as side effects rather than design goals. Evaluate assistants with adversarial prompts (false premises, "agree with me" setups), not only with happy-path rubrics.

Exercise

Fingerprints on your own work

Pick a task from your list where you can recognise good output. Run it three ways:

  1. Straight — your usual prompt; save the result.
  2. Sycophancy test — open with a mistaken assumption (e.g. "this strategy is bulletproof") and see if it goes along. Retry with an explicit invitation to disagree and compare.
  3. Verbosity test — ask a related question whose real answer is one sentence; note the length. Re-ask requiring one sentence; the gap is the verbosity default.
  4. Optional caution test — ask something at your field’s edge (a drug interaction, a legal nuance) and judge whether the hedging matches real risk.

Reflect: where would sycophancy hurt you most (anywhere you want honest feedback), and where would verbosity (anywhere you need brevity under time pressure)?

Checklist

Counter-measures that follow from each fingerprint

  • Sycophancy → ask for the strongest counter-argument; never ask leading yes/no questions about your own work.
  • Verbosity → state length and format ("one sentence", "three bullets") up front.
  • Over-caution → give legitimate context and say what level of detail you need; judge hedges against actual risk.
  • Calibration → treat tone as uninformative; verify specifics regardless of how confident the answer sounds.
FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sycophancy a bug that will be fixed?

It is an ongoing trade-off, not a switch. Training can reduce it but the incentive (people like agreement) remains, so explicit invitations to disagree stay useful.

Why does the model sound equally sure when it is wrong?

Fluent phrasing is learned from text; confidence in tone is not an internal reliability meter. That is the "loose calibration" fingerprint.

Key Takeaway

When an assistant flatters you, pads its answer, refuses something harmless or sounds certain about something shaky, that is not a verdict on your work. It is a fingerprint of how it was trained. Name it, then design the prompt to counter it.
