Most AI mistakes are not random. They cluster around four properties of generative models, and each one is a continuum with a capability end and a limitation end produced by the same mechanism. Learn the properties once and you can predict where an unfamiliar task will land.

Core idea: AI is neither uniformly capable nor uniformly unreliable; locate your task on each of four continuums and you know how much to trust, verify and supply.

Story · Same tool, two very different afternoons

Two colleagues use the same assistant on the same day. One pastes a client email and gets a flawless rewrite in seconds. The other asks for "three supporting studies" and ships a deck citing a paper that does not exist. Neither is careless, and neither is unlucky. The first task lived where the model is strong; the second sat exactly where its mechanism produces plausible fiction. The course exists to make that difference predictable instead of surprising.

Lesson 1

What the course is, and how it pairs with the 4D Framework

Claude Academy's AI Capabilities and Limitations course has 13 items: lessons, hands-on "Try It Out" activities, a quiz and a completion badge. It is the machine-side companion to the AI Fluency (4D) Framework: the 4Ds describe what you do (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence), while this course describes how AI behaves, which is what those competencies have to respond to.

The durability argument is the important design choice. Specific model limits move every release, but the properties that cause them stay the same. Learn the shape, not the current numbers.

The five-stage roadmap

  1. What we mean by AI — generative AI versus other AI.
  2. How AI is trained — how pretraining and fine-tuning shape character.
  3. Properties of AI — next token prediction, knowledge, working memory, steerability.
  4. Putting it together — what happens when properties collide.
  5. Next steps — applying it safely, effectively and ethically.

Lesson 2

What we mean by AI: generative versus classification

Most AI you touch daily is not generative: spam filters, recommendations and fraud scoring sort, rank or classify existing things. The course is about transformer-based text models that create new content one token at a time. The distinction matters because the failure modes differ: a classifier is wrong by picking the wrong bucket; a generator is wrong by producing fluent text that was never anchored to anything.

Classification / prediction AIGenerative AI
OutputA label, score or ranking over existing itemsNew content (text, code, images)
Typical failureWrong bucket; measurable error rateFluent but ungrounded output; hard to see
ExamplesSpam filter, fraud detection, recommendationsChat assistants, code assistants, summarizers

Four properties, each a continuum

The course's central claim: AI is neither uniformly capable nor uniformly unreliable. Its behaviour follows four axes, and for each one the same mechanism produces both the capability and the limitation.

PropertyQuestion it answersCapability zone → limitation zone
Next Token PredictionWhere do answers come from?Familiar patterns → novel, specific, checkable claims
KnowledgeWhat does it know?Frequent, in-window, consistent → rare, post-cutoff, niche, contested
Working MemoryWhat is it attending to right now?Fits in context → very long, cross-session, buried details
SteerabilityHow much am I in control?Short, concrete, checkable → long reasoning, abstract, precision tasks

Calibrated trust means locating your task on each continuum, instead of trusting or distrusting AI wholesale.

Exercise: generative or not?

List five AI features you used this week, decide whether each generates or classifies, and have an AI check your calls. Then re-open your Lesson 1 list and tag each task with the property question that looks most relevant (answers, knowledge, attention, control). The tags are predictions you test over the next four lessons.

Architect's note

Why a property model beats a failure list

Failure lists rot: every model release invalidates half of them. A property model compresses the same information into four causal levers you can reason about when something new breaks. This is the same move good SREs make with the four golden signals: you do not memorise every outage, you learn which signal to check first. Treat the four properties as your first-response checklist for any odd output.

Checklist

Practical checklist

  • Write down 4–6 concrete tasks you use AI for, with a one-line "worked / needed rework" note.
  • Tag each task: generative or classification? Which of the four property questions matters most?
  • Keep the list; every later page in this track annotates it.
  • Stop saying "AI is good/bad at X" — say where on the continuum your version of X sits.
FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this course about a particular Claude version?

No. It is deliberately version-agnostic: boundaries shift as models improve, but the four properties persist.

Do I need the 4D Framework course first?

No, but the course points to AI Fluency Framework & Foundations as the natural companion; this track covers the machine side only.

Why exclude spam filters and recommenders?

Their behaviour is governed by different mechanics (scoring and ranking). The properties here are specific to generative, token-by-token models.

Key Takeaway

Don’t memorise what today’s model can’t do. Memorise the four questions: where do the answers come from, what does it know, what is it attending to, and how much am I in control. Boundaries will move; the questions won’t.
