Payment Deducted But Order Failed: System Design Deep Dive on the Saga Pattern and Distributed Transactions

Why ACID transactions cannot span independent microservices, why Two-Phase Commit trades availability for consistency at exactly the wrong moment, and how the Saga pattern — with choreography, orchestration, compensating transactions, the Transactional Outbox, and idempotency keys — gives you the distributed atomicity guarantee your users expect.

Domain: System Design · Distributed Transactions · Backend Architecture Published: 2026-07-29

"Payment successful." "Order failed." Those two messages appearing together are not a rare edge case. They are the default outcome when a multi-step distributed transaction is not explicitly designed against partial failure. Distributed systems do not give you ACID across service boundaries for free. You have to build it yourself — and the mechanism is the Saga pattern.

Story · Flipkart Big Billion Days — 50,000 users, money gone, no laptop

11:57 PM on Big Billion Days. Aarav adds a ₹4,999 laptop to his cart, enters his UPI PIN, and watches the screen. Payment gateway responds in 800 ms: SUCCESS. The checkout service calls the inventory service to reserve the unit. The inventory service is under extreme load — 200,000 requests/second — and the response takes 12 seconds before timing out. The checkout service marks the order FAILED. Aarav's bank statement shows a debit. Flipkart's order history shows nothing.

The automatic refund process runs on a T+3 batch job. Aarav will not see his ₹4,999 for three business days. He opens a support ticket. He leaves a one-star review. He tells his family.

This scenario hit approximately 50,000 users during a single Big Billion Days event — not because the engineers were negligent, but because no one explicitly designed the rollback path. The payment service and the inventory service each worked correctly in isolation. The bug was in the gap between them.

Why ACID Breaks Across Services

ACID within a single database

Within a single relational database, ACID transactions are cheap and reliable. BEGIN; UPDATE inventory ...; INSERT INTO orders ...; COMMIT; — either both writes succeed or neither does. The database engine handles the rollback. Your application code never sees a half-written state.

The cross-service problem

In a microservices architecture, the Payment Service owns its database and the Inventory Service owns its database. They are independent processes connected only by a network. There is no shared transaction coordinator — no single COMMIT that spans both databases.

When you charge the card (Payment DB write succeeds) and then call the inventory service (which times out or crashes), you have an inconsistent distributed state: money is reserved in Payment DB, no reservation in Inventory DB, no order in Order DB. Nothing will automatically undo the payment.

Two-Phase Commit (2PC) — and why it fails at scale

2PC is the classical solution to distributed atomicity. Phase 1: the coordinator asks every participant "are you prepared to commit?" Phase 2: if all say yes, the coordinator sends COMMIT to all; if any say no (or time out), it sends ROLLBACK to all.

2PC fails at microservices scale for three compounding reasons:

  • Blocking locks — all participants hold their locks from Phase 1 until they receive the Phase 2 decision. A slow participant blocks every other transaction that touches the same rows. Under high load this cascades into lock storms.
  • Coordinator is a single point of failure — if the coordinator crashes between Phase 1 and Phase 2, participants are left in a "prepared" limbo, locks held, indefinitely waiting. Human intervention is required.
  • CAP theorem violation — 2PC requires all participants to be reachable. Under a network partition — the exact failure mode you must handle in production — 2PC is unavailable. It chooses consistency over availability, which is the wrong trade-off for an e-commerce checkout that must process orders even when one downstream service is degraded.

The fundamental truth: In a network partition, you must choose between Consistency and Availability (CAP theorem). For user-facing transactions, availability usually wins — you want to take the order and reconcile asynchronously, not block the user until every downstream service confirms atomically.

The Saga Pattern

A Saga is a sequence of local transactions. Each step commits to its own database and publishes an event (or receives a command). If step N fails, the saga executes compensating transactions for steps 1 through N-1 to undo the already-committed work.

The key property: no global lock is held across steps. Each step commits immediately. The saga accepts temporal inconsistency — for a brief period, money may be charged before the order is confirmed. The compensating transactions restore consistency when a failure is detected.

What Saga trades away

Saga trades atomicity for availability. It accepts eventual consistency: a user might see their card charged before they see their order confirmed (or before they see a refund initiated). The window of inconsistency is typically seconds to minutes — short enough to be invisible to humans in the normal case, and always resolved to a consistent final state.

Saga as a state machine

Every saga has exactly two terminal states: Completed (all forward steps succeeded) and Compensated (a failure occurred and all compensations ran successfully). A saga is never "abandoned" — it always runs to one of these two terminals. 

PENDING
  → step 1 succeeds → STEP_1_DONE
  → step 2 succeeds → STEP_2_DONE
  → step 3 fails   → COMPENSATING
  → comp 2 runs    → STEP_2_COMPENSATED
  → comp 1 runs    → STEP_1_COMPENSATED
  → all done       → COMPENSATED (terminal)

Choreography vs Orchestration

There are two implementation styles for Sagas. They differ in where the coordination logic lives.

Choreography — event-driven, decentralised

Each service knows what event to listen for and what event to emit after it completes its step. There is no central coordinator. The saga emerges from the chain of events.

JavaScript — Choreography event chain 
// OrderService: starts the saga
async function placeOrder(cart, userId) {
  const order = await db('orders').insert({ userId, status: 'PENDING', items: cart.items });
  await kafka.produce('ORDER_PLACED', { orderId: order.id, amount: cart.total, userId });
  return order;
}

// PaymentService: reacts to ORDER_PLACED
kafka.subscribe('ORDER_PLACED', async ({ orderId, amount, userId }) => {
  try {
    const charge = await paymentGateway.charge({ userId, amount, idempotencyKey: orderId });
    await db('payments').insert({ orderId, chargeId: charge.id, status: 'CHARGED' });
    await kafka.produce('PAYMENT_COMPLETED', { orderId, chargeId: charge.id });
  } catch (err) {
    await kafka.produce('PAYMENT_FAILED', { orderId, reason: err.message });
  }
});

// InventoryService: reacts to PAYMENT_COMPLETED
kafka.subscribe('PAYMENT_COMPLETED', async ({ orderId }) => {
  const order = await db('orders').findById(orderId);
  try {
    await db('inventory').reserveItems(order.items, orderId);
    await kafka.produce('INVENTORY_RESERVED', { orderId });
  } catch (err) {
    await kafka.produce('INVENTORY_FAILED', { orderId, reason: err.message });
  }
});

// PaymentService: compensates if inventory fails
kafka.subscribe('INVENTORY_FAILED', async ({ orderId }) => {
  const payment = await db('payments').findByOrderId(orderId);
  await paymentGateway.refund({ chargeId: payment.chargeId, idempotencyKey: `refund-${orderId}` });
  await db('payments').update(orderId, { status: 'REFUNDED' });
  await kafka.produce('PAYMENT_REFUNDED', { orderId });
});

Orchestration — command-driven, centralised

A dedicated SagaOrchestrator service drives the saga. It sends explicit commands to participants and waits for reply events. It holds the saga's state and decides what to do next at each step.

JavaScript — Saga Orchestrator (simplified) 
// SagaOrchestrator — owns the saga state machine
class CheckoutSagaOrchestrator {
  async start(sagaId, payload) {
    await this.saveSagaState(sagaId, 'CHARGING_PAYMENT');
    await kafka.produce('CMD_CHARGE_PAYMENT', { sagaId, ...payload });
  }

  async onPaymentCharged(sagaId, chargeId) {
    await this.saveSagaState(sagaId, 'RESERVING_INVENTORY');
    await kafka.produce('CMD_RESERVE_INVENTORY', { sagaId, chargeId });
  }

  async onInventoryReserved(sagaId) {
    await this.saveSagaState(sagaId, 'CREATING_ORDER');
    await kafka.produce('CMD_CREATE_ORDER', { sagaId });
  }

  async onOrderCreated(sagaId) {
    await this.saveSagaState(sagaId, 'COMPLETED');
    // saga done — notify user
  }

  // Compensation path
  async onInventoryFailed(sagaId) {
    await this.saveSagaState(sagaId, 'COMPENSATING_PAYMENT');
    const state = await this.loadSagaState(sagaId);
    await kafka.produce('CMD_REFUND_PAYMENT', { sagaId, chargeId: state.chargeId });
  }

  async onPaymentRefunded(sagaId) {
    await this.saveSagaState(sagaId, 'COMPENSATED');
    // saga ended with rollback — notify user
  }
}

// Participants only respond to commands — no business logic about the saga flow
kafka.subscribe('CMD_CHARGE_PAYMENT', async ({ sagaId, amount, userId }) => {
  try {
    const charge = await paymentGateway.charge({ userId, amount, idempotencyKey: sagaId });
    await kafka.produce('EVT_PAYMENT_CHARGED', { sagaId, chargeId: charge.id });
  } catch (err) {
    await kafka.produce('EVT_PAYMENT_FAILED', { sagaId, reason: err.message });
  }
});

Choreography vs Orchestration — comparison

Dimension Choreography Orchestration
Saga state visibility Implicit — reconstructed from events Explicit — orchestrator holds it
Service coupling Low — services know only their events Moderate — orchestrator knows all participants
Failure handling Hard — compensation logic spread across services Easy — compensation logic in one place
Operational observability Difficult — need event correlation Easy — query the orchestrator's state table
Adding a step Update emitters and listeners in multiple services Update orchestrator + add participant handlers
Best fit Simple, short sagas (2–3 steps) Complex, long sagas (4+ steps, many failure paths)

Compensating Transactions

A compensating transaction is a business-level semantic undo. It is not a database rollback — the original transaction already committed. A compensation is a new forward transaction that reverses the business effect.

For the full checkout saga, here is the forward-backward table:

Step Forward Transaction Compensating Transaction
Payment Charge card / debit UPI Issue full refund to original payment method
Inventory Reserve stock units for order Release reservation, return units to available pool
Order Create order record (status: CONFIRMED) Cancel order record (status: CANCELLED)
Notification Send "Order Confirmed" email + push Send "Order Cancelled, refund initiated" email
Shipping Schedule pickup with logistics provider Cancel pickup request with logistics provider

Compensations must be idempotent

The compensation step may be called multiple times — the orchestrator may retry due to a network timeout, the message broker may re-deliver. If the refund is issued twice, the customer receives double the money back. This is a real production bug.

The rule: calling a compensation N times must have the same observable effect as calling it once. The typical implementation is a check-before-act pattern:

JavaScript — Idempotent refund compensation 
async function issueRefund({ orderId, chargeId }) {
  // Check if we already processed this compensation
  const existing = await db('refunds').where({ orderId }).first();
  if (existing) {
    console.log(`[refund] Already refunded orderId=${orderId}, skipping`);
    return existing; // idempotent: return the same result
  }

  // Issue the actual refund via payment gateway
  const refund = await paymentGateway.refund({
    chargeId,
    idempotencyKey: `refund-${orderId}` // gateway-side idempotency too
  });

  // Record the refund (prevents duplicate processing on retry)
  await db('refunds').insert({
    orderId,
    refundId: refund.id,
    amount: refund.amount,
    createdAt: new Date()
  });

  return refund;
}

The Transactional Outbox Pattern

There is a subtle atomicity problem inside each saga step: the service must write to its database and publish an event to the message broker. These are two separate I/O operations. If the service crashes between them, the database write succeeds but the event is never published — the saga stalls silently.

The Transactional Outbox solves this by making the event publication part of the same local database transaction as the business write. Instead of publishing to Kafka directly, you write to an outbox_events table. A separate relay process polls the outbox and forwards events to the broker.

JavaScript — Transactional Outbox write + relay 
// Step 1: Business write + outbox event in ONE local transaction
async function createOrder({ id, userId, items, amount }) {
  await db.transaction(async (trx) => {
    // Business write
    await trx('orders').insert({
      id,
      userId,
      items: JSON.stringify(items),
      amount,
      status: 'PENDING',
      createdAt: new Date()
    });

    // Outbox event — same transaction, same commit
    await trx('outbox_events').insert({
      id: uuid(),
      event_type: 'ORDER_PLACED',
      aggregate_id: id,
      payload: JSON.stringify({ orderId: id, userId, items, amount }),
      published: false,
      created_at: new Date()
    });
    // If this transaction commits, BOTH the order row and the outbox row exist.
    // If it rolls back, NEITHER exists. No partial state.
  });
}

// Step 2: Relay process — separate process, runs on an interval
async function relayOutboxEvents() {
  // Fetch unpublished events in creation order
  const events = await db('outbox_events')
    .where({ published: false })
    .orderBy('created_at', 'asc')
    .limit(100);

  for (const event of events) {
    try {
      // Publish to Kafka (or any broker)
      await kafka.produce(event.event_type, event.payload, {
        key: event.aggregate_id // partition by aggregate for ordering
      });

      // Mark published only after broker confirms receipt
      await db('outbox_events')
        .where({ id: event.id })
        .update({ published: true, published_at: new Date() });
    } catch (err) {
      // Relay will retry this event on next poll cycle — do not mark published
      console.error(`[outbox-relay] Failed to publish event ${event.id}:`, err.message);
    }
  }
}

// Relay runs every second (or use Debezium CDC for zero-polling production setup)
setInterval(relayOutboxEvents, 1000);

Production enhancement: Instead of polling, use Change Data Capture (CDC) with Debezium. Debezium tails the PostgreSQL WAL and forwards new outbox_events rows to Kafka automatically — sub-second latency, no polling overhead, and the outbox table never grows unbounded.

Idempotency Keys

Network calls fail. Clients retry. Without idempotency, a retry of a payment call charges the card twice. Idempotency keys prevent this by tying a unique identifier to the operation's outcome, and returning the cached result on any duplicate call.

The key must be generated client-side — specifically, tied to the cart/checkout session, not to the request attempt. The same key must survive page refreshes, tab closures, and network retries.

JavaScript — Idempotency key storage and lookup 
// Server-side idempotency middleware
async function withIdempotency(idempotencyKey, operation) {
  // Check for cached result
  const cached = await db('idempotency_keys')
    .where({ key: idempotencyKey })
    .first();

  if (cached) {
    // Return the same result as the original call — do NOT re-execute
    console.log(`[idempotency] Cache hit for key=${idempotencyKey}`);
    return JSON.parse(cached.result);
  }

  // Execute the operation for the first time
  const result = await operation();

  // Store the result so future retries return it
  await db('idempotency_keys').insert({
    key: idempotencyKey,
    result: JSON.stringify(result),
    created_at: new Date(),
    expires_at: new Date(Date.now() + 24 * 60 * 60 * 1000) // 24-hour TTL
  });

  return result;
}

// Usage in checkout handler
app.post('/checkout', async (req, res) => {
  const idempotencyKey = req.headers['x-idempotency-key']; // UUID from client
  if (!idempotencyKey) return res.status(400).json({ error: 'x-idempotency-key required' });

  const result = await withIdempotency(idempotencyKey, async () => {
    return await checkoutService.initiateCheckout(req.body);
  });

  res.json(result);
});

// Client side — generate and persist key for the checkout session
function getOrCreateCheckoutKey(cartId) {
  const storageKey = `checkout-idempotency-${cartId}`;
  let key = sessionStorage.getItem(storageKey);
  if (!key) {
    key = crypto.randomUUID(); // standard browser API
    sessionStorage.setItem(storageKey, key);
  }
  return key; // same key survives page refreshes within the session
}

Timeout and Failure Handling

The most dangerous failure mode is not a clean error — it is a silent hang. A service call with no timeout waits forever. The caller's thread pool fills up. The caller becomes unresponsive. The entire system cascades.

Every inter-service call in a saga must have an explicit timeout. Not relying on OS defaults. Not "the network team said the SLA is 5s." An explicit timeout set in code, sized to 3× the p99 response time of the downstream service.

Classifying failures

  • Transient failures — network blip, brief database overload, GC pause. Expected to resolve if retried in a few seconds. Strategy: retry with exponential backoff + jitter (prevents synchronized retry storms). Cap retries at 3–5 attempts.
  • Permanent failures — invalid card, out-of-stock item, fraudulent order flagged. Will not resolve on retry. Strategy: immediately trigger compensation. Do not waste retries on a business error.
JavaScript — Retry with exponential backoff + jitter 
async function callWithRetry(fn, { maxRetries = 3, baseDelayMs = 200 } = {}) {
  let lastError;
  for (let attempt = 0; attempt <= maxRetries; attempt++) {
    try {
      return await fn();
    } catch (err) {
      if (isPermanentFailure(err)) {
        throw err; // Don't retry permanent errors — trigger compensation immediately
      }
      lastError = err;
      if (attempt < maxRetries) {
        // Exponential backoff with jitter: delay = base * 2^attempt + random(0, base)
        const delay = baseDelayMs * Math.pow(2, attempt) + Math.random() * baseDelayMs;
        console.warn(`[retry] attempt ${attempt + 1} failed, retrying in ${Math.round(delay)}ms`);
        await new Promise(r => setTimeout(r, delay));
      }
    }
  }
  throw lastError; // All retries exhausted — caller decides: compensate or DLQ
}

function isPermanentFailure(err) {
  // HTTP 4xx (except 429 Too Many Requests) are permanent
  return err.statusCode && err.statusCode >= 400 && err.statusCode < 500
    && err.statusCode !== 429;
}

// Usage in saga step
async function chargePayment({ sagaId, amount, userId }) {
  const timeout = 5000; // 5s explicit timeout
  const controller = new AbortController();
  const timer = setTimeout(() => controller.abort(), timeout);
  try {
    return await callWithRetry(
      () => paymentGateway.charge({ userId, amount }, { signal: controller.signal }),
      { maxRetries: 3, baseDelayMs: 300 }
    );
  } finally {
    clearTimeout(timer);
  }
}

Dead Letter Queue for Stuck Sagas

After all retries are exhausted and the failure is not classified as permanent, the saga is stuck. It cannot proceed forward (the step keeps failing) and no one has decided to compensate. Left unresolved, this is a customer with money taken and no order.

The mechanism: after N failed retries, publish the saga event to a Dead Letter Queue (DLQ). The DLQ is a special Kafka topic (or queue) that human operators monitor via an operations dashboard.

  • Each DLQ entry represents a specific customer waiting for resolution.
  • The operations team sees: sagaId, current step, failure reason, customer ID, amount, timestamp.
  • They choose: manually complete (override inventory, confirm order) or manually compensate (trigger refund).
  • The DLQ SLA should be defined in an SLA doc — e.g., "all stuck sagas resolved within 2 hours."
JavaScript — Publish to DLQ after exhausted retries 
async function processSagaStep(event) {
  const { sagaId, step, payload, retryCount = 0 } = event;
  const MAX_RETRIES = 5;

  try {
    await executeStep(step, payload);
  } catch (err) {
    if (retryCount >= MAX_RETRIES) {
      // Give up — send to DLQ for human review
      await kafka.produce('saga.dlq', JSON.stringify({
        sagaId,
        step,
        payload,
        failureReason: err.message,
        failedAt: new Date().toISOString(),
        retryCount
      }));
      await db('saga_executions').where({ sagaId }).update({
        status: 'STUCK',
        stuckAt: new Date(),
        stuckStep: step,
        stuckReason: err.message
      });
      // Alert ops team via PagerDuty / Slack webhook
      await alertOps({ sagaId, step, err });
      return;
    }

    // Requeue with incremented retry count
    const delay = Math.pow(2, retryCount) * 1000;
    await requeueWithDelay({ ...event, retryCount: retryCount + 1 }, delay);
  }
}

Saga State Persistence

Never hold saga state in process memory. If the orchestrator service restarts (deployment, crash, OOM kill), all in-flight sagas are lost. They become permanently stuck with no record of where they were — the DLQ cannot even pick them up because the DLQ entry was never written.

Saga state must be persisted durably in a database before any step is initiated. On restart, the orchestrator queries for all non-terminal sagas and resumes them.

SQL — Saga execution state table 
CREATE TABLE saga_executions (
  saga_id         UUID PRIMARY KEY,
  saga_type       VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,         -- e.g. 'CHECKOUT_SAGA'
  current_step    VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,
  status          VARCHAR(50)  NOT NULL,          -- PENDING, IN_PROGRESS, COMPLETED, COMPENSATING, COMPENSATED, STUCK
  payload         JSONB        NOT NULL,          -- full saga context (order, user, amounts)
  created_at      TIMESTAMPTZ  NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW(),
  updated_at      TIMESTAMPTZ  NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW(),
  completed_at    TIMESTAMPTZ,
  stuck_reason    TEXT
);

CREATE INDEX idx_saga_status ON saga_executions(status) WHERE status NOT IN ('COMPLETED', 'COMPENSATED');
JavaScript — Resume in-flight sagas on startup 
// On orchestrator service startup
async function resumeInFlightSagas() {
  const inFlight = await db('saga_executions')
    .whereNotIn('status', ['COMPLETED', 'COMPENSATED', 'STUCK'])
    .orderBy('created_at', 'asc');

  console.log(`[startup] Resuming ${inFlight.length} in-flight sagas`);

  for (const saga of inFlight) {
    // Re-emit the command for the current step — idempotency keys ensure no duplication
    await orchestrator.resumeFromStep(saga.saga_id, saga.current_step, saga.payload);
  }
}

Saga Implementation Checklist

Before you ship a new saga to production, verify every item on this list:

  1. All saga state persisted to a durable DB — no in-memory state that dies on restart.
  2. Every inter-service call has an explicit timeout — not OS default, not infinite.
  3. Compensating transaction defined for every forward step — every step that can succeed must have a compensation path.
  4. All compensating transactions are idempotent — calling them N times has the same effect as calling once.
  5. Transactional Outbox used for all event emissions — no dual-write to DB and broker in separate operations.
  6. Idempotency keys on all payment calls — both on your service and passed to the payment gateway.
  7. Retry logic distinguishes transient from permanent failures — permanent failures trigger immediate compensation, not retries.
  8. Exponential backoff with jitter on retries — no fixed-delay retry storms under load.
  9. Dead Letter Queue configured with operations dashboard — every stuck saga is visible to humans within minutes.
  10. DLQ SLA defined and monitored — e.g., "all DLQ entries resolved within 2 hours."
  11. Startup resume logic for in-flight sagas — service restart must not abandon live sagas.
  12. End-to-end saga test with forced mid-saga failure injection — verify the compensation path runs correctly in a staging environment before shipping.

Key Takeaway

"Payment deducted, order failed" is not a payment bug or an inventory bug. It is a distributed transaction bug — the absence of an explicit compensation path for partial failures across service boundaries. The Saga pattern does not eliminate the possibility of inconsistency; it makes inconsistency temporary and bounded by guaranteeing that every saga either reaches a COMPLETED or COMPENSATED terminal state. The Transactional Outbox ensures events are reliably emitted; idempotency keys ensure retries are safe; the DLQ ensures nothing gets silently lost. Saga is not a simple pattern — it replaces one hard problem (distributed atomicity) with a set of smaller, manageable engineering problems that can each be solved correctly.
