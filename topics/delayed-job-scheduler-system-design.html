Designing a Delayed Job Scheduler: Timers, Leases and Honest Delivery Guarantees

"Run this job at 9:00 tomorrow, let me cancel it, and keep working when machines die." Four problems hide in that sentence: timing, durability, distributed claiming and clocks. This page builds the system from a single heap to a multi-worker, database-backed scheduler, with SQL and runnable JavaScript.

Source: Medium · Emily (Amazon interview question: design a delayed job scheduler) Domain: System Design / Backend Published: Aug 2026

A delayed job scheduler looks like a toy until you list what it must survive: millions of pending jobs, a restart at any moment, ten workers that must not fire the same job twice, and clocks that disagree by hundreds of milliseconds. The naive answer, one sleeping thread per job, dies for two independent reasons. It burns a thread (and its stack memory) per pending job, and it forgets every job the moment the process restarts. Everything else in this design is a response to those two failures plus the new ones that appear once you add more machines.

Core idea: the database row is the source of truth and the lock; the in-memory heap is only a cache that tells you when to wake up. The honest contract is at-least-once execution with idempotent handlers, because exactly-once cannot be guaranteed when a worker may finish the work and die before recording it.

Story · What the interviewer is really testing (illustrative)

Picture the loop. The prompt sounds small: "design a delayed job scheduler." A weaker candidate draws a queue, a worker box and an arrow, then spends forty minutes on API shapes. A stronger candidate narrows the scope in two minutes (precision, scale, cancel, guarantees), builds the single-process version in five, and then spends the rest of the hour on what breaks when you add a second machine.

This is an illustrative framing, not a transcript, but the signals are consistent across loops like this. Interviewers tend to listen for: do you state the delivery guarantee out loud and defend it; do you reach for a lock service by reflex or notice the row is already a lock; do you treat time as an input you can control; and can you name the knee where the design stops scaling and say what you would do next. The closing line of the source article puts it well: the question is about stopping two machines from duplicating work while clocks tick and processes fail.

Step 1 · Requirements and API

Pin the contract before drawing boxes

Ask these, and write the answers on the board:

  • Precision: seconds, or milliseconds? "Within a second" is cheap. "Within 10 ms" changes the architecture.
  • Scale: pending jobs (millions? billions?) and the peak firing rate, not the average. Schedules cluster at round times.
  • Horizon: do jobs run seconds out or a year out? A year-long delay rules out in-memory-only designs and many broker delay features.
  • Cancel and reschedule: required, and for pending jobs only, or also in-flight?
  • Delivery guarantee: at-least-once, at-most-once, or "exactly-once" (which you will politely reframe).
  • Payload: small inline blobs, or a reference to an object store? Keep hot rows narrow.
API surface (HTTP-style sketch) 
POST   /jobs            { payload_ref, run_at, idempotency_key }  -> 201 { job_id }
                        (same idempotency_key => returns the existing job, no duplicate)
DELETE /jobs/{job_id}                                           -> 200 cancelled | 202 cancel_requested | 409 too_late
GET    /jobs/{job_id}                                           -> { state, attempt, run_at, ... }
(internal) claim / heartbeat / complete / fail                  -> used by workers only

The idempotency key on submission matters as much as the one on execution. Callers retry POST /jobs after timeouts, and without a unique key you schedule the welcome email twice before the scheduler has even started.

Step 2 · The single-process core

One min-heap and one timer thread

Start with the version that works on one box, because the distributed design is just this core with a durable table underneath. Keep a min-heap ordered by run time. Insert and extract-min cost O(log n); peeking at the next due job is O(1). A sorted array makes insertion O(n), and scanning an unsorted list for due jobs is O(n) on every tick, so the heap is the natural fit.

One timer thread owns the heap. Its loop is: look at the top, compute how long until it is due, and wait for that long. The key detail is wait with timeout plus notify, not sleep. If the timer is sleeping 40 minutes toward a job and someone submits a job due in 5 seconds, a sleeping thread oversleeps by 40 minutes. A condition variable lets the submit path wake the timer early so it can recompute its deadline. In Node you get the same effect by clearing and re-arming a single setTimeout whenever the heap's top changes.

The second rule is just as important: the timer thread decides what is due and hands off to a worker pool. It never executes the job. One slow handler on the timer thread delays every other job in the process, and you have rebuilt a single-threaded bottleneck with extra steps.

OperationMin-heapSorted listUnsorted list
Add jobO(log n)O(n)O(1)
Peek next dueO(1)O(1)O(n)
Pop next dueO(log n)O(1) or O(n)O(n)
Cancel by idO(n), or lazy deleteO(n)O(n)

Cancellation in a heap is awkward. The usual trick is lazy deletion: mark the job cancelled and discard it when it surfaces at the top. A hashed timing wheel (used by Netty and by the Linux kernel's timer machinery in spirit) gives O(1) insertion for huge numbers of short timers, which is why it shows up in network stacks. For a job scheduler measured in seconds to months, the heap is simpler and plenty fast.

The weakness is obvious: the heap lives in RAM, so a restart loses it. That is not a bug to patch inside the process, it is the signal that the real state belongs somewhere durable.

Step 3 · Durable schema

One table, one partial index, the database as the source of truth

Put every job in a single table. The scheduler does not need a separate "queue" and "metadata" store; state, lease and schedule are columns on the same row, so a claim is one atomic statement. Keep the payload small or store a reference to it (an object-store key) so the hot table stays compact.

SQL Example (PostgreSQL) 
CREATE TABLE jobs (
  job_id            BIGINT GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY PRIMARY KEY,
  payload_ref       TEXT        NOT NULL,                 -- pointer to the payload, not the payload
  run_at            TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL,
  state             TEXT        NOT NULL DEFAULT 'pending'
                    CHECK (state IN ('pending','running','done','cancelled','dead')),
  attempt           INT         NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,
  max_attempts      INT         NOT NULL DEFAULT 5,
  lease_owner       TEXT,
  lease_expires_at  TIMESTAMPTZ,
  cancel_requested  BOOLEAN     NOT NULL DEFAULT false,
  idempotency_key   TEXT        UNIQUE,                   -- NULLs are allowed to repeat
  created_at        TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

-- Partial index: only pending rows are indexed. Done/dead rows (the vast majority
-- over time) cost nothing in this index, so the poll query stays tiny and hot.
CREATE INDEX jobs_pending_run_at ON jobs (run_at) WHERE state = 'pending';

-- Lets the reaper find abandoned leases cheaply.
CREATE INDEX jobs_running_lease ON jobs (lease_expires_at) WHERE state = 'running';

The partial index is the quiet hero. A scheduler table is mostly history: after a month, 99% of rows are done. A full index on run_at drags all of them along, bloating memory and slowing every write. Indexing only pending rows keeps the structure proportional to outstanding work, not to total history. Archive or partition finished rows on a schedule anyway.

Why not Redis as the lock? A Redis lock adds a second failure domain: now a job can be claimed in Redis but not recorded in the database, or the lock can expire while the database says otherwise. The job already lives in a row, and a row can be locked and updated transactionally. Use the thing that already holds the truth.

Step 4 · Hybrid polling

Poll coarsely, fire precisely

Polling the database every 10 ms for millisecond precision would crush it. Polling every minute makes jobs up to a minute late. The hybrid is the standard answer: every few seconds each worker queries for jobs due within the next ~30 seconds and loads them into its local min-heap. The in-process timer then fires them with millisecond accuracy from memory. Database load is a few cheap indexed queries per worker per interval, and precision comes from RAM.

The heap is allowed to be stale or lost. If a worker crashes, the next worker's poll picks the same rows up from the table. If a job is cancelled after being loaded into a heap, the claim step (next section) is what protects you, since the claim re-checks state = 'pending' in the database. That is the property that makes the heap safe to treat as a cache.

Step 5 · Claiming with SKIP LOCKED

Many workers, disjoint batches, no lock service

With N workers polling the same table, the same due rows appear in everyone's result. Without protection, all N run them. SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED fixes this: each worker locks the rows it selects, and other workers simply skip rows that are already locked instead of waiting. The result is disjoint batches with no blocking.

SQL Example (claim a batch atomically) 
WITH due AS (
  SELECT job_id
  FROM   jobs
  WHERE  (state = 'pending' AND run_at <= now())
      OR (state = 'running' AND lease_expires_at <= now())   -- reclaim abandoned work
  ORDER  BY run_at
  LIMIT  50
  FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED
)
UPDATE jobs j
SET    state            = 'running',
       attempt          = attempt + 1,
       lease_owner      = $1,                       -- this worker's id
       lease_expires_at = now() + interval '30 seconds'
FROM   due
WHERE  j.job_id = due.job_id
RETURNING j.job_id, j.payload_ref, j.attempt;

Two notes from production. First, keep the claim transaction short: select, update, commit, then run the jobs outside the transaction. Holding row locks while executing handlers pins connections and turns SKIP LOCKED back into queueing. Second, the pattern is well known in Postgres-backed queues, and the same clause exists in MySQL 8 and Oracle, but verify behavior on your engine and version. Without it, workers either block on each other or you resort to optimistic UPDATE ... WHERE state='pending' retries that waste round trips under contention.

The knee: one hot table with a single index is a single contention point. SKIP LOCKED scales further than most people expect, since claims are cheap and non-blocking, but eventually the poll query and write rate become the limit. That is where the bucketing and sharding in the scaling section come in.

Step 6 · Leases and heartbeats

A claim is a rental, not a purchase

A worker that claims a job and then dies must not strand it forever. So a claim is a lease: the row records lease_owner and lease_expires_at, and if the lease lapses another worker may reclaim the row (that is the second branch of the WHERE clause above). Pick a lease comfortably longer than a typical run and a few missed heartbeats, but short enough that recovery after a crash is acceptable. Thirty seconds with a ten-second heartbeat is a reasonable starting point.

Long jobs extend the lease with periodic heartbeats. The gotcha that bites teams: the heartbeat update must check ownership.

SQL Example (ownership-checked heartbeat) 
UPDATE jobs
SET    lease_expires_at = now() + interval '30 seconds'
WHERE  job_id = $1
  AND  lease_owner = $2           -- still mine?
  AND  state = 'running'
  AND  lease_expires_at > now();  -- and not already expired
-- rows updated = 0  =>  I lost the lease. Stop work immediately.

Without the lease_owner predicate, a worker that stalled (long GC pause, VM freeze, network partition) wakes up, "extends" a lease that now belongs to someone else, and two workers both believe they own the job. Zero rows updated is the abort signal. The same ownership predicate belongs on complete and fail writes, so a zombie worker cannot overwrite the new owner's result.

Be honest about the limit: a paused worker may keep running side effects for a while after losing its lease, because it does not know yet. Leases bound the damage window; they do not eliminate it. That is the bridge to the next section.

Step 7 · Delivery guarantees

Exactly-once is not on the menu

Consider the sequence: the worker sends the email, then crashes before marking the row done. The lease expires and another worker runs the job again. There is no ordering of "do the side effect" and "record that I did" that is atomic across the database and the outside world. Swap the order (record first, then act) and a crash in between loses the job, which is at-most-once. So the real choice is at-least-once or at-most-once, and most business jobs want at-least-once.

Make duplicates harmless instead of pretending they cannot happen. Pass job_id (or a derived key) to every downstream call and dedupe there: a unique constraint on a processed-jobs table, an idempotency key on the payment API call, INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING for the effect. The scheduler delivers at-least-once; the handler turns that into effectively-once. State this contract in your documentation so handler authors know their half of the deal.

Step 8 · Retries

A retry is just rescheduling

When a handler throws, do not build a second mechanism. Set state back to pending, move run_at into the future, and let the normal machinery pick it up. The delay follows capped exponential backoff with jitter: delay = random(0, min(cap, base * 2^attempt)). The cap stops delays from growing to days; the jitter prevents a thousand jobs that failed together (because a dependency blipped) from retrying together and re-crashing it. "Full jitter" (uniform from zero to the ceiling) is a good default.

  • Bound retries by attempts and by age. A report that is useless after an hour should not retry for a day.
  • On exhaustion, move to a terminal dead state (dead-letter) and alert on it. A silent drop is the worst outcome.
  • Classify errors. A malformed payload will never succeed; send it straight to dead instead of burning five attempts.
  • Keep dead rows queryable with a requeue tool for humans. You will use it.
Step 9 · Scaling

Bucket the clock, spread the stampede

Poll-query bottleneck. At large scale every worker running WHERE state='pending' AND run_at <= ... over one big index becomes the load. Divide time into buckets (say, one per minute): store a bucket column (or partition by it) so workers scan only the current and next bucket. Old buckets are then dropped wholesale, which is a metadata operation (DROP PARTITION) instead of millions of deletes. You can additionally shard by job_id hash so workers own key ranges, but do that after buckets, not before.

The midnight stampede. Humans schedule at round times: midnight, the top of the hour, "9:00 sharp". A million jobs due in the same second means a thundering herd on the database, on workers and on every downstream service. Two answers depending on the business need:

  • Flexible deadlines: add jitter at scheduling time (spread "midnight" across the next few minutes). Cheap and effective.
  • Strict deadlines: rate-limit dispatch (a token bucket per tenant or per downstream), drain in order of priority, and publish a lateness metric (actual start minus run_at) so you can see the backlog and alert on it.

Per-tenant fairness belongs here too. One customer scheduling ten million jobs for the same minute should not starve everyone else. Weighted claiming, or per-tenant caps in the claim query, keeps the tail honest.

Step 10 · Clocks

Pick exactly one authority for time

If worker A's clock is 800 ms ahead of worker B's, A fires jobs early and B declares A's healthy leases expired. Distributed clocks drift, and NTP only bounds the error, it does not erase it. The cleanest fix is to never compare timestamps from different machines: let the database server's now() be the only clock, as in the SQL above. Leases, run times and expiry are all judged by one authority.

If you must trust local clocks (the in-memory timer necessarily does), document a tolerance, such as "jobs may fire up to one second early or late", and make the claim step the arbiter: a heap that wakes slightly early finds nothing claimable yet, and simply polls again. Never base a correctness decision, such as "my lease is still valid, so I may write", on a local clock alone. Use the ownership-checked update.

Step 11 · Cancellation

Clean for pending, cooperative for running

Cancelling a pending job is a single conditional update: UPDATE jobs SET state='cancelled' WHERE job_id=$1 AND state='pending'. One row changed means success; zero rows means the job already started (or finished), and you tell the caller exactly that. Because the claim re-checks state, a stale copy sitting in some worker's heap cannot resurrect it.

A job that is already running cannot be killed safely from outside, since you do not know what it has done. Set cancel_requested = true and let the handler check the flag at natural checkpoints (between batches, before each external call) and stop cleanly, ideally compensating for partial work. Say so explicitly in the API: the response is "cancel requested", not "cancelled". Be wary of hard-killing threads; you trade a clean cancel for corrupted state.

Runnable Code

A complete scheduler core in JavaScript with a fake clock

This is a self-contained model of the design: a min-heap, an in-memory table that mimics SKIP LOCKED and lease semantics, jittered backoff, cancellation, and a test suite that drives a FakeClock instead of waiting real seconds. Save it as scheduler.mjs and run node scheduler.mjs (Node 20+). It prints seven ok lines and "all tests passed". The table plays the role of the database, including being the only holder of time.

JavaScript Example 
// delayed-scheduler.mjs  (Node 20+, ES2022, zero dependencies)
import assert from 'node:assert/strict';

/* ---------- Clock: the ONLY source of time in this file ---------- */
class SystemClock { now() { return Date.now(); } }
class FakeClock {
  #t; constructor(t = 0) { this.#t = t; }
  now() { return this.#t; }
  advance(ms) { this.#t += ms; }
}

/* ---------- Binary min-heap: O(log n) push/pop, O(1) peek ---------- */
class MinHeap {
  #a = []; #lt;
  constructor(lt = (x, y) => x < y) { this.#lt = lt; }
  get size() { return this.#a.length; }
  peek() { return this.#a[0]; }
  push(v) {
    const a = this.#a; a.push(v);
    let i = a.length - 1;
    while (i > 0) {
      const p = (i - 1) >> 1;
      if (!this.#lt(a[i], a[p])) break;
      [a[i], a[p]] = [a[p], a[i]]; i = p;
    }
  }
  pop() {
    const a = this.#a; if (!a.length) return undefined;
    const top = a[0], last = a.pop();
    if (a.length) {
      a[0] = last; let i = 0;
      for (;;) {
        const l = 2 * i + 1, r = l + 1; let m = i;
        if (l < a.length && this.#lt(a[l], a[m])) m = l;
        if (r < a.length && this.#lt(a[r], a[m])) m = r;
        if (m === i) break;
        [a[i], a[m]] = [a[m], a[i]]; i = m;
      }
    }
    return top;
  }
  clear() { this.#a.length = 0; }
}

/* ---------- In-memory "jobs table" mimicking Postgres semantics ---------- */
class JobTable {
  #rows = new Map(); #byKey = new Map(); #seq = 0;
  #locked = new Set();             // rows locked by some OTHER open transaction
  constructor(clock) { this.clock = clock; }        // clock == DB now()

  insert({ payload, runAt, idempotencyKey = null, maxAttempts = 5 }) {
    if (idempotencyKey && this.#byKey.has(idempotencyKey))   // UNIQUE constraint
      return this.#rows.get(this.#byKey.get(idempotencyKey));
    const row = {
      job_id: `job-${++this.#seq}`, payload, run_at: runAt, state: 'pending',
      attempt: 0, max_attempts: maxAttempts, lease_owner: null, lease_expires_at: null,
      idempotency_key: idempotencyKey, cancel_requested: false, created_at: this.clock.now(),
    };
    this.#rows.set(row.job_id, row);
    if (idempotencyKey) this.#byKey.set(idempotencyKey, row.job_id);
    return row;
  }
  get(id) { return this.#rows.get(id); }
  lockRows(ids) { ids.forEach((i) => this.#locked.add(i)); }     // test hook
  unlockRows(ids) { ids.forEach((i) => this.#locked.delete(i)); }

  /** UPDATE ... WHERE job_id IN (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED) RETURNING * */
  claimDue({ owner, limit, leaseMs }) {
    const now = this.clock.now();
    const eligible = [...this.#rows.values()]
      .filter((r) =>
        (r.state === 'pending' && r.run_at <= now) ||
        (r.state === 'running' && r.lease_expires_at <= now))   // abandoned lease
      .filter((r) => !this.#locked.has(r.job_id))               // SKIP LOCKED
      .sort((a, b) => a.run_at - b.run_at)
      .slice(0, limit);
    for (const r of eligible) {
      r.state = 'running'; r.attempt += 1;
      r.lease_owner = owner; r.lease_expires_at = now + leaseMs;
    }
    return eligible.map((r) => ({ ...r }));
  }
  /** Earliest pending run_at (what the partial index on run_at serves). */
  nextRunAt(horizonMs) {
    const lim = this.clock.now() + horizonMs;
    const due = [...this.#rows.values()].filter((r) => r.state === 'pending' && r.run_at <= lim);
    return due.map((r) => r.run_at);
  }
  /** Heartbeat: 0 rows updated => we lost the lease => caller must abort. */
  heartbeat(id, owner, leaseMs) {
    const r = this.#rows.get(id), now = this.clock.now();
    if (!r || r.state !== 'running' || r.lease_owner !== owner || r.lease_expires_at <= now) return false;
    r.lease_expires_at = now + leaseMs; return true;
  }
  complete(id, owner) {
    const r = this.#rows.get(id);
    if (!r || r.state !== 'running' || r.lease_owner !== owner) return false;
    r.state = 'done'; r.lease_owner = null; r.lease_expires_at = null; return true;
  }
  /** Retry == reschedule. Ownership-checked just like complete(). */
  fail(id, owner, nextRunAt) {
    const r = this.#rows.get(id);
    if (!r || r.state !== 'running' || r.lease_owner !== owner) return false;
    r.lease_owner = null; r.lease_expires_at = null;
    if (r.attempt >= r.max_attempts || nextRunAt === null) r.state = 'dead';
    else { r.state = 'pending'; r.run_at = nextRunAt; }
    return true;
  }
  /** Clean only for pending rows; running rows just get a cooperative flag. */
  cancel(id) {
    const r = this.#rows.get(id);
    if (!r) return 'not_found';
    if (r.state === 'pending') { r.state = 'cancelled'; return 'cancelled'; }
    if (r.state === 'running') { r.cancel_requested = true; return 'cancel_requested'; }
    return 'too_late';
  }
}

/* ---------- Retry policy: capped exponential backoff, full jitter ---------- */
function backoffMs(attempt, { baseMs = 1000, capMs = 60_000, rng = Math.random } = {}) {
  const ceiling = Math.min(capMs, baseMs * 2 ** (attempt - 1));
  return Math.floor(rng() * ceiling);                  // uniform in [0, ceiling)
}

/* ---------- Scheduler node: heap = cache, table = truth ---------- */
class SchedulerNode {
  #heap = new MinHeap((a, b) => a < b);                // heap of run_at values
  constructor({ id, table, clock, handler, leaseMs = 30_000, batch = 10,
                horizonMs = 30_000, maxAgeMs = 3_600_000, backoff = {} }) {
    Object.assign(this, { id, table, clock, handler, leaseMs, batch, horizonMs, maxAgeMs, backoff });
  }
  /** Poll the table for jobs due inside the look-ahead window (every few seconds). */
  refill() {
    this.#heap.clear();
    for (const t of this.table.nextRunAt(this.horizonMs)) this.#heap.push(t);
  }
  /** How long a real timer thread would wait (wait-with-timeout, not sleep). */
  msUntilNext() {
    const top = this.#heap.peek();
    return top === undefined ? this.horizonMs : Math.max(0, top - this.clock.now());
  }
  /** One scheduling pass: claim due work and hand it to the "worker pool". */
  async tick() {
    this.refill();
    const claimed = this.table.claimDue({ owner: this.id, limit: this.batch, leaseMs: this.leaseMs });
    for (const job of claimed) await this.#run(job);
    return claimed.length;
  }
  async #run(job) {
    const ctx = {
      cancelled: () => this.table.get(job.job_id).cancel_requested,
      heartbeat: () => this.table.heartbeat(job.job_id, this.id, this.leaseMs),
    };
    try {
      await this.handler(job, ctx);
      this.table.complete(job.job_id, this.id);        // false => lease lost, someone else owns it
    } catch (err) {
      const tooOld = this.clock.now() - job.created_at > this.maxAgeMs;
      const next = tooOld ? null : this.clock.now() + backoffMs(job.attempt, this.backoff);
      this.table.fail(job.job_id, this.id, next);
    }
  }
}

/* ============================ self-test ============================ */
const sec = (n) => n * 1000;
const ok = (msg) => console.log('ok -', msg);

// 1. Fires on time, never early
{
  const clock = new FakeClock(0), table = new JobTable(clock), ran = [];
  const node = new SchedulerNode({ id: 'A', table, clock, handler: async (j) => ran.push(j.payload) });
  table.insert({ payload: 'email', runAt: sec(10) });
  clock.advance(sec(9)); await node.tick();
  assert.deepEqual(ran, []);
  assert.equal(node.msUntilNext(), sec(1));
  clock.advance(sec(1)); await node.tick();
  assert.deepEqual(ran, ['email']);
  ok('job fires at run_at, not before');
}

// 2. Cancellation: clean for pending, cooperative for running
{
  const clock = new FakeClock(0), table = new JobTable(clock), ran = [];
  const node = new SchedulerNode({ id: 'A', table, clock, handler: async (j) => ran.push(j.payload) });
  const j = table.insert({ payload: 'x', runAt: sec(5) });
  assert.equal(table.cancel(j.job_id), 'cancelled');
  clock.advance(sec(10)); await node.tick();
  assert.deepEqual(ran, []);
  const k = table.insert({ payload: 'y', runAt: sec(10) });
  table.claimDue({ owner: 'A', limit: 1, leaseMs: sec(30) });
  assert.equal(table.cancel(k.job_id), 'cancel_requested');
  ok('cancel: pending is clean, running is cooperative');
}

// 3. Retry = reschedule with backoff; exhaustion => dead
{
  const clock = new FakeClock(0), table = new JobTable(clock);
  let calls = 0;
  const node = new SchedulerNode({
    id: 'A', table, clock, backoff: { baseMs: 1000, capMs: 8000, rng: () => 0.999999 },
    handler: async () => { if (++calls < 3) throw new Error('boom'); },
  });
  const j = table.insert({ payload: 'p', runAt: 0 });
  await node.tick();                                   // attempt 1 fails
  assert.equal(table.get(j.job_id).state, 'pending');
  assert.ok(table.get(j.job_id).run_at > 0 && table.get(j.job_id).run_at < 1000);
  clock.advance(sec(2)); await node.tick();            // attempt 2 fails (ceiling 2s)
  clock.advance(sec(4)); await node.tick();            // attempt 3 succeeds
  assert.equal(table.get(j.job_id).state, 'done');
  assert.equal(table.get(j.job_id).attempt, 3);

  const t2 = new JobTable(clock);
  const n2 = new SchedulerNode({ id: 'B', table: t2, clock, handler: async () => { throw new Error('x'); } });
  const d = t2.insert({ payload: 'q', runAt: clock.now(), maxAttempts: 2 });
  await n2.tick(); clock.advance(sec(5)); await n2.tick();
  assert.equal(t2.get(d.job_id).state, 'dead');
  ok('retry reschedules with jittered backoff; exhausted jobs go dead');
}

// 4. Claim contention: SKIP LOCKED gives disjoint batches; each job runs once
{
  const clock = new FakeClock(0), table = new JobTable(clock);
  const ids = Array.from({ length: 6 }, (_, i) => table.insert({ payload: i, runAt: 0 }).job_id);
  table.lockRows(ids.slice(0, 3));                     // another tx holds the first three
  const b = table.claimDue({ owner: 'B', limit: 10, leaseMs: sec(30) });
  assert.deepEqual(b.map((r) => r.job_id), ids.slice(3));
  table.unlockRows(ids.slice(0, 3));
  const a = table.claimDue({ owner: 'A', limit: 10, leaseMs: sec(30) });
  assert.deepEqual(a.map((r) => r.job_id), ids.slice(0, 3));
  assert.equal(table.claimDue({ owner: 'C', limit: 10, leaseMs: sec(30) }).length, 0);
  ok('SKIP LOCKED: disjoint claims, no double dispatch');
}

// 5. Crash AFTER executing: at-least-once + idempotent handler => one effect
{
  const clock = new FakeClock(0), table = new JobTable(clock), effects = new Set();
  const handler = async (j) => { effects.add(j.job_id); };   // idempotent: Set keyed by job_id
  const nodeB = new SchedulerNode({ id: 'B', table, clock, handler, leaseMs: sec(30) });
  const j = table.insert({ payload: 'charge', runAt: 0 });
  const [claimed] = table.claimDue({ owner: 'A', limit: 1, leaseMs: sec(30) });
  await handler(claimed);                              // side effect lands... then A dies
  assert.equal(await nodeB.tick(), 0);                 // lease still valid: B must wait
  clock.advance(sec(31));
  assert.equal(await nodeB.tick(), 1);                 // lease expired: B re-runs it
  assert.equal(table.get(j.job_id).attempt, 2);
  assert.equal(effects.size, 1);
  ok('crash after execution: re-delivered, deduped by job_id');
}

// 6. Heartbeat racing expiry: the old owner must abort
{
  const clock = new FakeClock(0), table = new JobTable(clock);
  const j = table.insert({ payload: 'long', runAt: 0 });
  table.claimDue({ owner: 'A', limit: 1, leaseMs: sec(30) });
  assert.equal(table.heartbeat(j.job_id, 'A', sec(30)), true);
  clock.advance(sec(31));
  table.claimDue({ owner: 'B', limit: 1, leaseMs: sec(30) });     // B steals the expired lease
  assert.equal(table.heartbeat(j.job_id, 'A', sec(30)), false);   // A: 0 rows => abort
  assert.equal(table.complete(j.job_id, 'A'), false);
  assert.equal(table.complete(j.job_id, 'B'), true);
  ok('heartbeat/complete are ownership-checked');
}

// 7. Idempotent submission + stampede drained in batches
{
  const clock = new FakeClock(0), table = new JobTable(clock), ran = [];
  const node = new SchedulerNode({ id: 'A', table, clock, batch: 100, handler: async (j) => ran.push(j.job_id) });
  const a = table.insert({ payload: 1, runAt: 0, idempotencyKey: 'order-42' });
  const b = table.insert({ payload: 1, runAt: 0, idempotencyKey: 'order-42' });
  assert.equal(a.job_id, b.job_id);
  for (let i = 0; i < 999; i++) table.insert({ payload: i, runAt: sec(60) });
  clock.advance(sec(60));
  let passes = 0; while (await node.tick()) passes++;
  assert.equal(ran.length, 1000);
  assert.ok(passes >= 10);
  ok('duplicate submit collapses; 1000-job stampede drains in rate-limited batches');
}
console.log('all tests passed');

Notice what the tests prove. The crash-after-execution test shows the handler running twice at the scheduler level but producing one effect, because it dedupes on job_id. The heartbeat test shows the old owner getting false on both heartbeat and complete once the lease has been taken. And none of the tests sleep, because time is an injected dependency.

Testing

Time is a dependency, so inject it

Code that calls Date.now() or sleep() directly is untestable and flaky. Define a small Clock interface with now(), give production a system implementation and tests a fake you can advance by hours instantly. In the SQL-backed version the same idea applies at the boundary: pass the "current time" into queries as a parameter (or wrap now()) in tests, so you can age a lease without waiting for it.

The four tests that earn their keep:

  • Claim contention: N workers, one batch of due jobs, assert each job is claimed by exactly one. Run it against a real Postgres in CI, not just a mock, since SKIP LOCKED behavior is the thing under test.
  • Crash after execution: run the side effect, kill the worker before complete, advance past the lease, assert redelivery and that the idempotent effect happened once.
  • Heartbeat racing expiry: expire the lease, let a second worker claim, then heartbeat from the first. Assert zero rows and that the first worker aborts.
  • Stampede: schedule thousands of jobs for the same instant, assert the dispatch rate limit holds, nothing is dropped, and the lateness metric reflects the delay.
Failure modes

Production gotchas worth pre-empting

  • Long transactions in the claim path hold locks and bloat the table. Claim fast, commit, run outside.
  • Dead tuples: high-churn updates to the same rows create vacuum pressure in Postgres. Tune autovacuum for this table and archive finished rows.
  • Reaper storms: after a mass worker crash, thousands of leases expire at once and get reclaimed together. Jitter lease lengths slightly.
  • Poison jobs that crash the worker process (not just throw) never reach the failure path; the attempt counter on claim is what eventually dead-letters them. That is why attempt increments at claim time.
  • Timer drift under load: if the worker pool is saturated, jobs start late. Alert on lateness, not just on errors.
  • Clock changes: use monotonic clocks for local waits, wall clock only for stored timestamps.
Practical learning

Design checklist

  • Stated the contract: at-least-once execution, idempotent handlers, with job_id as the dedupe key.
  • Durable table with a partial index on pending rows; payloads referenced, not inlined.
  • Idempotency key on submission, enforced by a unique constraint.
  • Min-heap + one timer using wait/notify; timer never executes jobs.
  • Hybrid polling: coarse DB poll for a ~30 s window, precise firing from memory.
  • Claim via FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED in a short transaction; no external lock service.
  • Leases with heartbeats; every heartbeat, complete and fail checks ownership.
  • Retries as rescheduling: capped exponential backoff, jitter, attempt and age bounds, dead-letter state.
  • Cancellation semantics documented: clean for pending, cooperative for running.
  • Scale plan: time buckets, drop old partitions, jitter or rate-limited dispatch, lateness metric, per-tenant fairness.
  • One clock authority (DB now()) or a documented NTP tolerance.
  • Injected Clock and the four tests: contention, crash-after-execute, heartbeat race, stampede.
FAQ

Common questions

Why not just use cron or a cron-like library?

Cron answers "run this recurring task on one machine". It has no durable per-job state, no claiming across machines, no cancellation of individual future events, and no retry semantics. A delayed job scheduler deals with millions of independent one-off events, which is a different shape.

Isn't polling wasteful? Why not push notifications from the database?

Postgres LISTEN/NOTIFY can wake workers when a new job is inserted, which helps latency for immediate jobs, but delayed jobs are triggered by the passage of time, not by a write. Something must still look at the clock. Polling a narrow partial index for a 30-second window is cheap and robust, and NOTIFY can be layered on as an optimization.

Can we get exactly-once with transactions?

Only when the side effect lives in the same database transaction as the state change (for example, inserting a row into the same Postgres). For emails, payments and webhooks the outside world is not part of your transaction, so you rely on idempotency keys at the boundary.

What lease duration should I choose?

Longer than your p99 heartbeat gap by a safe multiple (three missed heartbeats is a common rule), shorter than the time you can tolerate a crashed job sitting idle. Measure p99 job duration, and have long jobs heartbeat rather than inflating the lease for everyone.

How do I handle recurring schedules?

Model each occurrence as a one-off job. When occurrence N completes, insert occurrence N+1 computed from the schedule definition (ideally in the same transaction as completing N). Do not pre-create a year of rows; and decide up front what happens to missed occurrences after downtime (skip, or catch up).

When do I outgrow this design?

Signs: the poll query dominates database CPU even with buckets, lateness grows during peaks, or a single table's write rate saturates one primary. Then shard by tenant or job hash, move the hot window to a log or in-memory tier, or adopt a managed scheduler. The contract (leases, idempotent handlers) stays the same.

Key Takeaway

A delayed job scheduler is a heap that tells you when to wake up, sitting on a database row that tells you the truth. Let the row be the lock (SKIP LOCKED plus ownership-checked leases), make time a single authority you can inject in tests, treat retries as rescheduling, and promise only at-least-once with idempotent handlers. If you can explain why a worker can finish a job and still have it run twice, and why that is fine, you understand the problem.
