Cache Stampedes, Hot Keys and Mass TTL Expiry: Mutex, XFetch, Jitter, Single-Flight and Multi-Level Caching

Cache stampedes strike in two forms: a single hot key expiring under massive concurrency, and the mass simultaneous expiry of millions of keys written with the same TTL. This guide covers the failure mechanics of both, then the full toolbox: mutex locking, background refresh, XFetch probabilistic early expiry, three TTL jitter formulas, single-flight request coalescing, L1/L2/L3 multi-level caching with invalidation cascades, a layered production defence, and a flash-sale readiness checklist.

Story · The Flash-Sale Database Crash

Flipkart Big Billion Days: How One Expiring Cache Key Took Down a Flash Sale

It is 11:58 PM. The Flipkart Big Billion Days sale is scheduled to open at midnight. The most anticipated listing — "iPhone 16 Pro — Limited Stock, Midnight Price Reveal" — has been warming the Redis cache all evening. Engineering ran a load test at 10 PM: everything passed. The Redis hit rate is 99.7%. MySQL is idle. The on-call team cracks open a laptop and watches the dashboard.

At 11:59:45 PM, the cache key for the iPhone 16 Pro product page reaches its configured TTL. The key was set to expire at exactly midnight to ensure the page would be refreshed with the new discounted price at the precise moment the sale opens. This seemed clever at the time it was written.

At 00:00:00.000, the Redis key expires. The marketing campaign fires across push notifications, SMS, and email. Fifty thousand concurrent users, who have been holding their phones with the product page still loading, all hit refresh at the same second. Every single one of those requests reaches the application server. Every single application server checks Redis. Redis returns a cache miss for every one of them. This is the moment the stampede begins.

Each of those 50,000 requests now falls through to the application's database fetch path. Every request independently constructs the same MySQL SELECT query: fetch product details, fetch inventory, fetch pricing for the iPhone 16 Pro. The MySQL connection pool has 500 connections. Within 50 milliseconds, all 500 connections are occupied. The remaining 49,500 requests begin queuing. The queue grows faster than connections are returned.

MySQL CPU spikes from 3% to 100% in under two seconds. Every one of those 500 connections is running the same query against the same product rows, holding row-level read locks, and competing for the buffer pool. Because MySQL is now saturated, the queries that were taking 40ms now take 4,000ms. The application servers begin logging connection timeout errors. Node.js request handlers hit their own 5-second timeout limit and begin returning 500s to users. Users see a spinner, then an error page. They tap refresh again. The retry storm layers on top of the initial stampede.

By 00:00:30, MySQL has crashed. The InnoDB crash recovery kicks in. The flash sale is completely unreachable. It takes 8 minutes to restore service — 8 minutes during which the limited stock is not selling, users are furious, and the social media backlash is already starting. The engineering post-mortem later estimates the revenue loss in the millions of rupees per minute of downtime.

The root cause was not a lack of caching. The cache was working perfectly. The root cause was a single architectural decision: setting the TTL to a round, predictable time aligned with a known traffic spike. Everything that followed — the stampede, the connection exhaustion, the cascading timeouts, the crash — was the inevitable consequence of that one decision.

This guide is about making sure you never make that decision again — and about what to do instead.

Story 2 · The Synchronized Expiry Disaster

How a Startup-to-Scale Migration Destroyed Its Own Database

The engineering team had done everything right — or so they thought. A mid-size e-commerce platform was preparing for its biggest flash sale of the year. Thirty days earlier, they had migrated from a single PostgreSQL instance to a horizontally scaled architecture: six application servers behind a load balancer, a Redis cluster with three primaries and three replicas, and a read-replica PostgreSQL pool for the cache-miss fallback path. The architecture review was clean. The load tests had passed. Confidence was high.

What the team had not thought through was the cache warming strategy. On the day of the sale, at 9:00 AM, a warm-up script ran. It was elegant code: it iterated through the product catalog (1.4 million SKUs), fetched each product's pricing, inventory, and metadata from PostgreSQL, and wrote each result to Redis with a TTL of exactly 3600 seconds. The script ran for 47 minutes. By 9:47 AM, the Redis cache was warm and the cache hit rate was 99.8%. The database was near-idle. The team felt good.

At 10:47 AM — exactly 3600 seconds after the first cache keys were written — the disaster began. At 10:47:00, the first batch of keys expired. Within the next 47 minutes, all 1.4 million keys expired in the same sequence and at the same rate they were written, in waves. The traffic spike from the flash sale had begun at 10:00 AM. By 10:47 AM, there were 180,000 concurrent active users on the site. Every product page view, every "add to cart" operation, every recommendation widget — each needed a cache lookup.

Within the first 90 seconds of the expiry wave, 340,000 cache misses had occurred. Each miss spawned a PostgreSQL query. The read replica pool had 800 connections. All 800 were occupied within 3 seconds. The remaining 339,200 database queries queued. PostgreSQL CPU went to 100%. Query times that were 12ms under normal load bloated to 8,000ms. Application servers hit their 5-second request timeout limits and began returning HTTP 503 to users. Users refreshed. The retry storm tripled the outstanding request count.

By 10:49 AM, the primary PostgreSQL instance — which had been untouched by the read replicas — began receiving overflow queries as the replica pool collapsed entirely. At 10:50 AM, the primary OOM-killed due to uncontrolled connection growth. At that point, the entire platform was down. All six app servers had no usable backend. Redis was running perfectly and completely empty. The site was dark for 23 minutes. The flash sale had been live for exactly 50 minutes of its planned 24 hours before it was killed by the cache layer that was supposed to protect it.

The root cause was not a bad architecture. The architecture was sound. The root cause was one line of code: const TTL = 3600; — a fixed, unjittered, round-number TTL applied uniformly to every single cache key during a single warm-up pass. That one constant transformed a gradual cache refresh into a synchronized time-bomb.

Fundamentals

What Is a Cache Stampede and Why It's Dangerous

A cache stampede — also called a thundering herd or dog-pile effect — occurs when a large number of concurrent requests all experience a cache miss at the same moment and all independently attempt to regenerate the cached value by querying the underlying data store. The problem is not any single request. Each individual request is doing exactly the right thing: checking the cache, finding nothing, falling through to the database. The problem is that thousands of requests do this simultaneously.

Consider the cost formula. If each database query takes 200ms of wall clock time and consumes 1 CPU-second of database server compute, then under steady traffic at 100 requests per second, you might have at most 20 concurrent queries running at any time (100 req/s × 0.2s = 20 concurrent). That is manageable. But a stampede of 50,000 simultaneous requests means 50,000 CPU-seconds of work arrive at the database in the same instant. The database must serve 50,000 CPU-seconds of work with, say, 8 CPU cores. It would take over 6,250 seconds — nearly two hours — to clear that backlog, if the database did not crash first.

This is why a stampede is categorically more dangerous than steady high traffic. A site serving 50,000 requests per second sustains that load continuously: work arrives and drains at roughly the same rate. A stampede delivers 50,000 requests of work in a single instant. Queuing theory tells us that a system operating at exactly its capacity limit will have infinite queue length; a stampede drives utilisation far past 100% instantly.

The failure cascade is mechanical and fast:

  1. Cache miss storm — TTL expires, all in-flight requests simultaneously miss.
  2. DB connection pool exhaustion — available connections fill in milliseconds.
  3. Query queue buildup — subsequent requests queue waiting for connections. Latency spikes from 40ms to seconds.
  4. App-tier timeouts — application servers hit their own timeout limits and start returning 5xx errors.
  5. Retry storms — clients retry on 5xx, doubling or tripling the load already hitting a saturated system.
  6. Complete outage — database OOMs or process-crashes; or app servers exhaust their thread pools; the entire tier goes dark.

The paradox of popular content: the more popular a cache key, the more dangerous its expiry. A key that is read by 10,000 concurrent users causes a 10,000x amplification when it expires. Your most important content carries your highest risk. The homepage is not safer than the product page — it is more dangerous.

Cache stampedes are also surprisingly easy to trigger accidentally. Any of these common practices can cause one: setting TTLs to round numbers aligned with peak traffic (00:00, 09:00, on the hour); flushing the entire cache after a deployment; setting the same TTL for all keys in a category so they all expire in the same second; or simply underestimating how popular a particular piece of content will become.

Fundamentals · Two Kinds of Stampede

Why Mass TTL Expiry Is Categorically Worse Than a Single Hot-Key Expiry

There is an important distinction between two failure modes that are often conflated in system design discussions. Both are called "cache stampede," but they have different root causes, different magnitudes, and require different solutions.

Single hot-key stampede is the scenario most commonly discussed in interviews: one popular key (the iPhone 16 Pro listing, the homepage hero banner) expires while 50,000 concurrent users are reading it. All 50,000 requests simultaneously miss the cache and hit the database. This is dangerous, but the blast radius is bounded: one key, one query pattern, one table, one row. A mutex lock on that one key serialises the reconstruction and limits the DB to a single query.

Mass simultaneous TTL expiry is what happened in the second story above: not one key but tens of thousands or millions of keys all becoming stale at the same moment. The effects are qualitatively different:

  • No single lock can help. A mutex works when all stampede participants are competing to reconstruct the same value. With mass expiry, each expired key has its own unique data to fetch. You would need a separate mutex for every single expired key — and a million simultaneous lock acquisitions creates its own Redis stampede.
  • Every query pattern fires simultaneously. A single hot-key stampede hits one table, one query. Mass expiry hits every query pattern across the entire application — product lookups, inventory checks, user session hydrations, config reads, recommendation fetches — all simultaneously. The database must handle an uncorrelated flood of diverse queries, not a duplicated flood of identical ones.
  • Memory allocation spikes. With millions of cache misses, each application server allocates memory for every in-flight database response simultaneously. Node.js heap usage and Python memory footprints can spike 10–20× in seconds, triggering GC pauses or OOM kills before the database even responds.
  • It cannot be fixed reactively. A single hot-key miss is noticed immediately from a single latency spike. Mass expiry produces a slow-rolling wave that may not be detected in monitoring until the database is already failing — by which point the queue is too deep to drain.

The key intuition: a single hot-key stampede is a spike — intense but targeted. Mass TTL expiry is an avalanche — every wall of the dam collapses simultaneously. The correct counter-measure for mass expiry is jitter, applied before the fact — not locks, applied reactively. You cannot lock your way out of a million simultaneous misses.

Mass expiry is triggered by four common engineering practices that seem reasonable in isolation:

  1. Cache warm-up scripts with a single fixed TTL — as in the second story above. The script's sequential nature means keys expire in the same sequence and bunching they were written.
  2. Post-deployment cache flushes — flushing Redis after a code deploy to ensure stale data is cleared, then letting the application repopulate all keys organically. Every user who visits in the next few minutes generates a cache miss.
  3. Batch jobs that write many keys at midnight — nightly ETL jobs that regenerate pricing, rankings, or recommendation scores and write them all at 02:00 AM with a 24-hour TTL. Every night at 02:00 AM, the entire cache turns cold simultaneously.
  4. Feature flags or configuration values cached at application startup — all app servers start simultaneously after a deploy and cache config values with the same TTL. All expire simultaneously one hour later.
Strategy 1

Mutex / Distributed Lock

The mutex strategy is the most intuitive approach: when a cache miss occurs, only one request is allowed to fetch from the database and repopulate the cache. All other requests must wait — or, if a stale value exists, be served the stale value while the refresh happens.

The implementation uses Redis's SET key value EX ttl NX command (NX means "only set if not exists"). This is an atomic operation: only one caller can succeed. The first request to experience the cache miss calls SET lock:product:123 1 EX 10 NX. If it gets back OK, it is the lock holder. It fetches from the database, writes the result to Redis, then deletes the lock key. All other requests that called the same SETNX command got back nil instead of OK — they know someone else holds the lock.

There are two variants for what the non-lock-holders do while waiting:

Variant A — Wait and poll: Non-lock-holders sleep for a short interval (10–50ms), then check the cache again. When the lock holder has written the value, the next poll finds the cache populated and returns it. This introduces latency for all waiting requests equal to the DB fetch time plus polling overhead, but ensures every response is fresh.

Variant B — Serve stale: If the cache stores a stale (expired-but-still-present) copy of the value, non-lock-holders serve it immediately. This requires a two-TTL design: the "logical TTL" (when the value should be refreshed) and the "physical TTL" (when Redis actually deletes the key — set much longer, e.g. logical + 60 seconds). When a request sees logical TTL has passed, it attempts the lock. If it gets the lock, it refreshes. If not, it returns the stale value from the physical TTL window.

The drawbacks of the mutex approach are real and worth understanding for an interview:

  • Lock TTL must exceed DB query time — if the lock expires before the database fetch completes (e.g., a slow query), the lock is released and a second stampede begins. You must set the lock TTL conservatively high, but then a crashed lock-holder blocks all traffic for the full lock duration.
  • Single point of contention — 50,000 requests all competing for one Redis key creates a serialisation bottleneck. On extremely high-traffic keys, this itself becomes a performance problem.
  • Herd at lock release — if using Variant A (wait and poll), all waiting threads wake up and re-check simultaneously when the lock is released. The stampede is not eliminated, just deferred by one DB round-trip.
  • Lock holder crash — if the process holding the lock crashes before populating the cache, the lock expires after its TTL and then one new request attempts the lock. During that entire window, traffic is either blocked or serving stale data.

The mutex strategy is best suited to moderate-traffic scenarios where a simple, correct implementation is more valuable than theoretically optimal performance, and where stale data is not acceptable (so Variant B is off the table).

Strategy 2

Background / Proactive Refresh

Background refresh inverts the problem entirely: instead of waiting for a cache miss and then reacting, the system proactively refreshes hot keys before they expire. From a user's perspective, the cache never misses — because the value is always there.

There are two implementation variants:

Variant A — Fixed-schedule warmer: A cron job or scheduled worker runs on a fixed interval (e.g., every 5 minutes) and refreshes a known list of hot keys. For the Flipkart scenario, this warmer would have refreshed the iPhone 16 Pro cache entry at 11:55 PM, 11:50 PM, and so on — ensuring the key never expired naturally during the traffic spike. The new discounted price would be pushed at exactly midnight by the warmer, not by a TTL expiry.

Variant B — TTL-minus-buffer async trigger: This variant does not require knowing hot keys in advance. The cache entry stores two values: the cached content, and a refreshAt timestamp set to expiry - buffer (e.g., 30 seconds before natural expiry). When any request reads the cache and finds that refreshAt is in the past, it fires an asynchronous background task to refresh the value — but immediately returns the current (still valid) cached value to the user. The first request to see the refresh trigger kicks off the background job; subsequent requests during the refresh continue to serve the current value. By the time natural expiry would have occurred, the background job has already written a fresh value with a new TTL.

The key data structure for Variant B looks like this: store a JSON object in Redis rather than a plain string — {"value": "...", "refreshAt": 1720000000, "expiry": 1720000030}. The physical Redis TTL is set to expiry + safetyBuffer so the key is never evicted before the refresh has a chance to complete.

Background refresh has significant advantages: zero wait time for users, zero database stampede, and the refresh is completely invisible to end users. It is the most operationally clean solution when it applies.

Background refresh is perfect for predictable hot keys — homepage content, product catalog, app configuration, top-10 leaderboards — but it is blind to surprise viral content. A tweet that sends 200,000 users to an obscure product page in 60 seconds cannot be pre-warmed. For unknown hot keys, you need a probabilistic strategy like XFetch.

The other drawback of background refresh is wasted compute: if a key is refreshed every 5 minutes but rarely read in the last 4.5 minutes of its TTL, those refresh cycles were pure overhead. For a cache with hundreds of thousands of keys, pre-warming all of them is infeasible. Selective warming requires either heuristics (refresh only the top-N most-read keys) or a dynamic hot-key detection system that adds operational complexity.

Strategy 3

XFetch — The Optimal Probabilistic Early-Expiry Algorithm

XFetch was published at VLDB 2015 by Vattani, Chiariotti, and Tolomei in "An Optimal Algorithm for Invalidation-based Cache Coherence." It is arguably the most elegant solution to the cache stampede problem because it requires no coordination, no locks, no background workers, and no knowledge of which keys are hot. Every application instance independently decides, on each cache read, whether to proactively refresh the cached value — using a single probabilistic formula.

The algorithm replaces the standard "if TTL expired, fetch from DB" logic with:

XFetch formula:
currentTime - (delta × beta × Math.log(Math.random())) >= expiry

If this condition is true, refresh the cache proactively. Otherwise, return the cached value as normal.

Each variable has a precise meaning:

  • currentTime — the current UNIX timestamp in seconds.
  • expiry — the UNIX timestamp at which the cached value expires (i.e., when it was set: setTime + ttl). Stored alongside the cached value.
  • delta — how long it took to compute or fetch the cached value from the database, in seconds. This is measured at write time and stored alongside the value (e.g., if the DB query took 0.2 seconds, delta = 0.2). Keys that are expensive to recompute get proportionally more aggressive early refreshes.
  • beta — a tuning parameter, default 1.0. Higher values (e.g., 2 or 3) make the algorithm refresh earlier and more aggressively. Lower values (e.g., 0.5) make it more conservative. The paper proves that beta = 1 is optimal: it produces exactly one early refresh on average before natural expiry.
  • Math.log(Math.random()) — the natural logarithm of a uniform random number in (0, 1]. Because Math.random() returns values in [0, 1) and the log of a number in (0, 1) is always negative, this term is always negative. Multiplying by the negative sign implicit in subtraction (currentTime - (positive × negative) = currentTime + something positive) means the left-hand side is always greater than currentTime.

The intuition is straightforward: as the current time approaches the expiry time, the gap between expiry and currentTime shrinks. The random offset term on the left side has a fixed distribution. As the gap shrinks, the probability that the random offset exceeds the remaining gap increases. When there is still an hour left on the TTL, the probability is essentially zero — no request will trigger an early refresh. When there are 30 seconds left, the probability is appreciable. When there are 5 seconds left, almost every request will trigger a refresh.

The algorithm is also naturally self-throttling in a distributed system. Once one server refreshes the cache and writes a new value with a new expiry, all other servers immediately see a fresh key with a distant expiry — the XFetch formula returns false for all of them, and no further early refreshes occur until the next window.

To implement XFetch, you need to store three values alongside the cached content: value (the actual data), delta (the measured fetch time in seconds), and expiry (the UNIX timestamp of expiration). A Redis hash works well for this, or a JSON-encoded string value. The physical TTL on the Redis key should be set to exactly ttl seconds — XFetch handles the early-refresh window purely through the probabilistic check, not by extending the physical TTL.

When the XFetch condition is true, the correct behaviour is to trigger an asynchronous background refresh — not a synchronous one. The current request returns the still-valid cached value immediately. The background task fetches from the DB, measures the new delta, and writes the refreshed value back. This preserves the zero-latency user experience that makes XFetch superior to the mutex approach.

Strategy 4

TTL Jitter — The Simplest Defence

TTL jitter is the simplest and most universally applicable defence. Instead of setting every cache key to a fixed TTL like 3600 seconds, you add a small random offset: 

// Without jitter — all keys set at the same time expire at the same time
const ttl = 3600;

// With jitter — keys set at the same time expire in a 30-second window
const ttl = 3600 + Math.floor(Math.random() * 30);

The effect is to desynchronise expiry times across keys. If you flush and repopulate 10,000 cache keys during a deploy, without jitter all 10,000 expire simultaneously one hour later. With jitter spread over 30 seconds, only roughly 333 expire per second — a manageable rate that the database can absorb.

The important limitation to understand for system design interviews is that TTL jitter only helps when many different keys are at risk of expiring simultaneously. It does not help with a single hot key. If your iPhone 16 Pro product page has a TTL of 3600 + random(30), it still expires at some point, and at that moment all 50,000 concurrent users still miss simultaneously. Jitter spreads the expiry across a 30-second window, but a key still has exactly one expiry moment.

TTL jitter is best used as a baseline hygiene measure for:

  • CDN invalidations after deploys — use staggered TTLs so not every edge node revalidates at once
  • Cache warming after a full flush — populate keys with jittered TTLs to prevent a future mass-expiry
  • Category caches (e.g., all product listings with the same 1-hour TTL) — add jitter so they do not all expire in the same second

Never set TTLs to round numbers, especially ones that align with known traffic patterns. TTL = 3600 set at midnight means expiry at 1 AM — another peak hour. TTL = 3647 is significantly safer.

Strategy 4 · Jitter Formulas

TTL Jitter — Formulas, Mathematics, and When to Use Each

Jitter is a random offset applied to a base TTL value to desynchronise the expiry of multiple keys that would otherwise expire at the same time. The concept is simple; the subtlety is in choosing the right jitter formula for the situation. There are three well-established approaches, each with different statistical properties and use cases.

1. Additive Jitter

Additive jitter adds a fixed random window to the base TTL. The formula is:

actual_TTL = TTL_base + random(0, TTL_base × jitter_factor)

Example with 10% jitter: actual_TTL = 3600 + random(0, 360)

Result: expiry times spread uniformly over a 360-second window after the base TTL.

Additive jitter is the most common form and the easiest to reason about. If you write 1,000 keys simultaneously with a base TTL of 3600 and 10% additive jitter, the keys will expire at a roughly uniform rate of ~2.8 keys per second over the 360-second jitter window, instead of all 1,000 expiring in the same second. The database sees approximately 2.8 cache misses per second instead of 1,000 simultaneous misses — a 357× reduction in burst load.

When to use: General purpose. Good default for any caching scenario. The lower bound is the base TTL, so keys are guaranteed to live for at least that long. This matters when your data has a strict minimum freshness requirement.

2. Multiplicative Jitter

Multiplicative jitter scales the random window relative to the base TTL using a multiplier centered on 1.0:

actual_TTL = TTL_base × (1 - jitter_half + random() × jitter_width)

Example: actual_TTL = TTL_base × (0.9 + random() × 0.2)

Result: TTL uniformly distributed in [TTL_base × 0.9, TTL_base × 1.1] — ±10% of base.

Multiplicative jitter centers the distribution around the intended TTL rather than pushing all keys longer. Half the keys will expire slightly before the base TTL; half will expire slightly after. This is more appropriate when the TTL represents a target freshness guarantee and you want equal probability of being early or late.

The practical difference from additive jitter is subtle but important at scale. With additive jitter, your p50 expiry is TTL + 0.05 × jitter_window — slightly longer than intended. With symmetric multiplicative jitter, p50 expiry is exactly TTL_base.

When to use: When TTL represents a data freshness SLA and you need to honor it on average — e.g., "user session data must not be older than 30 minutes on average." Multiplicative jitter keeps the mean expiry at exactly 30 minutes while spreading the distribution to avoid synchronized expiry.

3. AWS-Style Full Jitter

Full jitter, popularized by the AWS Architecture Blog's 2015 article on exponential backoff, does not preserve the base TTL at all. The key expires at a uniformly random time between 0 and the base TTL:

actual_TTL = random(0, TTL_base)

Example: actual_TTL = random(0, 3600)

Result: keys expire uniformly distributed over the entire [0, TTL_base] window.

Mean TTL: TTL_base / 2 — on average, keys live only half as long.

Full jitter provides the absolute maximum desynchronisation — expiry is spread uniformly over the entire TTL window rather than bunched near the end. The cost is that on average you are refreshing twice as often as the base TTL implies, consuming twice the database query budget. Individual keys may expire almost immediately (if random() returns near 0), which can be surprising in debugging.

When to use: Cases where stampede prevention is paramount and the cost of more frequent refreshes is acceptable — for example, cache keys that are very cheap to reconstruct but have extremely high concurrency. Also appropriate for retry backoff timers (the original AWS use case), not just TTL assignment. Not appropriate when your data has a minimum freshness guarantee that must be honored.

JavaScript — All Three Jitter Formulas 
// jitter-strategies.js
// All functions take TTL in seconds, return jittered TTL in seconds

/**
 * Additive jitter: TTL_base + random(0, TTL_base * factor)
 * Guarantees minimum freshness equal to TTL_base.
 * Default factor = 0.10 (10% window)
 */
function additiveJitter(ttlBase, factor = 0.10) {
  const jitterWindow = Math.floor(ttlBase * factor);
  return ttlBase + Math.floor(Math.random() * jitterWindow);
}

/**
 * Multiplicative jitter: TTL_base * (center ± half_range)
 * Symmetric: mean TTL equals TTL_base exactly.
 * Default: ±10% (0.9 to 1.1)
 */
function multiplicativeJitter(ttlBase, halfRange = 0.10) {
  const lowerBound = 1.0 - halfRange;
  const spread = halfRange * 2;
  const multiplier = lowerBound + Math.random() * spread;
  return Math.floor(ttlBase * multiplier);
}

/**
 * Full jitter (AWS-style): random(0, TTL_base)
 * Maximum desynchronization. Mean TTL = TTL_base / 2.
 * Use only when minimum freshness is not required.
 */
function fullJitter(ttlBase) {
  return Math.floor(Math.random() * ttlBase);
}

// Usage examples:
const BASE_TTL = 3600; // 1 hour

console.log('Additive:', additiveJitter(BASE_TTL));
// e.g. 3742 — always >= 3600

console.log('Multiplicative:', multiplicativeJitter(BASE_TTL));
// e.g. 3518 or 3681 — centered around 3600

console.log('Full:', fullJitter(BASE_TTL));
// e.g. 1847 — anywhere from 0 to 3600

// In practice — wrap your Redis SET calls:
async function setWithJitter(redis, key, value, baseTtl, strategy = 'additive') {
  const jitteredTtl = strategy === 'full'
    ? fullJitter(baseTtl)
    : strategy === 'multiplicative'
      ? multiplicativeJitter(baseTtl)
      : additiveJitter(baseTtl);

  await redis.set(key, JSON.stringify(value), 'EX', jitteredTtl);
  return jitteredTtl; // Return for logging/debugging
}
Python — Jitter Formulas with functools.lru_cache Integration 
import random
import math
import time
import redis

r = redis.Redis(host='localhost', port=6379, decode_responses=True)

def additive_jitter(ttl_base: int, factor: float = 0.10) -> int:
    """TTL_base + random(0, TTL_base * factor). Minimum = TTL_base."""
    jitter_window = int(ttl_base * factor)
    return ttl_base + random.randint(0, jitter_window)

def multiplicative_jitter(ttl_base: int, half_range: float = 0.10) -> int:
    """TTL_base * (0.9 to 1.1). Mean = TTL_base. Symmetric distribution."""
    lower = 1.0 - half_range
    spread = half_range * 2
    return int(ttl_base * (lower + random.random() * spread))

def full_jitter(ttl_base: int) -> int:
    """random(0, TTL_base). Mean = TTL_base / 2. Maximum desync."""
    return random.randint(0, ttl_base)

def cache_set(key: str, value, base_ttl: int = 3600, strategy: str = 'additive'):
    """Write to Redis with jittered TTL."""
    if strategy == 'multiplicative':
        ttl = multiplicative_jitter(base_ttl)
    elif strategy == 'full':
        ttl = full_jitter(base_ttl)
    else:  # additive
        ttl = additive_jitter(base_ttl)

    r.setex(key, ttl, __import__('json').dumps(value))
    return ttl

    for product_id in product_ids:
        data = fetch_product_from_db(product_id)  # your DB fetch fn
        ttl = additive_jitter(base_ttl, factor=0.15)  # 15% window
        key = f"product:{product_id}"
        pipeline.setex(key, ttl, __import__('json').dumps(data))
        # With 1M products and 15% jitter, expiry is spread over 540 seconds
        # = ~1,852 expirations per second instead of 1M/second
    pipeline.execute()
Strategy 3 · In Production

XFetch and Jitter: Two Layers for Two Problems

TTL jitter prevents mass expiry but does not help with individual hot keys that attract extreme concurrent load. For those, XFetch (the formula and variables are described in Strategy 3 above) lets a single node proactively rebuild the value before it expires, eliminating the miss window. The implementation in the code section below applies additive jitter on every write, so the two techniques compose.

The key production insight missed in textbook explanations is the tight coupling between delta and the TTL jitter strategy. XFetch works per-key, probabilistically. Jitter works at population level, deterministically. They solve different parts of the problem:

  • Jitter prevents 1.4 million keys from expiring in the same second (population-level problem).
  • XFetch prevents the single product page with 200,000 concurrent readers from generating a stampede when its individual TTL arrives (hot-key problem).
  • You need both, applied at different layers.

In the production outage story, XFetch alone would not have saved the system — because the problem was mass expiry of unique keys, not a hot single key. But adding XFetch as a second layer means that even after jitter has spread the expiry wave, the most popular individual products (which attract the highest concurrent load) receive an additional layer of protection when their individual TTL approaches.

Production Architecture

Layered Defence — The Production Combination

No single strategy is sufficient for a production system at scale. Each strategy addresses a different failure mode, and each has gaps that the others cover. The right answer for a system design interview — and for production — is to layer them.

Layer 1 — TTL Jitter on all keys. This is pure hygiene with zero operational overhead. Add it to every cache write unconditionally. It prevents mass-expiry events caused by simultaneous cache population (deploys, cache flushes, batch jobs). Cost: two lines of code. Benefit: eliminates an entire class of stampede.

Layer 2 — Background refresh for known hot keys. Maintain a list of high-priority keys whose absence would cause catastrophic load: homepage, top product listings, app configuration, feature flags. A scheduled worker refreshes these on a fixed cadence shorter than their TTL. For the Flipkart scenario, the iPhone 16 Pro listing would be on this list, and the midnight price reveal would be pushed by the warmer rather than by a TTL expiry. Cost: a small warmer service and a hot-key registry. Benefit: zero miss rate for your most important content.

Layer 3 — XFetch on all cache reads. For everything not covered by the background warmer — all the unknown viral content, the tail of the long-tail catalog, user-specific caches — wrap every cache read in the XFetch formula. This is a drop-in replacement for the standard cache-aside pattern. Cost: storing delta and expiry alongside cached values; a few nanoseconds of computation per cache read. Benefit: coordination-free probabilistic protection for every key in the cache.

Layer 4 — Circuit breaker on the DB read path. Even with the above layers, your database will occasionally see elevated load during unusual events. A circuit breaker sits in front of the database call and opens when the error rate or latency exceeds a threshold (e.g., >5% error rate in 10 seconds, or p99 latency > 2 seconds). When open, the circuit returns a fast failure (503 or stale data) rather than adding more load to an already-struggling database. This prevents retry storms from turning a degradation into a complete outage. Libraries like Hystrix, Resilience4j, and Cockatiel implement this pattern.

Layer 5 — Redis High Availability. All of the above strategies assume Redis is available. If Redis goes down, every request misses the cache simultaneously — the worst possible stampede. Redis Sentinel provides automatic failover to a replica in approximately 30 seconds. Redis Cluster provides instant failover with no single point of failure (each shard is replicated). For critical applications, Redis Cluster is the right default.

The request flow in the layered system looks like this: a request arrives and reads from Redis. The application checks the XFetch condition. If XFetch triggers, an async background task refreshes the value while the current request returns the cached value. Separately, the background warmer maintains freshness for known hot keys on a schedule. TTL jitter ensures no mass-expiry event occurs after bulk cache operations. If Redis is unavailable, the circuit breaker limits the load reaching the database.

Defence in depth for caching: Jitter prevents mass expiry. Background refresh protects known hot keys. XFetch protects unknown hot keys. Circuit breaker prevents cascading failure. Redis HA prevents total cache unavailability. Each layer compensates for the blind spots of the others.

Two further layers from the production deep dive slot in beneath these: single-flight on the L3 (database) fallback collapses concurrent misses for the same key into one query per process, and an L1 in-process cache (added only after the others are validated, and only for keys that tolerate brief staleness) cuts Redis round trips and the stampede surface.

Pattern

Request Coalescing: The Single-Flight Pattern

TTL jitter and multi-level caching solve the mass-expiry problem and reduce per-request latency. But they do not fully solve the case where a single expensive cache key expires during a burst of concurrent requests all targeting the same key. For that scenario, the single-flight pattern (also called request coalescing or request deduplication) is the most elegant solution.

The idea is simple: if 1,000 requests simultaneously ask for the same cache key and the cache is cold, only one of those requests should issue the database query. The other 999 should wait in-process for that one query to complete, then share its result. There is no distributed coordination required — this is a per-process, in-memory deduplication.

Single-flight in Go: The golang.org/x/sync/singleflight package implements this pattern natively. In Go, it is idiomatic and widely used. In JavaScript, it is implemented manually using a Promise map. In Java, it can be implemented with CompletableFuture and a ConcurrentHashMap.

JavaScript — Single-Flight / Request Coalescing 
// single-flight.js
// A per-process in-flight request deduplicator.
// Guarantees that only one DB call is in-flight at a time per unique key.

class SingleFlight {
  constructor() {
    // Map from cache key → Promise of in-flight fetch
    this._inFlight = new Map();
  }

  /**
   * If a fetch for `key` is already in-flight, join it.
   * Otherwise, start a new fetch. All callers waiting on the same key
   * receive the same resolved value from a single database call.
   *
   * @param {string}   key     Cache key (used as dedup key)
   * @param {Function} fetchFn Async function that fetches from DB
   * @returns {Promise}   Resolved value (shared across all waiters)
   */
  async do(key, fetchFn) {
    // If a fetch is already in-flight for this key, join it
    if (this._inFlight.has(key)) {
      return this._inFlight.get(key);
    }

    // Start a new fetch — all future callers will join this promise
    const promise = fetchFn().finally(() => {
      // Clean up after the fetch completes (success or failure)
      this._inFlight.delete(key);
    });

    this._inFlight.set(key, promise);
    return promise;
  }
}

// Singleton per process
const singleFlight = new SingleFlight();

// Integration with the multi-level cache:
async function getProductWithCoalescing(productId) {
  const cacheKey = `product:${productId}`;

  // Check L1 first (no coalescing needed — nanosecond lookup)
  const l1Hit = l1Cache.get(cacheKey);
  if (l1Hit) return l1Hit;

  // Check L2 (Redis) — no coalescing needed, Redis handles concurrency
  const l2Raw = await redis.get(cacheKey);
  if (l2Raw) {
    const data = JSON.parse(l2Raw);
    l1Cache.set(cacheKey, data);
    return data;
  }

  // L2 miss → use single-flight for L3 (database) call
  // Even with 10,000 concurrent requests for the same cold key,
  // only ONE database query will execute. The rest wait and share the result.
  return singleFlight.do(cacheKey, async () => {
    const rows = await db.query(
      'SELECT * FROM products WHERE id = $1',
      [productId]
    );
    const data = rows[0] ?? null;

    if (data) {
      const ttl = additiveJitter(3600);
      await redis.set(cacheKey, JSON.stringify(data), 'EX', ttl);
      l1Cache.set(cacheKey, data);
    }

    return data;
  });
}

Go — Single-Flight Using golang.org/x/sync/singleflight 
// product_cache.go
package cache

import (
    "context"
    "encoding/json"
    "time"

    "github.com/go-redis/redis/v8"
    "golang.org/x/sync/singleflight"
)

type ProductCache struct {
    redis  *redis.Client
    group  singleflight.Group
    db     DB
}

func (c *ProductCache) GetProduct(ctx context.Context, productID string) (*Product, error) {
    cacheKey := "product:" + productID

    // L2: Try Redis first
    raw, err := c.redis.Get(ctx, cacheKey).Result()
    if err == nil {
        var product Product
        json.Unmarshal([]byte(raw), &product)
        return &product, nil
    }

    // L2 miss: use singleflight to deduplicate DB calls
    // If 50,000 goroutines are waiting for this key, singleflight
    // ensures exactly ONE db.GetProduct() call fires.
    result, err, _ := c.group.Do(cacheKey, func() (interface{}, error) {
        product, err := c.db.GetProduct(ctx, productID)
        if err != nil {
            return nil, err
        }

        // Write back to Redis with jitter
        data, _ := json.Marshal(product)
        ttl := time.Duration(3600+rand.Intn(360)) * time.Second
        c.redis.Set(ctx, cacheKey, data, ttl)

        return product, nil
    })

    if err != nil {
        return nil, err
    }

    return result.(*Product), nil
}

Single-flight is particularly powerful in Go because the language's goroutine model means many thousands of goroutines can cheaply block on a channel waiting for a shared result. In JavaScript, the Promise-based implementation achieves the same effect via the event loop. In Python, an asyncio.Event or asyncio.shield achieves the same pattern in async code.

The limitation of single-flight is that it is per-process. If you have 20 application servers and a key goes cold simultaneously, each server will issue one database query — for a total of 20 queries, not 1. For truly extreme traffic, combine single-flight (per-process) with a distributed mutex (cross-process) for the L3 fallback. In practice, 20 queries instead of 200,000 queries is still a 10,000× improvement, and the additional complexity of a distributed mutex is rarely justified.

Architecture

Multi-Level Caching: L1, L2, L3 Architecture

Even a Redis cluster with microsecond read latency can become a bottleneck at extreme concurrency. If your application has 20 instances each serving 5,000 requests per second, and every request requires one Redis lookup, you are issuing 100,000 Redis operations per second. Redis handles this comfortably — but the network round-trip (even at 0.3ms per hop) adds up: 100,000 × 0.3ms = 30 CPU-seconds of wait time per second across the fleet.

The solution is multi-level caching — a hierarchy of three stores, each with different latency, capacity, and scope:

L1: In-Process Cache (Nanoseconds)

An in-process LRU (Least Recently Used) cache lives in the same memory space as the application. There is no network hop, no serialisation — just a hash map lookup in process memory. This is the fastest possible cache.

  • Latency: 50–500 nanoseconds (memory access)
  • Capacity: bounded by process heap — typically 64MB–512MB
  • Scope: per-process only — each application instance has its own L1
  • TTL: short — 1–30 seconds. Stale data risk is highest here.
  • Node.js: lru-cache npm package
  • Python: functools.lru_cache (size-bounded) or cachetools.TTLCache
  • Java: Caffeine or Guava Cache

The critical L1 tradeoff is data consistency across instances. If you have 20 app servers and a product price changes, up to 20 separate L1 caches may hold the stale price for up to the L1 TTL (e.g., 10 seconds). For most applications, this is acceptable. For financial or inventory data, it may not be.

L2: Shared Redis Cluster (Microseconds)

Redis is the canonical L2 cache: all application instances share it, so a write from one instance is immediately visible to all others. This is the primary cache tier for most production systems.

  • Latency: 0.1–2 milliseconds (network round-trip to local Redis)
  • Capacity: bounded by Redis cluster memory — typically GBs to TBs
  • Scope: shared across all application instances in the cluster
  • TTL: medium — minutes to hours. Jitter applied here.

L3: Database (Milliseconds)

The database (PostgreSQL, MySQL, DynamoDB) is the source of truth. All cache misses eventually fall through to L3. This tier is expensive, has limited connection pools, and must be protected at all costs.

  • Latency: 5–500 milliseconds depending on query complexity and index usage
  • Capacity: effectively unlimited (bounded by storage, not memory)
  • Scope: single source of truth for all instances
  • TTL: no TTL — the data lives here permanently until mutated

Three-Tier Lookup Code

The lookup order is always L1 → L2 → L3 (fast to slow). The write-back order is always L3 → L2 → L1 (authoritative to derived). On a write, you update the database first (L3), then invalidate or update L2 Redis, then invalidate L1 on each instance (via pub/sub or short TTL expiry).

JavaScript — Three-Level Cache Lookup 
// multi-level-cache.js
import { LRUCache } from 'lru-cache';
import Redis from 'ioredis';

const redis = new Redis(process.env.REDIS_URL);

// L1: In-process LRU cache
// max 500 items, each lives at most 15 seconds in-process
const l1Cache = new LRUCache({
  max: 500,
  ttl: 15_000,  // 15 seconds in milliseconds
  ttlAutopurge: false,  // Lazy eviction — only purge on access
});

// L2: Redis (configured externally)
// L3: Database client (configured externally)

const L2_BASE_TTL = 3600;      // 1 hour base
const L2_JITTER_FACTOR = 0.10; // ±10% jitter window

function jitteredTtl(base = L2_BASE_TTL) {
  return base + Math.floor(Math.random() * base * L2_JITTER_FACTOR);
}

/**
 * Multi-level cache read: L1 → L2 → L3
 * Populates higher-level caches on miss (write-back).
 */
async function getProduct(productId) {
  const l1Key = `product:${productId}`;

  // ── L1 lookup (in-process, nanoseconds) ──────────────────────────────────
  const l1Hit = l1Cache.get(l1Key);
  if (l1Hit !== undefined) {
    // metrics.increment('cache.l1.hit');
    return l1Hit;
  }
  // metrics.increment('cache.l1.miss');

  // ── L2 lookup (Redis, microseconds) ─────────────────────────────────────
  const l2Raw = await redis.get(l1Key);
  if (l2Raw !== null) {
    const parsed = JSON.parse(l2Raw);
    // Populate L1 from L2 (short TTL — 15s)
    l1Cache.set(l1Key, parsed);
    // metrics.increment('cache.l2.hit');
    return parsed;
  }
  // metrics.increment('cache.l2.miss');

  // ── L3 lookup (database, milliseconds) ──────────────────────────────────
  // metrics.increment('cache.l3.query');
  const product = await db.query(
    'SELECT id, name, price, inventory FROM products WHERE id = $1',
    [productId]
  );
  if (!product.rows[0]) return null;

  const data = product.rows[0];

  // Write-back: populate L2 with jitter, then L1
  const ttl = jitteredTtl();
  await redis.set(l1Key, JSON.stringify(data), 'EX', ttl);
  l1Cache.set(l1Key, data);

  return data;
}

/**
 * Multi-level cache invalidation: database write → L2 del → L1 del
 * Always invalidate from source-of-truth outward.
 */
async function updateProduct(productId, updates) {
  // 1. Write to database (L3) — source of truth
  await db.query(
    'UPDATE products SET price = $1, inventory = $2 WHERE id = $3',
    [updates.price, updates.inventory, productId]
  );

  // 2. Invalidate L2 (Redis) — all instances will miss on next read
  const key = `product:${productId}`;
  await redis.del(key);

  // 3. Invalidate L1 on all instances via Redis pub/sub
  // Each app server listens to 'cache:invalidate' channel and clears its L1
  await redis.publish('cache:invalidate', JSON.stringify({ key }));
}

// Subscribe to invalidation events (run once per process at startup)
function subscribeToInvalidations() {
  const subscriber = redis.duplicate();
  subscriber.subscribe('cache:invalidate');
  subscriber.on('message', (channel, message) => {
    const { key } = JSON.parse(message);
    l1Cache.delete(key); // Evict from this process's L1
    // console.log(`[L1 invalidated] ${key}`);
  });
}
Python — Three-Level Cache with TTLCache and Redis 
# multi_level_cache.py
import json
import time
import redis
from cachetools import TTLCache
from threading import Lock

r = redis.Redis(host='localhost', port=6379, decode_responses=True)

# L1: thread-safe in-process TTL cache
# Holds at most 1000 items, each for 15 seconds
_l1_cache = TTLCache(maxsize=1000, ttl=15)
_l1_lock = Lock()  # TTLCache is not thread-safe; protect with lock

L2_BASE_TTL = 3600

def _jittered_ttl(base: int = L2_BASE_TTL, factor: float = 0.10) -> int:
    import random
    return base + random.randint(0, int(base * factor))

def get_product(product_id: str) -> dict | None:
    cache_key = f"product:{product_id}"

    # L1: in-process cache (nanoseconds)
    with _l1_lock:
        if cache_key in _l1_cache:
            return _l1_cache[cache_key]

    # L2: Redis (microseconds)
    raw = r.get(cache_key)
    if raw is not None:
        data = json.loads(raw)
        with _l1_lock:
            _l1_cache[cache_key] = data  # Populate L1
        return data

    # L3: Database (milliseconds)
    # data = db.execute("SELECT * FROM products WHERE id = %s", (product_id,))
    data = fetch_from_db(product_id)  # your DB fetch fn
    if data is None:
        return None

    # Write-back: L3 → L2 → L1
    ttl = _jittered_ttl()
    r.setex(cache_key, ttl, json.dumps(data))
    with _l1_lock:
        _l1_cache[cache_key] = data

    return data

def update_product(product_id: str, updates: dict) -> None:
    """Invalidate from source of truth outward: L3 → L2 → L1."""
    cache_key = f"product:{product_id}"

    # 1. Persist to database (L3)
    save_to_db(product_id, updates)

    # 2. Delete from Redis (L2)
    r.delete(cache_key)

    # 3. Invalidate L1 locally (pub/sub handles remote instances)
    with _l1_lock:
        _l1_cache.pop(cache_key, None)

    # Publish invalidation to other instances
    r.publish('cache:invalidate', json.dumps({'key': cache_key}))

L1 Cache: The Consistency-Latency Tradeoff

The L1 cache trades consistency for latency. For a 15-second L1 TTL across 20 app servers, after a product price update, up to 20 × 15 = 300 person-seconds of stale price serves will occur before all L1 caches naturally expire. The pub/sub invalidation above reduces this to near-zero for most cases, but pub/sub delivery is not guaranteed under network partitions.

Engineering judgment is required:

  • Product descriptions, images, non-financial metadata: 30-second L1 TTL, no pub/sub needed.
  • Product prices, discount percentages: 5-second L1 TTL + pub/sub invalidation.
  • Inventory counts: Skip L1 entirely — serve from Redis directly. Inventory stale data causes overselling.
  • User authentication tokens: Skip L1 — security-sensitive, must be authoritative.
Advanced

Invalidation Cascade Strategy

Multi-level caching introduces a new problem: when data changes, how do you ensure all three cache tiers reflect the change promptly? Stale data sitting in L1 across 20 app servers can cause subtle bugs — users seeing old prices, ghost inventory, outdated permissions.

The canonical strategy is event-driven invalidation via a message bus:

  1. Write completes at L3 (database). The application, a CDC (Change Data Capture) connector, or a database trigger emits a product.updated event to Kafka or Redis Pub/Sub.
  2. L2 (Redis) invalidation. The event consumer deletes or updates the Redis key. All subsequent L2 reads will miss and fall through to L3.
  3. L1 invalidation across all instances. The event consumer publishes to a Redis Pub/Sub channel (cache:invalidate). Every app server process subscribes and immediately evicts the key from its in-process L1 cache.

Invalidation vs. update: prefer invalidation (delete the key) over in-place update (write the new value) for L2 and L1. Invalidation is simpler, avoids races between the write and a concurrent read, and ensures the next reader fetches fresh data from L3 directly. The only exception is when you have a high-frequency write pattern and can tolerate brief read amplification — in that case, update-in-place avoids the L3 fallback read.

The weak point of pub/sub invalidation is delivery guarantees. Redis Pub/Sub is fire-and-forget: if a subscriber is temporarily disconnected or lagged, it misses the invalidation message. The mitigation is a short L1 TTL (15–30 seconds) as a backstop — even if the pub/sub message is lost, the L1 entry will expire naturally within 30 seconds, bounding the stale-data window. This is an intentional design choice: pub/sub for fast-path invalidation, TTL for guaranteed eventual consistency.

For stricter consistency requirements (financial data, access control lists), skip L1 entirely and serve directly from L2 (Redis). Redis provides sufficient latency for most financial systems (sub-millisecond reads from a local cluster), and it is a shared, centralised store that can be invalidated with strong consistency.

Interview Framing

"What If Redis Goes Down?" — The Follow-Up Question

In nearly every system design interview that involves caching, the follow-up question is: "What happens if Redis goes down entirely?" This is a different problem from key expiry. It is total cache unavailability. Your answer to this question signals whether you think in production failure modes or only in happy-path architectures.

Here is the answer framework:

Circuit breaker — detect and limit. When Redis connection timeout rate exceeds a threshold (e.g., 10% of requests in a 5-second window), open the circuit. Stop sending requests to Redis at all. This does two things: it stops accumulating timed-out Redis connections in your application thread pool, and it forces a decision about what to do instead. The circuit breaker should have a half-open state: periodically send one probe request to Redis; if it succeeds, close the circuit and resume normal operation.

DB fallback with rate limiting. When the circuit is open and traffic falls through to the database, the database will see more traffic than it was dimensioned for. This must be controlled. Use a token bucket rate limiter to cap how many DB queries per second reach the database (based on known capacity), returning 503 to requests that exceed the limit. This is graceful degradation: some users get errors, but the database remains alive for the users who do get through.

Secondary in-memory LRU cache. Application servers can maintain a small in-process LRU cache (e.g., 500 MB of heap, last 10,000 keys by recency). This is not a replacement for Redis — it is much smaller, not shared across instances, and goes stale. But it means that during a Redis outage, the most recently accessed keys are still served from memory. The cache hit rate drops significantly, but it does not drop to zero.

Redis Sentinel vs Redis Cluster — knowing the difference matters. Redis Sentinel is the older HA mechanism: one primary, one or more replicas, and a set of sentinel processes that monitor and execute failover. Failover takes approximately 30 seconds (detect failure → elect new primary → update clients). During those 30 seconds, writes may be lost and reads are unavailable unless clients fall back to replicas. Redis Cluster is the modern approach: data is partitioned across multiple primary-replica pairs. If one primary goes down, its replica is automatically promoted — typically within a few seconds, and other shards remain fully available throughout. For stampede prevention, the key distinction is that Cluster avoids a single point of failure entirely; Sentinel just shortens the recovery window.

What NOT to say in an interview: "we would just restart Redis." This shows no understanding of distributed failure, replication lag, or the blast radius of a cache going dark. What TO say: "We have a circuit breaker that opens when Redis connection timeout rate exceeds a threshold, routing traffic to the database with rate limiting to protect it. Simultaneously, Redis Sentinel automatically promotes a replica. Once Redis is healthy and the circuit half-opens, we verify Redis connectivity before gradually closing the circuit and resuming normal caching."

XFetch + Redis Mutex Implementation

JavaScript Example 
// cache-stampede-prevention.js
// Requires: ioredis  (npm install ioredis)

import Redis from 'ioredis';

const redis = new Redis({ host: 'localhost', port: 6379 });

// ── XFetch: coordination-free probabilistic early expiry ──────────────────

/**
 * Read-through cache using the XFetch algorithm (VLDB 2015).
 * Triggers an async background refresh as the TTL approaches.
 *
 * @param {string}   key       Redis key
 * @param {Function} fetchFn   Async function that fetches fresh data from the DB
 * @param {object}   opts
 * @param {number}   opts.ttl  TTL in seconds (with jitter applied automatically)
 * @param {number}   opts.beta XFetch tuning param (default 1.0; higher = earlier refresh)
 */
export async function xfetchGet(key, fetchFn, { ttl = 300, beta = 1.0 } = {}) {
  const raw = await redis.get(key);

  if (raw) {
    const cached = JSON.parse(raw);
    const { value, delta, expiry } = cached;
    const now = Date.now() / 1000; // UNIX seconds

    // XFetch condition: should we refresh early?
    const shouldRefresh =
      now - delta * beta * Math.log(Math.random()) >= expiry;

    if (shouldRefresh) {
      // Fire-and-forget: refresh in background, return current value immediately
      refreshInBackground(key, fetchFn, ttl).catch(console.error);
    }

    return value;
  }

  // True cache miss — fetch synchronously and populate
  return await refreshInBackground(key, fetchFn, ttl);
}

async function refreshInBackground(key, fetchFn, ttl) {
  const start = Date.now();
  const value = await fetchFn();
  const delta = (Date.now() - start) / 1000; // seconds

  // Add jitter: ±10% of TTL
  const jitter = Math.floor(Math.random() * ttl * 0.1);
  const actualTtl = ttl + jitter;
  const expiry = Date.now() / 1000 + actualTtl;

  const payload = JSON.stringify({ value, delta, expiry });
  await redis.set(key, payload, 'EX', actualTtl);
  return value;
}

// ── Mutex: distributed lock for cases where stale data is not acceptable ──

/**
 * Read-through cache using a Redis SETNX distributed lock.
 * Non-lock-holders serve stale data if available, or wait.
 *
 * @param {string}   key     Redis key
 * @param {Function} fetchFn Async function that fetches fresh data
 * @param {number}   ttl     TTL in seconds
 */
export async function mutexGet(key, fetchFn, ttl = 300) {
  const value = await redis.get(key);
  if (value) return JSON.parse(value);

  const lockKey = `lock:${key}`;
  // Acquire lock — NX ensures only one caller succeeds
  // EX 30 — lock auto-expires in 30s if holder crashes
  const acquired = await redis.set(lockKey, '1', 'EX', 30, 'NX');

  if (acquired === 'OK') {
    try {
      const fresh = await fetchFn();
      const jitter = Math.floor(Math.random() * 30);
      await redis.set(key, JSON.stringify(fresh), 'EX', ttl + jitter);
      return fresh;
    } finally {
      await redis.del(lockKey); // Always release lock
    }
  } else {
    // Did not get lock — poll until cache is populated (max 3s)
    for (let i = 0; i < 30; i++) {
      await new Promise(r => setTimeout(r, 100));
      const retry = await redis.get(key);
      if (retry) return JSON.parse(retry);
    }
    // Fallback: fetch directly (avoid deadlock if lock holder crashed)
    return fetchFn();
  }
}

// ── Circuit Breaker stub ──────────────────────────────────────────────────

let circuitOpen = false;
let failureCount = 0;
const FAILURE_THRESHOLD = 5;

export async function withCircuitBreaker(fn) {
  if (circuitOpen) throw new Error('Circuit open — DB unavailable');
  try {
    const result = await fn();
    failureCount = 0; // Reset on success
    return result;
  } catch (err) {
    failureCount++;
    if (failureCount >= FAILURE_THRESHOLD) {
      circuitOpen = true;
      // Auto-reset after 30 seconds (half-open probe)
      setTimeout(() => { circuitOpen = false; failureCount = 0; }, 30_000);
    }
    throw err;
  }
}
Checklist

Practical Checklist: Validate Before a Flash Sale

  • Never use round-number or fixed TTLs. Add jitter (at least 10-15% of the base TTL) on every write, including cache warm-up scripts. A warm-up script that writes 500,000 keys in 30 minutes will make them expire in the same 30-minute window one TTL later, which reproduces the production outage above.
  • Scan Redis for synchronized TTLs. Run redis-cli --scan --pattern '*' | xargs -L 1 redis-cli TTL and check the distribution. Thousands of keys with the same remaining TTL are a synchronized expiry bomb; jitter them with a read-and-rewrite pass.
  • Store the computation delta alongside every cached value so XFetch has accurate timing data, and use XFetch for all cache reads in read-through caches as a zero-cost drop-in for the miss-fetch-store loop.
  • Use background refresh for known hot keys: homepage, top-N product listings, app configuration, feature flags.
  • Keep L1 caches short and bounded. L1 TTL of 5-30 seconds with a fixed maximum item count; measure heap overhead under peak load first.
  • Test single-flight under synthetic load. Hammer one cold key with 10,000 concurrent requests against an empty Redis and count database queries. The answer should be 1 (or 1 per app instance).
  • Set the application DB connection pool below the database maximum (60-70%) so a miss storm does not exhaust every connection and create a slow-query feedback loop.
  • Put a circuit breaker on every database read path and test it. Simulate Redis failure, confirm fallback to the DB is rate limited (not a 10,000-QPS blast), and confirm the breaker half-opens and closes correctly.
  • Set Redis maxmemory-policy to allkeys-lru to prevent out-of-memory crashes; volatile-lru only evicts keys with TTLs set.
  • Monitor cache hit rate per tier (cache.l1.hit, cache.l2.hit, cache.l3.query). A sudden drop from 99% to 60% is a stampede in progress, minutes before the DB alarm fires. Also alert on DB query rate spikes and pool utilisation.
  • Load test with a cold cache, not a warm one. Flush Redis at the start of the ramp, measure time to target hit rate, and verify the DB stays under 70% connection pool utilisation during the cold-start window.
Production Gotchas

Failure Modes and Common Mistakes

  • Jitter too small for the key population. Adding ±30 seconds of jitter to 1 million keys sounds reasonable — but if those keys were written in a 30-second window (fast warm-up script), they expire over a 60-second window (30 original spread + 30 jitter). At 1M keys / 60s, that is still 16,667 expirations per second. For large key populations, calculate the required jitter window: target <100 expirations/second means you need at least key_count / 100 seconds of jitter window.
  • L1 cache causing memory pressure under load. An unbounded or large-bounded L1 cache can cause application server OOMs during traffic spikes. Each cache entry holds the deserialized object in heap. Under a traffic spike where many cold keys are fetched and written to L1, heap usage climbs rapidly. Set a hard item count maximum (e.g., 1000 items) and validate the p99 heap size under load test before enabling L1 in production.
  • Single-flight leaking errors to all waiters. If the one in-flight database query returns an error, the single-flight implementation shares that error with all 9,999 waiting callers simultaneously. Depending on your error handling, this can cause a large burst of error-path logic (retries, alerts, logging) to fire simultaneously — a different kind of stampede. Test your error paths explicitly.
  • Cache invalidation pub/sub message ordering. If a product is updated twice in rapid succession (price correction immediately followed by another update), there is a risk of the invalidation messages being processed out of order on some subscribers. If your L1 invalidation handler re-fetches from Redis on invalidation (rather than just deleting), it may fetch and cache the first version after the second update has already been written. Design invalidation as delete-only, not update-on-invalidation.
  • Forgetting to jitter your background refresh scheduler. A scheduled job that refreshes 100,000 hot keys every 5 minutes, if it runs at exactly 00:00, 05:00, 10:00, will write 100,000 keys with the same actual write timestamp. Even if you apply jitter at write time, the batch is still completed within a short window — and all 100,000 keys expire within that same jitter window. Add a sleep between batches within the job, or offset the job's start time by a random amount each run.
FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Is XFetch safe to use with distributed caches across multiple app servers?

Yes — the algorithm is specifically designed for distributed environments. Each server independently runs the probabilistic check without any coordination. Because the probability of triggering an early refresh increases as the expiry approaches, the expected total number of early refreshes across all servers before natural expiry is exactly one (when beta = 1). Once any server successfully writes a refreshed value, all other servers see a fresh key with a distant expiry, and the XFetch condition immediately becomes false for all of them. The algorithm is self-correcting.

What beta value should I use in XFetch?

The VLDB 2015 paper proves that beta = 1 is the optimal default — it produces exactly one early refresh before expiry in expectation. Increase beta (e.g., 2–3) for very expensive database queries where you want to begin refreshing much earlier, accepting the cost of slightly more refreshes in exchange for more buffer time. Decrease beta (e.g., 0.5) for cheap, fast queries where a slight stampede is acceptable and you want to avoid unnecessary DB calls. In practice, start with beta = 1 and tune based on your observed delta values.

Does TTL jitter help with a single hot key?

No. Jitter only helps when many different keys are at risk of expiring simultaneously — for example, after a cache flush or a bulk population event. For a single hot key with hundreds of thousands of concurrent readers, jitter has no effect: the key still has one expiry moment, and all readers miss simultaneously when that moment arrives. For single hot keys, you need XFetch or mutex locking.

How do I handle cache stampedes in a CDN?

Most major CDNs have native support for stale-while-revalidate, which is conceptually equivalent to background refresh. Set your Cache-Control header to max-age=3600, stale-while-revalidate=60. This tells the CDN edge node to serve the cached (potentially stale) content immediately while triggering a background revalidation request to the origin. Cloudflare, Fastly, and AWS CloudFront all support this directive. For aggressive cache stampede prevention at the CDN layer, also look at request coalescing (or "request collapsing") — a feature where the CDN sends only one origin request for a cache miss, even if thousands of user requests arrive simultaneously.

Takeaways

Key Takeaways

Cache stampedes are not an edge case. They are a systemic risk that grows with the popularity of your content and the size of your user base. Both stories trace back to a single TTL decision: a round expiry aligned with a traffic spike (one hot key), and TTL = 3600 applied uniformly to 1.4 million keys (mass expiry). Every expiry under high concurrency is a latent stampede.

Single hot-key stampedes and mass TTL expiry are distinct problems. Jitter (additive, multiplicative, or full) solves mass expiry at the population level, and it is two lines of code. XFetch solves the individual hot-key problem without coordination, locks, or shared state, adapting to each computation's cost through delta. Single-flight handles the per-process concurrent miss storm. They are complementary, and a production caching layer deploys them together.

Multi-level caching (L1 in-process, L2 Redis, L3 database) is the right architecture for extreme scale but adds consistency complexity. Use event-driven invalidation from L3 outward, pub/sub for L1, and a short L1 TTL as the guaranteed-consistency backstop. For production, layer jitter, background refresh for known hot keys, XFetch for unknown ones, single-flight on the DB fallback, a circuit breaker, and Redis HA.

Instrumentation is the most important operational practice: per-tier hit rate is a leading indicator that appears minutes before database alarms. In an interview, naming XFetch (formula, beta, coordination-free property) and distinguishing hot-key stampede from mass expiry will differentiate you, but the real signal of seniority is treating stampede prevention as a layered posture designed in from the start.
