How to Solve a Rubik's Cube Without Memorizing Algorithms: Even a Kid Can Understand

Most Rubik's Cube tutorials give you a list of algorithms to memorize — sequences like R U R' U' that you repeat without understanding why they work. This guide teaches the cube layer by layer using only one concept you need to grasp: how to move a piece to where you want it without disturbing pieces you have already placed. No rote memorization required. If you understand the goal of each step, you can rediscover the moves yourself.

Domain: Learning · Problem Solving · Logical Thinking Published: 2026-08-05

The Rubik's Cube looks impossibly complex — 43 quintillion possible combinations — but it has exactly one core insight: you solve it layer by layer, and every move in each layer either places a piece or sets up the next placement without ruining what you have already done. You do not need to see 20 steps ahead. You need to understand what you are trying to do right now, and you can figure out the how.

Core idea: Every "algorithm" in Rubik's Cube solving is just a way to move one piece to where it needs to go while temporarily moving, then restoring, the pieces in its way. Understanding this one concept makes every step in this guide obvious rather than arbitrary.

Story · The Puzzle That Stumped Mathematicians

Why 43 Quintillion Possibilities Can Be Solved in Under 20 Moves

Ernő Rubik invented the cube in 1974. He originally called it the Magic Cube and used it to demonstrate 3D spatial movement to his architecture students. It took him a full month to solve his own invention. In 1980 it was renamed the Rubik's Cube and became the best-selling toy in history.

Mathematicians spent 30 years trying to prove the minimum number of moves needed to solve any possible scramble. In 2010, using 35 CPU-years of Google computing time, they proved that any scramble can be solved in at most 20 moves. This is called "God's Number" — because an omniscient solver would never need more than 20. A beginner using the layer-by-layer method takes around 100–150 moves. A world-record speedcuber (sub-4-second solves) takes an average of 55–65 moves using advanced algorithms. But the beginner's method gets the same result — a solved cube — with just logic and patience.

You do not need 20 moves. You need to understand the structure, and the rest follows.

Foundation

Understanding the Cube Before Touching It

The Three Types of Pieces

A standard 3×3 Rubik's Cube has 26 visible pieces of three types. Understanding which type a piece is determines where it belongs and how to move it:

  • Center pieces (6 total) — The single-color piece in the middle of each face. Centers do not move relative to each other when you turn the cube. The white center is always opposite the yellow center. The red center is always opposite the orange center. Centers define what color each face should be when solved. This is the most important fact about the cube.
  • Edge pieces (12 total) — The pieces with two colored stickers on edges between two faces. Each edge belongs in one specific position.
  • Corner pieces (8 total) — The pieces with three colored stickers on corners where three faces meet. Each corner belongs in one specific position and must also be correctly rotated.

Why centers matter: When you look at a scrambled cube and see the white center, that is the white face. The white face will be assembled around that center. You are never moving the centers — you are moving all other pieces to surround them correctly.

Notation: The Six Moves

Hold the cube so one face is toward you. The six basic moves are named by which face turns, and whether it turns clockwise or counterclockwise (marked with '). Clockwise = when you look directly at that face.

RRight face clockwise
R'Right face counter-CW
LLeft face clockwise
L'Left face counter-CW
UTop face clockwise
U'Top face counter-CW
DBottom face clockwise
D'Bottom face counter-CW
FFront face clockwise
F'Front face counter-CW
BBack face clockwise
B'Back face counter-CW

The key insight about these moves: any sequence of moves can be fully reversed by doing those same moves in reverse order with the directions flipped. R U R' U' reversed is U R U' R'. This is why every "algorithm" can be undone — and it is what lets you "set aside" solved pieces while you work on others.

Step 1: White Cross

Goal: place all 4 white edges correctly around the white center.

Hold the cube with white on top. Find the four white edge pieces (white-red, white-blue, white-orange, white-green). Each needs to be placed between the white center and the matching color center.

The intuition: White edges can be in any of 24 positions. For each one, ask yourself: where is it, and how do I get it to the top without destroying what I've already placed?

  • If the white edge is on the bottom layer: rotate the bottom (D) until the edge is directly below where it needs to go on the top layer. Then do: F F (turn that edge's face twice) to bring it to the top. Two turns of the same face is called a "double turn" and it only moves pieces in that face's layer — the rest of the cube is untouched.
  • If the white edge is in the middle layer sideways: it is a bit harder to visualize. Position it so the white sticker faces you (front face) and the other color sticker faces left. Then: F' U' L' U moves it to the top. Do not memorize this — understand it: you rotate front counterclockwise (pulling the piece to the top-left), fix with U', then L' puts it correctly, then U restores the top.

Success check: White cross formed on top, and each white edge's second color matches the center of the adjacent side face.

Step 2: White Corners — Complete the White Face

Goal: place all 4 white corners to complete the white face and the first layer.

Find a white corner piece (it has three colors — white + two side colors). It belongs in the corner where those two side colors' centers meet the white center.

The one move you need: R U R' U' — this is sometimes called the "sexy move." Here is what it does in plain English: turn the right face clockwise (R), turn the top counterclockwise (U), turn the right face back counterclockwise (R'), turn the top clockwise (U'). This swaps a corner from the bottom-right-front position into the top-right-front corner. That is it — one effect, one use.

  • If the white corner is in the bottom layer: rotate the bottom (D) to place the corner directly below where it belongs on top. Then look at which way the white sticker is facing and apply R U R' U' 1–3 times until it slots in correctly. Yes, you might need to apply the same 4-move sequence multiple times. That is fine.
  • If a white corner is already in the top but twisted wrong: use R U R' U' once to pop it to the bottom, rotate the bottom to re-position it, then apply again.

Success check: Full white face solved. Flip the cube — white face on bottom. The first layer should show one complete row of the matching color on each side face.

Step 3: Middle Layer Edges

Goal: place the 4 middle layer edges (none of which contain yellow).

Flip the cube so white is on the bottom. Now you need to place the four edges in the middle layer — the edges between the four side faces (red-blue, blue-orange, orange-green, green-red). None of these pieces have yellow on them.

For each middle edge, find it in the top layer. Rotate the top (U) until the side sticker of the edge matches the center below it. Now the edge needs to go either left or right into the middle layer. Two cases:

  • Going right: U R U' R' U' F' U F
  • Going left: U' L' U L U F U' F'

Do not memorize these as strings. Read them: for "going right," you move the top away (U), insert with R, restore the top (U'), undo R (R') — that is the core. The second 4 moves fix the piece you disturbed. Every middle layer algorithm follows this "disturb, insert, restore" structure.

If a middle edge is already placed but wrong direction: apply either algorithm to replace it with a top-layer edge — this pops the wrong edge out, then you can re-insert it correctly.

Step 4: Yellow Cross on Top

Goal: form a yellow cross on the top face (yellow center + 4 yellow edges).

You may see one of four patterns on the top: a dot (only yellow center), an L-shape (2 yellow edge stickers up), a line (2 yellow edge stickers up, across center), or a cross (all 4 yellow edges up — you are done with this step).

The one algorithm: F R U R' U' F'

Apply it once and see which pattern you now have. If you started with a dot, applying once gives an L. Applying from an L gives a line. Applying from a line gives a cross. You will need to apply 1–3 times. Position the top layer between applications so the existing yellow edges are where the algorithm expects them (L: bottom-left corner; Line: horizontal across you).

Step 5: Orient Yellow Edges — Fix the Cross Colors

Goal: rotate the top so each yellow edge's side sticker matches its center.

The yellow cross is formed, but the four cross edges may not be aligned with the side centers. Rotate the top layer (U) and count how many yellow edges are already aligned (side sticker matches the side center).

  • 0 or 2 facing you: Use the algorithm once, then check again.
  • 2 opposite each other: Hold with one matching edge at the back. Apply R U R' U R U U R'
  • 2 adjacent (L-shape): Hold with both matching edges at back-right. Apply same algorithm.

Algorithm: R U R' U R U U R' — read it as: three mini-sequences of (R U R'), with the last R followed by two U moves. The pattern helps you remember it.

Step 6: Position Yellow Corners

Goal: move yellow corners to their correct positions (ignore orientation for now).

Look at the top layer corners. You need each corner to be in the right position — the three colors of the corner must match the three faces it touches — even if it is twisted.

Find a corner that is already in the right position (all 3 colors match the adjacent centers, even if they face the wrong direction). Hold it at the front-right position.

Apply: U R U' L' U R' U' L — this cycles the other three corners counterclockwise without moving the front-right corner. Apply until all four corners are in their correct positions. If no corner is already correct, apply the algorithm once — one corner will become correct.

Step 7: Orient Yellow Corners — The Final Step

Goal: twist each corner so yellow faces up. The cube solves.

All pieces are in the right positions. The only remaining issue is that some top corners have their yellow sticker facing sideways instead of up.

This step looks alarming — it will appear to destroy the cube while you do it. It does not. Trust the process.

  1. Hold the cube with the yellow face on top.
  2. Find a corner whose yellow sticker is not facing up (facing front or right instead).
  3. Position that corner at the front-right position.
  4. Apply R' D' R D repeatedly — 2 or 4 times — until the yellow sticker on that corner faces up. The rest of the cube will look scrambled. That is expected.
  5. Without rotating the top layer, move the next incorrectly-oriented corner to the front-right position using only U or U' to turn the top layer.
  6. Apply R' D' R D again 2 or 4 times until it is correct.
  7. Repeat for all remaining corners. After the last corner is fixed, the entire cube solves automatically.

Why it works: R' D' R D cycles the orientation of the front-right-top corner through three positions. 2 applications = rotate clockwise. 4 applications = rotate counterclockwise. The key rule: never rotate the whole cube or the top layer while performing this sequence on a single corner. Finish each corner completely before moving to the next.

FAQ

Common Questions

Do I need to do all 7 steps in order every time?

Yes. The layer-by-layer method depends on not disturbing lower layers while solving upper ones. Steps 1–2 build the first layer; steps 3 builds the second; steps 4–7 build the third. Always in order.

What if I get stuck and the cube seems impossible?

If you followed all steps and the last 1–2 pieces seem unsolvable, the cube may have been physically taken apart and reassembled incorrectly. A Rubik's Cube assembled wrong cannot be solved algorithmically — about 11 out of 12 possible random assemblies are unsolvable. Take it apart and reassemble it from a solved state.

How do I get faster?

Finger tricks (flicking moves with one finger rather than full-hand grips), look-ahead (planning the next step while executing the current one), and practicing F2L (First Two Layers combined) intuitively instead of step by step. Most people who practice 15 minutes daily can reach a 2-minute solve within a month of learning.

What comes after the beginner method?

CFOP (Cross, F2L, OLL, PLL) is the method used by virtually all top speedcubers. It solves the cross + first two layers in one phase (F2L), then the top in two passes. CFOP requires memorizing 78 algorithms for OLL and 21 for PLL — but the intuition from this guide makes learning them much easier because you understand what each algorithm is doing.

Key Takeaway

Solving a Rubik's Cube is not about memorizing a script — it is about understanding one recursive idea: to place a piece where you want it, temporarily move the pieces in its way, insert it, then restore what you moved. Every "algorithm" in this guide is just that idea applied to a specific position. Once you solve the cube with this understanding three or four times, you will stop needing the instructions — because you will know why each step works, not just what keys to press. That understanding is the difference between following a recipe and being able to cook.
