How Netflix Handles 260 Million Concurrent Streams Without Buffering

Netflix serves 260M+ subscribers across 200+ countries, accounting for 37%+ of peak US internet traffic. The secret is not a single clever trick — it is a layered architecture of purpose-built CDN appliances, parallel encoding pipelines, adaptive bitrate algorithms, and a microservices backend on AWS that can evacuate an entire region in 6 minutes.

Source: Netflix Tech Blog · High Scalability Domain: Streaming / CDN / System Design Published: 2026-07-16

Netflix is the internet's single largest consumer of bandwidth. At peak US evening hours, its traffic represents more than 37% of all downstream internet traffic in the country. Serving that load — without buffering, across 200+ countries and 2,200+ device types — requires every layer of the stack to be purpose-engineered. This guide walks through every piece: encoding, CDN hardware, adaptive bitrate, the AWS backend, and the chaos engineering that validates it all.

Three-layer model: Netflix infrastructure has exactly three tiers: the Client SDK (on your device), the AWS Backend (microservices, databases, encoding pipeline), and Open Connect (the custom CDN). Each tier has a distinct failure mode and a distinct scaling strategy.

Story · Stranger Things Season 1 launch

July 2016. Stranger Things Season 1 drops on Netflix. Within the first weekend, 20 million streams are active simultaneously across the globe. Behind the scenes, that launch required encoding 9,570 output files — one for every combination of video quality, audio track, subtitle language, and device profile — consuming 190,000 CPU hours just for a single season of one show.

Every one of those files had to be distributed to the right Open Connect Appliance (OCA) nodes around the world before the launch date. A single missed distribution to a major ISP would mean buffering for millions of users. Netflix's engineering solved this with a nightly proactive caching algorithm that predicts demand and pre-positions content — so that by the time any user presses play, the data is already sitting in a server inside their ISP's datacenter.

That single use case — 9,570 files, 190,000 CPU hours, zero buffering on launch night — captures the engineering ambition behind the entire Netflix infrastructure stack.

Architecture Overview

The Three-Layer Model: Client, AWS Backend, Open Connect CDN

Netflix's architecture separates cleanly into three layers, each with a distinct function:

  • Client SDK: Runs on every device (Smart TV, phone, browser, game console). Responsible for adaptive bitrate selection, OCA health probing, license acquisition, and the playback state machine. The client is not thin — Netflix engineers call it "the most sophisticated video player in the world."
  • AWS Backend: Runs in three AWS regions (N. Virginia, Portland OR, Dublin). Handles everything non-video: user authentication, content metadata, recommendations, billing, A/B testing, and the control plane for playback (which OCAs to use, which license server, which manifest URL). Also runs the encoding pipeline — 300,000 CPUs encode content in parallel.
  • Open Connect CDN: Netflix's private CDN. Over 1,000 OCA locations embedded directly inside ISP datacenters worldwide. Handles 100% of actual video byte delivery. No third-party CDN is in the critical path.

The key insight of this architecture is the separation of control plane (AWS) from data plane (Open Connect). When you press play, your device contacts AWS to get a list of OCA URLs. Then it streams video bytes directly from the OCA — AWS never touches the video data.

Why build a private CDN? By 2012, Netflix accounted for so much internet traffic that it made economic sense to offer ISPs free OCA hardware in exchange for datacenter colocation. ISPs save money on peering costs (traffic stays inside their network rather than crossing expensive interconnects). Netflix gets faster delivery and lower CDN costs. Both sides win.

Encoding Pipeline

Content Ingestion: From Studio Master to 9,570 Output Files

When a studio delivers a show to Netflix, it arrives as a multi-terabyte high-resolution master file. Before a single user can watch a second of it, Netflix runs it through an encoding pipeline with 70+ processing components.

The pipeline is heavily parallelized. The source video is first split into short chunks (typically a few seconds each). Each chunk is encoded independently across thousands of machines simultaneously. This is why 300,000 CPUs can work on a single season at once — they are each processing a different chunk at a different quality level.

The output of this pipeline for a single season includes:

  • Video tracks at multiple resolutions (240p, 360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p, 4K HDR)
  • Multiple codec versions (H.264 for legacy devices, H.265/HEVC for 4K, AV1 for modern clients)
  • Audio tracks for every language dub
  • Subtitle tracks for every language
  • Variants optimized for 2,200 different device profiles

The 9,570 files for Stranger Things S2 and the 190,000 CPU hours represent a real production number from Netflix engineering blog posts. Each combination of (codec × resolution × audio track × subtitle × device profile) is a distinct output file. Every file must be validated before distribution begins.

Codec Strategy

Video Encoding Deep Dive: H.264, HEVC, AV1, and Per-Title Encoding

Netflix uses three video codecs simultaneously, each targeting a different device tier and bandwidth profile:

  • H.264 (AVC): The legacy codec. Supported by essentially every device ever made. Netflix uses it for older smart TVs, mobile devices with limited CPU, and anywhere hardware decoding is not available for newer codecs. Higher file sizes than alternatives.
  • H.265 (HEVC): Required for 4K HDR content. Approximately 40% smaller file sizes than H.264 at equivalent quality. Hardware decoding is available on most modern devices. Licensed codec with per-device royalty costs.
  • AV1: Open source, royalty-free codec developed by the Alliance for Open Media (Google, Netflix, Mozilla, Amazon, Intel, ARM). Approximately 20% more efficient than HEVC — meaning the same visual quality at 20% lower bitrate. Netflix began rolling out AV1 for mobile streaming in 2020. AV1 encoding is compute-intensive (can take 20x longer than H.264) but the streaming savings are significant at Netflix scale.

Beyond codec choice, Netflix pioneered per-title encoding. The traditional approach was a fixed bitrate ladder — everyone gets the same 240p/360p/480p/720p/1080p quality levels regardless of content. Netflix discovered that this wastes bandwidth for complex content and wastes bits on simple content.

A static animated show (low spatial complexity, minimal scene changes) can achieve excellent quality at much lower bitrates than live-action with rapid camera movement. Per-title encoding analyzes each title independently and builds a custom bitrate ladder for it. A simple animated show might look great at 1 Mbps. A dark, high-action thriller might need 4 Mbps to look equivalent. Per-title encoding reduced Netflix's average streaming bitrate by ~20% without any perceptible quality loss.

Per-shot encoding (Shot-Constrained Encoding): Netflix went further in 2018, encoding not just per-title but per-scene. A feature film might have quiet dialogue scenes (encodable at low bitrate) and explosive action sequences (requiring much higher bitrate). The encoder now adjusts dynamically within a single title.

CDN Infrastructure

Open Connect: The ISP-Embedded CDN with 1,000+ Locations

Open Connect is Netflix's private CDN, and it is one of the most architecturally unusual CDN designs in existence. Most companies use shared CDN providers (Akamai, Cloudflare, Fastly). Netflix built its own hardware and physically installs it inside ISP network infrastructure.

OCA hardware: Each Open Connect Appliance is a commodity PC server running FreeBSD and NGINX as the HTTP server. They come in two hardware profiles:

  • Large OCAs (Flash-based): Flash storage arrays with 100+ TB of NVMe SSD capacity. Deployed at Tier-1 ISPs and large exchanges. Serve the full Netflix catalog. Very high IOPS, low latency for popular content.
  • Small OCAs (HDD-based): Traditional hard drive arrays at smaller ISPs. Cannot hold the entire catalog — instead they cache a predicted subset of content likely to be requested by that ISP's subscriber base in the near future.

The nightly proactive fill algorithm: This is the key design insight. Netflix does not wait for a cache miss to populate an OCA (unlike a traditional pull CDN). Instead, every night, Netflix's demand prediction system queries AWS to identify which content is likely to be popular in the next 24-48 hours for each ISP's subscriber base. It then proactively copies that content from nearby OCAs (or from AWS origin if needed) to local OCAs, completing the fill before demand peaks.

The goal is that by the time any user presses play on a title, the content is already on an OCA inside their ISP's datacenter — a cache miss should never happen for popular content. Netflix has reported that up to 100% of Netflix video traffic can be served from within ISP networks via OCAs, meaning zero bytes cross expensive peering connections.

Why ISPs accept free hardware: Netflix traffic accounts for a massive fraction of peak internet bandwidth. Without OCAs, that traffic crosses expensive peering interconnects between ISPs. When an OCA is installed inside an ISP's network, that traffic stays internal — saving the ISP real money on transit costs. ISPs get free hardware and reduced costs; Netflix gets faster delivery and zero CDN bills. The arrangement works because Netflix's scale makes it economically rational.

By 2013, Netflix's catalog was already over 3 petabytes. Today it is substantially larger. Distributing that data globally and keeping it fresh on OCAs is itself a significant distributed systems challenge — essentially a planet-scale file synchronization system.

Adaptive Streaming

Adaptive Bitrate Streaming: How the Client Decides Quality

Adaptive Bitrate Streaming (ABR) is the mechanism that prevents buffering when your network degrades. Netflix uses DASH (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP) and HLS (HTTP Live Streaming) depending on the device and platform.

At its core, ABR works as follows: the video is pre-encoded at multiple quality levels (the bitrate ladder from the encoding pipeline). The client downloads short segments (typically 4-10 seconds each) one at a time, and before each download, it estimates available bandwidth and selects the highest quality level that can be downloaded faster than it is played back.

The Netflix ABR algorithm is more sophisticated than a simple bandwidth check:

  • Throughput estimation: The client maintains a running estimate of download throughput using a windowed average of recent chunk download speeds, with exponential decay to weight recent observations more heavily.
  • Buffer health: The client tracks how much video is buffered ahead. If the buffer is large (say, 30+ seconds), the algorithm is more aggressive about upgrading quality. If the buffer is thin, it downgrades quality to protect playback continuity.
  • OCA switching: If a specific OCA is delivering poor performance (high latency, low throughput, packet loss), the client can switch to a different OCA URL from the list returned by the AWS playback service. The client continuously probes all returned OCA URLs and routes chunks to the best-performing one.
  • Quality descent before OCA switch: The algorithm degrades video quality before deciding to switch OCA servers, because a quality downgrade is transparent to the user whereas switching OCAs mid-stream can cause a brief stall.

The net result is that most users never see the buffering spinner — the quality silently drops from 1080p to 720p to 480p during a congested moment, then silently climbs back when conditions improve.

AWS Backend

The AWS Backend: Three Regions, Seven Pillars

Netflix's non-video backend runs entirely on AWS, spread across three geographic regions: US East (N. Virginia), US West (Portland, OR), and EU West (Dublin, Ireland). Each region runs three Availability Zones. This gives Netflix 9 independent failure domains, and the system is designed to survive the complete loss of any single region.

The seven key components of the AWS backend:

  1. Zuul (API Gateway): The edge proxy. All client API calls enter through Zuul. Handles authentication, rate limiting, A/B test routing, and canary deployments. Zuul is open-source and maintained by Netflix.
  2. Eureka (Service Discovery): The service registry. Every microservice registers itself with Eureka on startup. Other services discover it by name rather than hardcoded IP. Eureka is designed to prefer availability over consistency — it will serve stale registrations rather than return errors during partition events.
  3. Hystrix (Circuit Breaker): Wraps every inter-service call. If a downstream service begins failing or slowing, Hystrix opens the circuit and executes a fallback (return cached data, return a default, skip the call entirely). This prevents cascading failures from propagating across the microservices graph. Hystrix is now in maintenance mode; Resilience4j is the modern replacement.
  4. Cassandra: Distributed wide-column store used for viewing history, user profiles, and other large-scale persistent data. Netflix is one of Cassandra's largest production users and has contributed significantly to its development. Cross-region replication ensures data is available even during a region failure.
  5. DynamoDB: Used for session state and other low-latency key-value lookups.
  6. Kafka: The event backbone. Every user action (play, pause, seek, rate, search) generates events that flow through Kafka to analytics pipelines, recommendation engines, and A/B test tracking systems.
  7. Mantis: Netflix's internal stream processing platform (similar to Apache Flink). Used for real-time analytics, anomaly detection, and operational intelligence. Mantis queries can be pushed to running jobs without redeployment.

Region evacuation is a tested capability: Netflix can route 100% of traffic away from a failing AWS region within approximately 6 minutes using pre-configured DNS failover and auto-scaling in the remaining regions.

Resilience Engineering

Chaos Engineering: The Simian Army

Netflix's most famous infrastructure contribution is the concept of Chaos Engineering — deliberately injecting failures into production systems to verify they can survive them. This emerged from the insight that you cannot rely on testing in staging environments, because staging never perfectly mirrors production load patterns and failure modes.

The Simian Army consists of several tools:

  • Chaos Monkey: Randomly terminates EC2 instances in production during business hours. Forces teams to build services that survive random instance death without impacting users. Created in 2011 and open-sourced.
  • Chaos Gorilla: Simulates the failure of an entire AWS Availability Zone. Tests that multi-AZ architecture actually works at traffic scale.
  • Chaos Kong: Simulates the failure of an entire AWS region. Netflix runs monthly forced region-failure tests using Chaos Kong. The 6-minute region evacuation time is validated by these tests.
  • Latency Monkey: Injects artificial delays into service calls to test that timeouts and fallbacks are correctly configured.
  • Conformity Monkey: Checks that instances conform to best practices (correct security groups, proper tagging, right instance types) and terminates non-conforming instances.
  • Security Monkey: Monitors for security policy violations and alerts or disables violating configurations.

The underlying principle is not just "systems must tolerate failures" — it is "systems must survive failures without human intervention." Chaos Engineering validates that the auto-recovery mechanisms actually work before a real failure occurs at 3 AM on a Sunday.

The key insight: Any complex system will fail. The question is whether you discover those failures during a controlled chaos experiment during business hours, or during an uncontrolled production incident at 2 AM. Chaos Engineering shifts the discovery to the former.

Microservices Architecture

700+ Microservices, Spinnaker, and Titus

Netflix operates over 700 microservices. Each service is owned by a small team that controls its own deployment pipeline independently. This is the organizational scaling strategy: when you have hundreds of engineers, a single deploy pipeline becomes a bottleneck. Microservices let teams deploy independently at whatever cadence their service requires.

Three tools power Netflix's microservices operations:

  • Spinnaker: Open-source continuous delivery platform developed by Netflix in collaboration with Google. Handles canary deployments, blue-green deployments, automated rollbacks, and multi-cloud deployments. Every Netflix microservice deploys through Spinnaker.
  • Titus: Netflix's container management platform, built on top of Apache Mesos. Schedules and runs Docker containers at scale across their AWS fleet. Handles resource allocation, bin-packing, and failure recovery for containerized workloads.
  • Atlas: Netflix's time-series metrics system. Handles billions of metric data points per minute from all services. Powers the dashboards and alerting that keep 700+ services observable.

Interestingly, Netflix has gradually moved some workloads back toward larger services in recent years — finding that 700+ microservices introduces operational complexity that smaller teams struggle to manage. This is a recurring pattern in the industry: microservices are not free, and the right granularity depends on team size and organizational maturity.

End-to-End Flow

Press Play: The Complete Playback Flow

Every time a Netflix user presses play, the following sequence executes in under 1 second before a single video frame renders:

  1. Client sends play intent: The Netflix app on your device sends a play request to the Playback Apps service on AWS, authenticated via the user's session token through Zuul.
  2. License acquisition: For DRM-protected content (essentially everything), the client requests a Widevine/FairPlay/PlayReady license from Netflix's license server. This cryptographically binds the content to the device.
  3. OCA selection: The Playback Apps service queries the Open Connect steering service, which knows which OCAs are nearest to the user (based on BGP routing data and ISP identification). It returns up to 10 OCA URLs for the requested content.
  4. Client probes OCAs: The client sends test requests to all returned OCA URLs simultaneously to measure latency and throughput to each. This takes roughly 100-200ms.
  5. Best OCA wins: The client selects the OCA with the best performance characteristics and begins downloading the first video chunk at the highest quality level that its measured bandwidth can support.
  6. Continuous monitoring: Throughout playback, the client monitors chunk download times and adjusts quality level up or down. If the selected OCA degrades, the client can switch to another OCA from the original returned list.
  7. Playback events: Every quality change, buffer event, and chunk download is logged via Kafka to Netflix's data pipeline for analysis and recommendation improvement.

The client is not a dumb player: Netflix's client SDK contains sophisticated logic for OCA health scoring, bandwidth estimation, adaptive algorithm tuning, and DRM management. A significant amount of Netflix's engineering investment lives in the client — not just the backend.

JavaScript Example

Adaptive Bitrate Client Simulator

JavaScript Example 
/**
 * Simplified ABR client simulator — bandwidth estimation + quality selection + OCA health.
 * Demonstrates the core decision loop that runs inside every Netflix client.
 */

const QUALITY_LEVELS = [
  { name: '240p',  bitrate: 300_000  },   // 300 kbps
  { name: '480p',  bitrate: 1_000_000 },  // 1 Mbps
  { name: '720p',  bitrate: 3_000_000 },  // 3 Mbps
  { name: '1080p', bitrate: 8_000_000 },  // 8 Mbps
  { name: '4K',    bitrate: 16_000_000 }, // 16 Mbps
];

const SAFETY_FACTOR = 0.8;        // Only upgrade if bandwidth > 80% of next level's bitrate
const BUFFER_TARGET_SECONDS = 30; // Try to keep 30s buffered
const MIN_BUFFER_SECONDS = 5;     // If buffer < 5s, downgrade quality aggressively

class ABRClient {
  constructor(ocaUrls) {
    this.ocaUrls     = ocaUrls;
    this.ocaHealth   = Object.fromEntries(ocaUrls.map(u => [u, { score: 1.0, samples: [] }]));
    this.bwSamples   = []; // recent bandwidth measurements (bps)
    this.bufferLevel = 0;  // seconds of video buffered
    this.currentQualityIdx = 0;
  }

  /** Record a chunk download result to update bandwidth estimate */
  recordDownload(ocaUrl, bytes, durationMs) {
    const bps = (bytes * 8) / (durationMs / 1000);
    this.bwSamples.push(bps);
    if (this.bwSamples.length > 10) this.bwSamples.shift(); // sliding window

    // Update OCA health score (exponential moving average of throughput ratio)
    const health = this.ocaHealth[ocaUrl];
    const expectedBps = QUALITY_LEVELS[this.currentQualityIdx].bitrate;
    const ratio = Math.min(bps / expectedBps, 2.0);
    health.score = 0.7 * health.score + 0.3 * ratio;
    health.samples.push({ bps, timestamp: Date.now() });
  }

  /** Estimate available bandwidth using harmonic mean (less sensitive to spikes) */
  estimateBandwidth() {
    if (this.bwSamples.length === 0) return 1_000_000; // default 1 Mbps
    const harmonicMean = this.bwSamples.length /
      this.bwSamples.reduce((sum, x) => sum + 1 / x, 0);
    return harmonicMean;
  }

  /** Select quality level for next chunk */
  selectQuality() {
    const estimatedBw = this.estimateBandwidth();
    const bufferSafe  = this.bufferLevel >= MIN_BUFFER_SECONDS;

    // If buffer is critically low, drop to lowest quality immediately
    if (this.bufferLevel < MIN_BUFFER_SECONDS) {
      this.currentQualityIdx = 0;
      return QUALITY_LEVELS[0];
    }

    // Find the highest quality level we can sustain
    let targetIdx = 0;
    for (let i = QUALITY_LEVELS.length - 1; i >= 0; i--) {
      if (estimatedBw * SAFETY_FACTOR >= QUALITY_LEVELS[i].bitrate) {
        targetIdx = i;
        break;
      }
    }

    // Smooth transitions: only upgrade one level at a time, downgrade immediately
    if (targetIdx > this.currentQualityIdx && bufferSafe) {
      this.currentQualityIdx = Math.min(this.currentQualityIdx + 1, targetIdx);
    } else if (targetIdx < this.currentQualityIdx) {
      this.currentQualityIdx = targetIdx; // downgrade immediately
    }

    return QUALITY_LEVELS[this.currentQualityIdx];
  }

  /** Pick the best OCA for the next chunk request */
  selectOCA() {
    return this.ocaUrls.reduce((best, url) => {
      return this.ocaHealth[url].score > this.ocaHealth[best].score ? url : best;
    }, this.ocaUrls[0]);
  }

  /** Simulate fetching one chunk */
  async fetchNextChunk(chunkIndex) {
    const quality  = this.selectQuality();
    const oca      = this.selectOCA();
    const chunkUrl = `${oca}/chunk/${chunkIndex}/${quality.name}`;

    const start = Date.now();
    try {
      const response = await fetch(chunkUrl);
      const buffer   = await response.arrayBuffer();
      const elapsed  = Date.now() - start;

      this.recordDownload(oca, buffer.byteLength, elapsed);
      this.bufferLevel += 4; // each chunk = 4 seconds of video

      console.log(`Chunk ${chunkIndex}: ${quality.name} from ${oca} (${(buffer.byteLength/1024).toFixed(0)} KB in ${elapsed}ms)`);
      return { quality, oca, bytes: buffer.byteLength, ms: elapsed };
    } catch (err) {
      // OCA failed — penalize its health score
      this.ocaHealth[oca].score *= 0.5;
      console.warn(`OCA ${oca} failed for chunk ${chunkIndex}, retrying with next best`);
      throw err;
    }
  }
}

// Usage
const client = new ABRClient([
  'https://oca1.isp.example.com',
  'https://oca2.isp.example.com',
  'https://oca3.isp.example.com',
]);

// Simulate 5 chunks of playback
for (let i = 0; i < 5; i++) {
  const result = await client.fetchNextChunk(i);
  client.bufferLevel -= 4; // each second played consumes 1 second of buffer
}
JavaScript Example

Parallel Encoding Job Dispatcher with Worker Pool

JavaScript Example 
/**
 * Encoding job dispatcher — demonstrates how Netflix parallelizes encoding
 * across 300,000 CPUs by distributing independent chunk-encode jobs.
 * This simulates the coordinator that splits a video into chunks and
 * dispatches them to a worker pool.
 */

const CHUNK_DURATION_SECONDS = 6;
const QUALITY_PROFILES = ['240p', '360p', '480p', '720p', '1080p', '4K-HEVC', '4K-AV1'];
const MAX_CONCURRENT_JOBS = 50; // In reality, Netflix uses hundreds of thousands

class EncodingJobDispatcher {
  constructor(workerCount = MAX_CONCURRENT_JOBS) {
    this.workerCount  = workerCount;
    this.activeJobs   = new Map();  // jobId -> Promise
    this.completedJobs = [];
    this.failedJobs   = [];
  }

  /** Splits a video into chunks and returns a list of encode jobs */
  generateJobs(videoPath, totalDurationSeconds) {
    const jobs = [];
    const chunkCount = Math.ceil(totalDurationSeconds / CHUNK_DURATION_SECONDS);

    for (let chunk = 0; chunk < chunkCount; chunk++) {
      for (const profile of QUALITY_PROFILES) {
        jobs.push({
          id:        `${videoPath}:chunk${chunk}:${profile}`,
          videoPath,
          startSec:  chunk * CHUNK_DURATION_SECONDS,
          endSec:    Math.min((chunk + 1) * CHUNK_DURATION_SECONDS, totalDurationSeconds),
          profile,
          attempts:  0,
        });
      }
    }

    console.log(`Generated ${jobs.length} encoding jobs (${chunkCount} chunks × ${QUALITY_PROFILES.length} profiles)`);
    return jobs;
  }

  /** Encode a single chunk — in production, this dispatches to a remote worker */
  async encodeChunk(job) {
    job.attempts++;
    const duration = job.endSec - job.startSec;
    const complexityMs = Math.random() * 2000 + 500; // simulated encoding time

    await new Promise(resolve => setTimeout(resolve, complexityMs));

    // Simulate occasional failures (5% failure rate)
    if (Math.random() < 0.05) {
      throw new Error(`Encoder worker failure for ${job.id}`);
    }

    const outputSizeKB = duration * { '240p': 50, '360p': 100, '480p': 200, '720p': 500,
      '1080p': 1000, '4K-HEVC': 1800, '4K-AV1': 1500 }[job.profile];

    return {
      jobId:       job.id,
      outputPath:  `/encoded/${job.videoPath}/${job.profile}/chunk_${job.startSec}.mp4`,
      sizeKB:      outputSizeKB,
      encodedInMs: complexityMs,
    };
  }

  /** Process all jobs with a bounded worker pool and retry on failure */
  async dispatch(jobs, maxRetries = 3) {
    const queue  = [...jobs];
    const active = new Set();
    const results = [];

    const runNext = async () => {
      if (queue.length === 0) return;
      const job = queue.shift();

      const promise = (async () => {
        let lastError;
        for (let attempt = 0; attempt <= maxRetries; attempt++) {
          try {
            const result = await this.encodeChunk(job);
            this.completedJobs.push(result);
            results.push(result);
            return result;
          } catch (err) {
            lastError = err;
            if (attempt < maxRetries) {
              console.warn(`Retry ${attempt + 1}/${maxRetries} for ${job.id}: ${err.message}`);
              await new Promise(r => setTimeout(r, 100 * (attempt + 1))); // exponential backoff
            }
          }
        }
        console.error(`Job permanently failed: ${job.id} after ${maxRetries} retries`);
        this.failedJobs.push({ job, error: lastError });
      })();

      active.add(promise);
      promise.finally(() => {
        active.delete(promise);
        runNext(); // start next job when this one finishes
      });
    };

    // Start initial batch up to worker count
    const initialBatch = Math.min(this.workerCount, queue.length);
    for (let i = 0; i < initialBatch; i++) await runNext();

    // Wait for all remaining jobs
    while (active.size > 0) {
      await Promise.race(active);
    }

    return {
      completed: this.completedJobs.length,
      failed:    this.failedJobs.length,
      results,
    };
  }
}

// Encode a 1-hour episode (3,600 seconds = 600 chunks × 7 profiles = 4,200 jobs)
const dispatcher = new EncodingJobDispatcher(50);
const jobs = dispatcher.generateJobs('stranger-things-s2-ep1', 3600);
const summary = await dispatcher.dispatch(jobs);

console.log(`Encoding complete: ${summary.completed} succeeded, ${summary.failed} failed`);
Practical checklist

Building a Video Streaming Platform — What to Get Right

  • Separate control plane (metadata, auth, CDN routing) from data plane (actual video bytes) — they have different scaling requirements.
  • Implement ABR from day one — never serve a single bitrate to all users on all connections.
  • Pre-position popular content on edge nodes proactively; do not wait for cache misses on popular releases.
  • Use per-title (or per-scene) encoding rather than a fixed bitrate ladder to save 20%+ on storage and bandwidth.
  • Encode in AV1 for modern clients, H.265 for 4K, H.264 as fallback — never assume codec support.
  • Build chaos engineering into your release process before you need it, not after an incident.
  • Make region evacuation a tested and automated procedure with a known time bound (Netflix: ~6 minutes).
  • Use circuit breakers on all downstream service calls — a slow recommendation service should never degrade playback.
  • Collect per-chunk playback telemetry — quality, OCA chosen, bandwidth — for tuning and anomaly detection.
  • ISP partnerships (or CDN selection) should optimize for last-mile proximity, not just geographic proximity.
FAQ

Common questions about Netflix's architecture

Does Netflix use AWS for everything, or do they own datacenters?

Netflix closed its last proprietary datacenter in 2016 and runs its entire microservices backend on AWS. However, the Open Connect CDN appliances (the actual video delivery hardware) are owned by Netflix and co-located inside ISP network facilities. So the answer is: AWS for the backend, private hardware for video delivery.

What happens when an OCA goes offline?

The client receives multiple OCA URLs from the playback service. If the primary OCA fails mid-stream, the client's health scoring immediately penalizes that OCA and switches to the next best option. The AWS playback service also tracks OCA health in real time and stops returning unhealthy OCAs in new playback sessions.

How does Netflix handle 4K HDR content on slow connections?

The ABR algorithm will not attempt 4K quality on a connection that cannot sustain 16 Mbps. The client will select 1080p or lower. Netflix also has a streaming quality setting in account preferences that caps the maximum quality, which is useful for mobile data saving.

Why does Netflix invest in AV1 encoding when it takes 20x longer than H.264?

Because at 260M subscribers streaming many hours per week, a 20% reduction in bitrate translates to enormous bandwidth savings — enough to justify very large encoding compute investments. AV1 encoding runs once (at ingest time); the savings run forever on every stream served. The math is overwhelmingly in favor of AV1 for a platform at Netflix's scale.

How does Chaos Kong actually work — do real users see failures?

Chaos Kong is run in a controlled way. Netflix monitors user experience metrics during the test and is prepared to abort if real user impact is detected. The goal is that with proper multi-region architecture, traffic evacuates to other regions fast enough that most users experience only a brief quality dip rather than an error. Monthly testing ensures this remains true as the system evolves.

Key Takeaways

Netflix's ability to serve 260 million concurrent streams without buffering comes down to five compounding design decisions: private CDN hardware inside ISP networks (eliminating expensive last-mile transit), nightly proactive cache filling (eliminating cache misses on popular content), per-title adaptive bitrate encoding (minimizing bytes per stream), client-side ABR intelligence (adjusting quality invisibly before buffering occurs), and chaos-validated multi-region AWS architecture (surviving any single region failure). No single one of these is the secret — the system performs because every layer is purpose-engineered and they compound together.
